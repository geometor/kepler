Bee Honeycombs & the Two Rhombic Polyhedra¶

If you ask Geometers in what order the cells of Bees are constructed, they will answer: in hexagonal order. That is a simple answer from a simple glance at the openings or doorways and the walls by which the cells are shaped—for around each individual cell stand six others, each sharing one of the walls of the middle cell.

Yet once you have examined the bottoms of the cells, you will see each one descend with three planes into an obtuse angle. This bottom—or rather, you might call it a keel—is joined to the six walls of the cell by six other corners: three higher trihedral ones, wholly like the lowest angle of the keel, and three lower four-edged ones interspersed between them. Moreover, one must notice that there is a double layer of cells, with their openings facing in opposite directions and their backs touching and tightly packed together, the apex of each keel in the one layer being inserted between the three corners of three keels in the opposite layer, with such art that every cell shares not only its six side walls with six

f. 0029 · p. 7

cells standing around it in the same layer, but also the three planes of its bottom with three other cells of the opposite layer. Thus it happens that every single bee has nine neighbors, separated from each by a single shared wall. The three planes of the keels are all similar to one another, of that shape which Geometers call a Rhombus.

Prompted by these Rhombi, I began to inquire in Geometry whether any Solid—similar to the five regular solids and the fourteen Archimedean ones—could be constructed out of rhombi alone; and I discovered two, of which the first is akin to the Cube and Octahedron, and the remaining one to the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron (for the cube itself takes the place of a third, akin to two tetrahedra fitted together). The first is enclosed by twelve rhombi, the second by thirty.

Now the first shares this property with the cube: just as eight corners of eight cubes fitted around the same point fill the entire space leaving no void, so four obtuse (or trihedral) corners of the first Rhombic solid accomplish the same, and likewise six four-edged corners. Therefore solid space can be built up out of these rhombi alone, in such a way that four trihedral corners and six four-edged corners always meet at each single point. And to sum up: when solid space is filled with equal cubes in straight array, thirty-two other cubes touch one cube at single corners, and six more along four corners (a full face), so that thirty-eight touch it in all; whereas when solid space is filled with equal rhombic solids, six others touch one rhombic solid at single four-edged corners, and twelve more at four corners (a full face), so that eighteen touch it in any manner.

This, therefore, is that geometric figure—as close to regular as possible—which fills solid space, just as the hexagon, square, and triangle fill plane space: this, I say, is what Bees fashion in their hives, save for this one omission, that their cells lack roofs resembling their keels. For if they added roofs as well, and each bee were shut up inside twelve or eighteen others, no way out would remain open for her, enclosed on every side. Accordingly, having no need of roofs, nothing prevented them from extending the six walls to suit the measure of their tiny bodies beyond the measure of the Rhombi in the keels, making them externally dissimilar to those rhombi.

f. 0030 · p. 8

Furthermore, if anyone cuts open a rather large Pomegranate, he will see most of its seeds squeezed into this very same figure, save in so far as the row of little stalks through which their nourishment is supplied hinders them.

Now the question arises in these two examples: what is the author of the Rhombic shape in the bee’s cell and in the seeds of the pomegranate? Matter is not the cause: for nowhere do Bees find little rhombic wax leaves already prepared which they might gather and fit together to fashion their tiny dwellings. Nor is it likely that in Pomegranates alone the seeds grow into angles of their own accord, when in all other fruits they turn out round wherever they are unobstructed, as the supplied sap fills and swells their yielding skins so that they bulge out wherever room is given.

Therefore in the pomegranate seed the cause of the figure lies indeed in the soul of the plant, which provides for the growth of the fruit; yet this is not the adequate cause of the shape, for it does not bestow this on the fruit out of a formal property, but is assisted by material necessity. For although the seeds are round at first while small, so long as space inside the rind suffices for them, at last as the rind hardens while the seeds keep growing, they become crowded and pressed together, just like peas inside their oblong pods. Peas, however, have nowhere to yield, being set in a row inside oblong pods, and so are flattened on two sides only; whereas the round seeds in Pomegranates, having freer space at first, easily insinuate themselves each between three protruding from the opposite layer, aided by their roundness and diverting their juice from where they are pressed into the empty spaces.

Indeed, if anyone encloses a great multitude of round, equal pellets made of soft material inside a round vessel and begins to tighten it from every direction with bronze hoops, most of the pellets will be squeezed into the Rhombic shape—especially if first, by carefully shaking the vessel, you allow the pellets by free rolling to settle into a tighter arrangement. For with a straight-lined array of pellets that cannot be disturbed, you will even produce cubes under pressure.

In general, equal spheres gathered in some vessel are arranged among themselves in two ways, corresponding to the two ways of arranging them upon a flat plane.
