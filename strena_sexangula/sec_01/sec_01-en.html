Dedication & Snow Stars on the Prague Bridge¶

f. 0025 · p. 3

To the Illustrious Imperial Aulic Councilor of His Sacred Imperial Majesty, Lord Johann Matthäus Wackher von Wackenfels, Knight of the Golden Spur, etc., Patron of Men of Letters and Philosophers, my gracious Lord.

Since I am not unaware how dearly you love Nothing (Nihil)—not indeed for its cheapness of price, but on account of the witty and most charming jest of the playful Sparrow—it is easy for me to guess that a gift will be all the more welcome and pleasing to you the closer it approaches to Nothing.

Whatever it is that might delight you by some thought of Nothing ought to be rare, tiny, utterly inexpensive, and least enduring—that is, almost nothing. Although many such things exist in the nature of things, there is still a choice among them. Perhaps you will think of one of the Atoms of Epicurus: yet that is Nothing itself, and you already have Nothing from me before now. Let us therefore make a tour through the Elements—that is, through what is smallest in each thing.

First, do not dream of Earth—that is, of the treasures of my Archimedes, who resolves the Earth into grains of sand and packs ten thousand tiny dust-motes into a single poppy seed. For if I were to subtract one grain, I would thoroughly confound his reckonings of Numbers and Myriads. Add to this that the shape of such corpuscles is neither seen by the eyes nor disclosed by Archimedes.

f. 0026 · p. 4

There is therefore no ingenuity in them, and no desire for an unseen thing. Dust is also an enduring thing, ruling undisturbed atop ancient beams eaten away with rot. Too much, therefore, would I give if I gave these.

Again, sparks of fire, though small and fleeting, are never smaller than grains of flint chipped off by striking, or parings from glowing coals—which I have already dismissed among dust-motes. I therefore leave shaped Pyramids, which I have never seen, to Plato, so that out of them he may piece together fire at his pleasure. We must turn to the middle Elements.

Wind and smoke I could give, yet these are sold—and not merely in Icelandic leather bags, but on paper too, and even in words, and that everywhere throughout the world. Smoke is therefore a costly commodity, and one that costs me dear; nor is it fit for wit, being crude and shapeless.

We descend, therefore, to the Waters. The sacred prophets count a drop clinging to a bucket as a thing of the utmost contempt; and our Germans hold nothing smaller than that little drop of wine shaken out onto the thumbnail after a goblet is drained, where it stands clinging by its own bulk. If I were to offer this drop, I should certainly give less than that Persian who poured water of the Choaspes from the hollow of his hand for his King—and a more honorable gift too is a drop of wine from a German’s nail than a bit scraped from the fingernail of a teeth-gnashing Italian refusing even the tiniest trifle. Finally, the spherical shape of a drop already promises geometric speculation; yet I fear lest even this be too much for you, who delight so greatly in Nothing.

What if we pass on to living creatures? I fear carrying owls to Athens. For lately at your house I saw volumes of singular and rare things by him who, from the ancient school of Parmenides, abolishes motion because motion does not possess one of its parts (namely the past) in finished form. Since many marvels are found in that work, I scarcely suppose tiny creatures of prodigious minuteness will be wanting there. Yet there is no need to rely on guesswork: you own Scaliger’s criticisms upon Cardano’s Subtleties, where in Exercise 194, number 7, you will find the smallest animal—the little skin-burrowing mite. Yet even this is too much: for since that tiny creature walks, it does not lack a soul. Why then should I offer you a Soul, when I hesitate to give you even a lifeless drop—unless perhaps you hope some novelty might be discovered by dissecting the corpse of the marauding beast, a matter for Dr. Jessenius the anatomist to look into!

f. 0027 · p. 5

While anxiously turning over such thoughts, I cross the bridge, embarrassed at my discourtesy in appearing before you without a New Year’s gift—except that, forever harping on the same string, I bring Nothing again and again—and unable to find what could come closest to Nothing while still admitting an edge of wit. Just then it happily befell that, as vapors condensed into snow by the force of the cold, tiny flakes settled here and there upon my cloak—all six-cornered, with feathered rays. By Hercules! Here is a thing smaller than any drop, yet shaped with figure—here is the most longed-for New Year’s gift for a lover of Nothing, and one worthy for a Mathematician (who has Nothing and receives Nothing) to bestow, since it descends from heaven and bears the likeness of the stars!

Let me hurry back to my patron while the little gift lasts, lest it dissolve into nothing from the warm breath of my body.

And lo, its fateful name! O thing most welcome to Wackher, lover of Nothing! For if you ask a German what Nix (“snow”) is, he will answer Nihil (“nothing”), provided he knows Latin.

Accept, therefore, this increment of Nothing with a cheerful countenance, and if you are wise, hold your breath lest once again you receive nothing.

For Socrates must discourse upon the leap of a flea—that is, why Snowflakes at their first fall, before they are entangled into larger clumps, perpetually fall six-cornered, with six feathered rays like tiny plumes.

Nay, away from here with popular scorn and Aristophanes, pander of ignorance! What need have I of Socrates, the butt of his play? I look instead to the Royal Psalmist, who recalls among the praises of God that He gives snow like wool—by which word, if I mistake not, he expressed those feathery rays of my little snow-star. For it is likely that while he sat weary, or stood leaning on his staff keeping watch over his flock, he saw and noticed these snowy little stars drifting down upon the fleeces of his sheep and clinging there.

But let us come to the matter, jesting aside. Since it is a perpetual fact, whenever it begins to snow, that those first elements of Snow display the shape of a six-cornered Star, there must necessarily be a definite cause. For if it happens by chance, why do five-cornered or seven-cornered ones not fall equally often? Why always six-cornered, so long as they are not yet jumbled and packed together by a crowd and varied jostling, but fall scattered and distinct?

When I recently had a conversation on this matter with someone, we first

f. 0028 · p. 6

agreed between ourselves that the cause is not to be sought in the matter, but in the agent. For the matter of snow is vapor; and while vapor rises from the earth, borne upward by a certain warmth of its own, it is nothing other than continuous and as it were fluid—not therefore separated into individual little stars of this kind.

You may ask how I know this, since even if vapor were such, it could not be discerned by the eyes because vapor is transparent. I answer: vapor arises from the loosening of subterranean moisture, as is proved by its lightness and ascent. Yet in loosening, figures have no place; for only that has a figure of itself which is bounded by itself, since boundaries constitute a figure. Vapor, once loosened, belongs to the class of fluids and flows—that is, it is not bounded by itself—and therefore retains no figure until it condenses into snow or a drop.

Since it was thus established that the cause of the impressed six-cornered figure lies with the agent, it was next asked what that agent might be and how it might act: whether as an indwelling form or as an extrinsic efficient cause; whether it produces the six-cornered figure from the necessity of matter, or from its own nature—namely one endowed with an innate archetype of the beauty residing in the hexagon, or with knowledge of an end to which this shape conduces?

To make the distinction between these questions plain, let us employ noble examples, geometrically described; for this digression will contribute greatly to our inquiry.
