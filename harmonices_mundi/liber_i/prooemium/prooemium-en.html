Preface¶

Since the causes of Harmonic Proportions must be sought by us from the divisions of a circle into equal aliquot parts—divisions accomplished geometrically and scientifically, that is, from regular plane figures capable of demonstration—I thought it needful to state at the outset that the mental distinctions among geometrical entities are today, so far as published books reveal, completely unknown. Indeed, even among the ancients, scarcely anyone appears to have declared that he knew these specific differences of geometrical things with exactness, apart from Euclid and his commentator Proclus (In Euclidem). For the division of Problems into Plane, Solid, and Linear, handed down by Pappus of Alexandria and the ancients whom he follows, is well suited to explain the habits of mind that arise around each division of a geometrical subject; yet it is brief in words and directed toward practice, making no mention of theory. And in truth, unless we occupy our whole mind in the theory of this matter, we shall never attain to harmonic ratios. Proclus Diadochus, having published four books upon the first book of Euclid, expressly played the part of a theoretical philosopher upon a mathematical subject; and had he also left us his commentaries upon the tenth book of Euclid (Elements X), and had he not been neglected, he would both have freed our geometers from ignorance and relieved me entirely of this labor of setting forth the differences of geometrical entities. That those distinctions among mental beings were well known to him is readily apparent from his very opening, where he establishes as the principles of all mathematical essence the same principles that run through all beings and generate all things from themselves: namely, the Limit and the Unlimited—or the Boundary and the Boundless—recognizing the boundary or circumscription as the Form, and the boundless as the Matter of geometrical things.

For the proper attributes of quantities are Figuration and Proportion: figuration of single quantities, proportion of quantities joined together. Figuration is completed by boundaries: a straight line is bounded by points, a plane surface by lines, a solid body is bounded, circumscribed, and figured by surfaces. Those things, therefore, which are finite, circumscribed, and figured can also be grasped by the mind; whereas infinite and indeterminate things, insofar as they are such, cannot be brought under any science—which is acquired through definitions—nor confined within the barriers of demonstrations. Moreover, figures exist first in the Archetype before they exist in the Work, first in the divine mind before in creatures: in a different manner indeed as to their subject, yet with the same form of their essence. Hence, for quantities, figuration becomes a certain mental essence, or act of understanding—their essential difference. This is far clearer from proportions. For since figuration is completed by several boundaries, it follows that because of this plurality figuration makes use of proportions. Yet what proportion is without an operation of the mind cannot be understood in any way at all. On this account as well, whoever assigns boundaries to quantities as an essential principle posits that figured quantities possess an intellectual essence. But there is no need of further argument; let the whole book of Proclus be read, and it will be clear enough that the intellectual differences of geometrical things were thoroughly known to him, even if he does not set forth this assertion by itself in an isolated, open, and conspicuous place so as to alert even an inattentive reader; for his discourse flows as though in a full channel, strewn on every side with rich maxims of the more recondite Platonic philosophy, among which is also this singular argument of the present book.

Yet our own age has not hitherto had the leisure to penetrate into matters so deeply hidden. The book of Proclus was read by Petrus Ramus, yet insofar as it concerns the kernel of philosophy, it was despised and cast aside equally with the tenth book of Euclid; and he who had written a commentary on Euclid was rejected and bidden to hold his peace, as though he had written an apology for him. Indeed, the anger of an exasperated censor turned against Euclid as an accused criminal, and Euclid’s tenth book was condemned by a harsh sentence not to be read—a book which, if read and understood, could have unlocked the mysteries of philosophy. Hear, I pray, the words of Ramus, than which he never uttered anything more unworthy of Ramus, in Book 21 of his Scholae Mathematicae: “The subject matter set forth in the tenth book is handed down in such a manner that I have nowhere encountered a like obscurity in human letters and arts. I mean obscurity not as to understanding what Euclid prescribes—for that could be clear even to the unlearned and unlettered who look only at what is at hand and immediately before them—but as to discerning thoroughly and discovering what end and use are proposed for the work, and what are the genera, species, and differences of the things treated; for nothing so confused or tangled have I ever read or heard. Indeed, Pythagorean superstition seems to have been led into this sort of cavern.” And so forth. But by Hercules, Ramus, had you not believed this book too easy to understand, you would never have slandered it with so great a charge of obscurity! Greater toil is needed, quiet is needed, earnest care is needed, and singular attention of the mind until you grasp the intention of the writer. Once a noble mind has striven thus far, then at length beholding itself moving in the light of truth, it is filled with incredible delight and exults, and as from a watchtower it surveys the whole World and all the differences of its parts with utmost exactness. As for you, who here act as patron of ignorance and of the crowd of men who chase after profit from every thing divine and human—for you, I say, let these be “monstrous sophistries”; for you let Euclid have intemperately abused his leisure; for you let these subtleties have no place in geometry; let it be your part to carp at what you do not understand. For me, who search out the causes of things, no paths toward them have lain open save in the tenth book of Euclid.

Following Ramus, Lazarus Schonerus confessed in his Geometry that he had been unable to see any use whatever in the World for the five Regular Solids, until he read through my little book which I entitled Mysterium Cosmographicum, in which I prove that the number and intervals of the Planets are taken from the five Regular Bodies. Behold what harm Ramus the master did to Schonerus his pupil! First, after reading Aristotle—who had refuted the Pythagorean philosophy regarding the properties of the Elements deduced from the five bodies—Ramus straightway conceived in his mind a contempt for the entire Pythagorean philosophy. Next, knowing that Proclus belonged to the Pythagorean school, he refused to believe him when he affirmed what was most true: namely, that the ultimate end of Euclid’s work, to which absolutely all the propositions of all the books are referred (except those that lead to a Perfect Number), is the five regular solids. From this arose in Ramus that overconfident persuasion that the five solids ought to be struck out from the end of Euclid’s Elements.

Once the end of the work was taken away, as if the form of a building had been removed, there remained in Euclid a shapeless heap of propositions, against which—as against some phantom—Ramus rails throughout all twenty-eight books of his Scholae, with great

bitterness of speech and great rashness, most unworthy of so eminent a man. Following this persuasion of Ramus, Schonerus himself came to believe that the regular bodies are of no use; nor this alone, but he also neglected or despised Proclus, following the judgment of Ramus—from which Proclus he might have learned the use of the five bodies both in the Elements of Euclid and in the fabric of the World. Yet indeed the pupil was more fortunate than the master, because he welcomed with congratulation the use of the bodies in the Fabric of the World once laid open by me, which he had rejected when urged by Proclus. For what did it matter if the Pythagoreans assigned these figures to the elements rather than, as I have done, to the Spheres of the World? Ramus ought to have striven to remove this error of theirs concerning the true subject of the figures, as I have done, rather than abolish this whole philosophy with one tyrannical word.

What if the Pythagoreans taught the very same doctrine as I, and veiled their meaning in wrappings of words? Does not the Copernican form of the World stand forth in Aristotle himself, wrongly refuted by him under other names, while they called the Sun Fire and the Moon Counter-Earth (Antichthon)? For if the arrangement of the orbs among the Pythagoreans was the same as with Copernicus, if the five Bodies and the necessity of their fivefold number were known, if they all steadfastly taught that the five bodies are the Archetypes of the World’s parts, how little is lacking for us to believe that their teaching was read by Aristotle as a riddle and refuted as though under the literal sense of the words—when Aristotle reads “Earth,” to which they gave the Cube, whereas they perhaps meant Saturn, whose Orb is separated from Jupiter by the interposition of the Cube! Indeed, the common sort ascribe rest to the earth, whereas Saturn has been allotted the slowest motion, nearest to rest, whence even among the Hebrews it obtained its name from Rest. So Aristotle reads that the Octahedron was given to Air, when perhaps they meant Mercury, whose orb is enclosed by the Octahedron; nor is Mercury (being the swiftest of all) less swift than Air is accounted mobile. Under the name of Fire, Mars was perhaps hinted at—which elsewhere also takes its name Pyrois from fire—and to it the Tetrahedron was given, perhaps because its orb is enclosed by this figure. And under the veil of Water, to which the Icosahedron was assigned, the star of Venus (as whose course is contained by the Icosahedron) could lie hidden, because moistures are subject to Venus, and she herself is said to have sprung from the foam of the Sea, whence the word Aphrodite (Ἀφροδίτη). Finally, the word “World” (Mundus) could signify the Earth, and the Dodecahedron be ascribed to the World, because the Earth’s course is contained by this figure, marked out into twelve parts of longitude, just as that figure is bounded over its whole circuit by twelve plane faces. That in the Mysteries of the Pythagoreans the five figures were thus distributed not among the Elements, as Aristotle supposed, but among the Planets themselves, is confirmed most strongly by the fact that Proclus hands down this among the other ends of Geometry: that it teaches how the heavens received the figures fitting to certain of their parts.

Nor is that yet the end of the harm Ramus has done us: behold the most acute of present-day geometers, Willebrord Snellius, plainly voting with Ramus in his preface to the Problems of Ludolph van Ceulen: “First,” he says, “that division of the ineffables into thirteen species is useless for practice.” I grant it, if he recognizes no use except in ordinary daily life, and if contemplations of natural philosophy have no use for life.

But why does he not follow Proclus, whom he cites, who recognizes a higher good in Geometry than the arts necessary for bodily life? Then indeed the use of the tenth book too would have appeared in weighing the species of figures. Snellius cites geometric authors who do not use the tenth book of Euclid. Assuredly all of them treated either linear problems or solid ones, and dealt with figures or quantities that do not have their end within themselves, but manifestly tend toward other uses, nor would they be sought out apart from those uses. Regular figures, however, are sought for their own sake as Archetypes; they possess their perfection within themselves; and they belong among the subjects of plane Problems, notwithstanding that a solid is also enclosed by plane faces; similarly, the matter of the tenth book pertains chiefly to planes. Why then should things of a different kind be brought forward? Or why should merchandise be reckoned cheap because Codrus does not buy it to fill his belly, when Cleopatra buys it to adorn her ears? “It is merely a cross set up to torment wits.” True enough, for those who torment the Ineffables with numbers—that is, by trying to express them in rational speech. I, however, treat these species not with numbers, nor by Algebra, but by reasoning of the Mind—certainly because I have no need of them to cast up accounts in the marketplace, but to explain the causes of things. “He judges that these subtleties ought to be separated from the Elements (στοιχείωσις) and hidden away in libraries.” In every way he plays the faithful disciple of Ramus, nor does he spend futile labor: Ramus stripped the Euclidean edifice of its form and cast down its roof, the five solids; and once these were removed, the whole framework fell apart—the walls stand cracked, the arches threaten ruin. Snellius therefore carries off the mortar too, as a thing of no use save for the solidity of a house bonded together beneath the five figures. Oh, happy grasp of the pupil—how deftly he learned from Ramus to understand Euclid! No doubt they think the Elements (Στοιχεῖα) were so named because in Euclid there is found an assorted store of propositions, problems, and theorems for every kind of quantity and the arts busy about them; whereas the book was called Stoicheiosis (Στοιχείωσις) from its form, because every subsequent proposition leans upon the preceding one right down to the last proposition of the last book (and partly of the ninth book), which can dispense with none of those before it. Out of an Architect they make a forester or timber-merchant, imagining that Euclid wrote his book solely to supply every other builder while having no house of his own. But more than enough here of these matters; we must return to the main thread of our discourse.

For when I saw that the true and genuine differences of geometrical things—from which I must draw the causes of Harmonic Proportions—are wholly unknown to the generality of men; that Euclid, who had handed them down with care, was overwhelmed by the quibbles of Ramus and hissed off the stage, and amid the clamor of mocking voices was heard by none, or seemed to tell the mysteries of Philosophy to deaf ears; and that Proclus, who could have opened Euclid’s mind, dug out what lay hidden, and made harder points easy to grasp, was also held in derision and had not continued his Commentaries as far as the tenth book: I saw that I must by all means take this course—at the beginning, to transcribe from the tenth book of Euclid those things that bear especially upon my present design; also to bring to light the sequence of

f. 0024 · p. 6

matters in that book by inserting certain divisions, and to indicate the reasons why certain members of the divisions were omitted by Euclid; and only then to treat of the figures themselves. Where things had been demonstrated most clearly by Euclid, I contented myself with simply citing the propositions; many things demonstrated by Euclid in another way had here to be repeated, or separated points joined together, or their order changed, on account of the end proposed to myself—namely, the comparison of knowable and unknowable figures. I have embraced the series of Definitions, Propositions, and Theorems under one continuous numbering, as I did in the Dioptrice, for convenience of reference; even in the lemmas themselves I have not striven for finicky precision, nor been overly anxious about terminology, being more intent upon the things themselves—as one who plays the geometer no longer in Philosophy, but plays the Philosopher in this part of Geometry. And would that I could have discoursed of geometrical matters in an even more accessible style, provided only it were clearer and more tangible. Yet I trust that fair-minded readers in both respects—both because I handle geometry in a popular manner and because by no industry could I overcome the obscurity of the subject—will take my labor in good part. To whom also at the end I give this advice: that if they are wholly unversed in mathematical matters, they may pass over my expositions, read only the propositions from XXX to the end, and, putting trust in the propositions themselves without their demonstrations, proceed to the remaining books, especially the last—lest, frightened off by the difficulty of geometrical proofs, they deprive themselves of the most delightful fruit of Harmonic contemplation. Now let us approach the subject with God’s help.
