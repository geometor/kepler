Chapter I: Origin of Consonances from Constructible Polygons¶

Chapter I.

On the causes of consonances.

Definition.

Whereas the ancients used these terms—Monophonon, Antiphonon, Homophonon, Diaphonon, Symphonon, Asymphonon—for us, Dissonant shall signify the same as Asymphonon, and Consonant the same as Symphonon, of which the subspecies are Identical, which we shall employ for Homophonon, and Non-Identical, which we shall employ for Diaphonon. And of the Identical there will be two species: the unison, and the Identical by opposition.

Definition.

In geometry, the terms Part [Pars] and Parts [Partes] differ: for that is called a Part of which the Whole is a Multiple according to a certain proportion, say double, triple, or quadruple; whereas Parts is said when not one single Whole alone, but some multitude of Wholes is a Multiple of them. Thus, one seventh is called a Part, because the whole circle is sevenfold of this part; yet three sevenths are called not a Part but Parts, because the sum of three circles is sevenfold of this arc.

Here, however, we shall not use this distinction; rather, we shall call either of the aforesaid portions a Part—that is, every portion expressible in length will be called by us a Part, with this restriction, however, that it be no greater than a semicircle.

That will be called a Remainder [Residuum] which stays behind when an expressible portion is taken away from the Whole, being no less than a semicircle.

The distinction of the Remainder from the Part is exceedingly necessary, because a Part can be consonant while its Remainder is dissonant, as we shall see.

Definition.

A string [Chorda] is here taken not for the chord subtending an arc of a circle, as in geometry, but for any length whatever that is suited to emit a sound; and because sound is elicited through motion, in the abstract a string must be understood of the length of any motion whatsoever, or of any other length, even one conceived by the mind.

f. 0101 · p. 11

Axiom I.

The diameter of a circle, and the sides of the radical figures explained in Book I that have their own proper demonstration, determine a part of the circle consonant with the whole circle.

How a circle may be stretched so as to emit a sound, and how it must be fastened to a hollow body so that resonance arises—either at one mark so that the whole sounds, or at two so that parts do—this would be long to explain here; yet we had to begin in this way, because we are treating not only of song, which is harmony embodied in sounds, but must also understand the interval in the abstract apart from sounds. As for song, it suffices that a string stretched in a straight line can be divided in the same way as it is divided when bent into a circle by the side of an inscribed figure.

Corollary.

The consonances are infinite, because the demonstrable figures are infinite. However, it is not yet time to speak of the selection of concords, which does not extend very far. Here the Pythagoreans sought in their numbers, as causes, the bounds of the magnitude of consonant intervals—bounds which human hearing alone fixes for them, since it is not of infinite capacity. Therefore that narrowing of the number of concords is merely accidental to harmonic intervals, and not causal. Even the musicians of our own day go beyond the Pythagorean bounds—to say nothing for now of the celestial harmonies.

Axiom II.

By whatever degree the demonstration of a side stands distant from the first, by the same degree also the consonance with the whole circle of the part cut off by the side recedes from the most perfect consonance of the unison: or, whatever is the lot of the figure to which the side belongs among the other figures, the same is the lot of that consonance among the other consonances.

This axiom will be employed below to obtain the selection of concords, for the sake of sweetness.

Axiom III.

The indemonstrable sides of regular and star figures determine a part of the circle that is dissonant from the whole circle; so too the side of a figure that is indeed demonstrable, yet not by itself nor by its own proper demonstration—fetch from Book II the defect of congruence: in both ways the fifteen-sided figure is excluded.

f. 0102 · p. 12

This axiom will complete the integrity of the cause of concords, which I substitute after rejecting the abstract numbers of the Pythagoreans.

Corollary.

Therefore these parts are dissonant from the Whole:

  • 1, 2, 3 — [with respect to] 7

  • 1, 2, 4 — [with respect to] 9

  • 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 — [with respect to] 11

  • 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — [with respect to] 13

  • 1, 3, 5 — [with respect to] 14

  • 1, 2, 4, 7 — [with respect to] 15

  • 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 — [with respect to] 17

  • 1, 5, 7 — [with respect to] 18

  • 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 — [with respect to] 19. And so on to infinity.

Axiom IV.

Figures that have kindred demonstrations of their sides also give birth to kindred harmonies.

By this axiom the origin and cause of Harmonic Proportions will be proved in abundance.

Axiom V.

Strings or arcs of a circle of equal tension having between themselves, by reason of length, the same proportion as exists between a Part or Remainder of a circle and the Whole circle, also have the same consonance or dissonance, even though it be contained between other terms or sounds.
