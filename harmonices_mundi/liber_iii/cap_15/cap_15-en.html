Chapter XV: Which Modes Serve Which Emotional Affects¶

Chapter XV.

Which Modes or Tones serve which Affects.

Thus far on the principles from which the Tones consist and are distinguished against one another: now I shall say a little of their effect, in keeping indeed with my principles.

First, the reader must be reminded that here we are not treating the difference of mental dispositions that cause a man to sing rather than use plain and tranquil speech; nor with those effects that follow singing on the one hand and simple speech on the other in the listener. For just as cheerfulness of mind is what precedes all singing whatsoever in the singer, and as it were dictates the Melody to him, so too pleasure in the listeners follows every kind of concinnous song.

We must set aside this general disposition of the Soul, or rather,

f. 0165 · p. 75

place it as matter beneath that diversity of affects which we are seeking here.

Now since all parts of concinnous and natural Song are fitted to stir the affects, by imitation of the sounds that animals utter to bear witness to their desires, it is clear from this that this inquiry is varied and manifold, and thus akin to the infinite; and since it surpasses my strength, the whole matter would more rightly be handed over to the Empiricists, that is, practical Musicians: for they, without rules, by nature’s guidance alone, repeatedly become the authors of admirable Melodies— which is far easier for them than to set forth in a long and eloquent speech what song is, in what it consists, or how it is made.

For since, as prose Speech is represented by Verse, so voices and gestures of Animals are represented and as it were depicted by Song: surely, like Poetics, the Musical faculty of composing song must be learned by practice and exercise alone, provided one’s talent is also made by Nature for this.

Yet because it is part of the most honorable recreations of the Soul to search out the causes of things, or to gaze closely upon those found by others—so much so that even Aristotle devoted no small part of his Problems to Harmonic contemplations—we must try something here too, advance the bounds of Philosophy, and, applying the laws of Method, curb that infinity of dispute and bring it back under general headings, so that through particulars the same judgment may afterward be made of similar cases.

  1. The elements of song itself, and of the affects that follow the species of song, are proportional, almost as many in number on each side: for the very names of the Parts of Song—agoge [ἀγωγὴ], tone [τονὴ], petteia [πεττεία], ploke [πλοκὴ]—allude to definite affects. The first serves simplicity, the last luxury; the former resembles the body, the latter colors; equality of Tone stirs attention; Petteia, or play, is fitted for delight and recreation. These are general across all Musical Modes.

  2. On Agoge, Galilei makes this particular observation: that there are two differences of it, one upward, the other downward, of which the former serves joy, the latter sadness and weeping. The cause is natural: below, a low voice is produced by slow motion, above, a high voice by swift motion; when therefore the voice descends, it approaches rest; when it ascends, it advances in motion—wherefore in choral song we generally end at the bottom; there, then, the voice languishes, here it flourishes. And indeed in sorrow the mind and all its functions languish, while in cheerfulness it lives and is active.

  3. Great too is the power of the leap, which is as it were potential Agoge: for it has rashness, motion, boldness; it is martial, virile, shameless if frequent, especially through the fifth [diapente], whose figure, the Triangle, consists of acute angles and exhausts the whole circle with three lines. On the other hand, a single ascending leap through the soft sixth, followed by a downward Agoge, expresses the greatness of grief, suited to wailing, from the likeness of the voice: as in Orlando’s In me transierunt.

  4. Much also depends on how great a height in the Octave system from the lowest root the chief part of the song occupies: for if it traverses or exceeds the whole Octave, the song is spirited; if only the fourth [Diatessaron], modest and pleasant; the fifth [Diapente] holds the mean; the soft Third,

lowliness and faintheartedness. Some attribute this to descent below the root by a fourth; and tones of this form they call plagioi or plagal of their Authentics.

  1. Among general elements must especially be numbered swiftness and slowness of rhythm, or beat; the former is suited to anger, movement, battles, mirth; the latter to the affections we enjoy in rest, sorrow, love, longing, and the pleasure of attainment.

  1. Add also measure, that is, Rhythm itself: for one is the force of what is called triple, another that of duple, drawing much perhaps from imitating dancing choruses: since their alternating variety of motions is known to all, it immediately alerts the hearers, bringing them as it were into the scene itself. Triple therefore is turbulent and active, duple quieter, calmer, more modest. But of Rhythms and their signs, nothing in this book; wherefore let us set them aside.

  2. We must now come to those things by which we said Tones are properly distinguished; chief among which is the diversity of the genera of Song. And here a most abundant crop of disputes springs up, as to which of the three genera of the ancients was assigned to which affect—which we shall pass over untouched, since neither is the division of song into three genera in number natural, nor do the ancients agree with themselves on this subject. My discourse will be of the two natural genera of song, namely the Hard and the Soft; the very words betray what affects they stir. For just as the female is made chiefly to suffer, the male to act, especially in the business of generation, so the Soft genus is fitted to feminine passions of the soul, the Hard to manly actions; the distinctions of which will shine forth more clearly from what follows.

[Marginal note on VII:] On the Enharmonic, Aristotle writes that minds are filled by it with divine frenzy; on the contrary, the magistrates of the Spartans, because this genus of song is held effeminate and painted, drove out of the city with a mark of disgrace the author of its admission.

  1. For not only the Genera of song, but also the Tones in general differed in the position of the semitone. This position of the semitone animates and affects both the Genus and the Mode or Tone: yet we must argue differently concerning this position with respect to the genera, and with respect to all the tones. For we said that the Genera arise first of all in the principal Octave, which begins from G, in which the position of the semitone in the second-to-last place forms the soft Genus, and in the third-to-last place the hard genus: there the soft third is in the lowest place, here the hard. What then is the connection of this position with the affections? Or what has the minor third in common with women, with passions, with softness; and what likewise the hard Third with manliness, hardness, actions, efficacy?

First, then, remember that the hard Third arose from the Pentagon; and that the pentagon uses division according to Extreme and Mean, which forms the divine proportion. Now in this proportion lies a fair image of Generation. For as a father begets a son, and the son another, each like himself, so in that division, when the greater part is added to the whole, the proportion continues: the composite takes the place of the Whole, and the former whole, that of the greater part. Although this ratio cannot be expressed in numbers, there is nevertheless given a certain series of numbers which continually draws nearer to the truth, in which series the very difference of the numbers from the true terms (which are not countable, but ineffable) by a marvelous alternation brings forth males and females, distinct by members indicative of sex. For instance, if the greater part be first 2, the lesser 1, the whole 3: here it is not plainly as 1 to 2 so 2 to 3; for the difference is unity, by which the rectangle of the extremes 1, 3 falls short of the square

f. 0167 · p. 77

of the mean 2. Then with 2 added to 3 is made a new whole 5, and with 3 added to 5 a whole 8, etc. The rectangle from 1, 3 creates a female, for it falls short of the square of 2 by unity; the rectangle from 2, 5 a male, for it exceeds the square of 3 by unity; the rectangle from 3, 8 a female, for it falls short of the square of 5 by unity. Again from 5, 13 a male arises with respect to the square of 8; from 8, 21 a female with respect to the square of 13; and this thus to infinity.

Since such is the nature of this division, which concurs in the demonstration of the pentagon, and since God the Creator conformed the laws of generation to it—to the genuine proportion of ineffable terms, perfect in itself alone, the seminal reasons of plants, each bidden to have its own seed within itself; and to pairs of Numeric proportions joined together (so that one’s lacking unity is offset by the other’s excess), the union of male and female: what wonder then if the pentagon’s offspring, the hard Third 4:5 and the soft 5:6, also move souls, God’s images, to affects akin to the work of generation? Here we must recall from Chapter III that although 1:6 is from the hexagon, yet the Remainder 5:6 is consonant not because of the hexagon, but because of its derivation from three tenths of the circle, by doubling and halving of terms; therefore this Remainder also, and its Offspring the minor Third, is from the Pentagonal class of figures. This then being established, that the partnership of the two Thirds represents the partnership of male and female, with no trouble now each sex is assigned its own Third. For the major third turns out manly, the Minor feminine, since the proportion of bodies themselves and of powers both bodily and mental is the same. And since the major comes from an odd-sided figure, the Pentagon, while the minor comes originally from an even-sided Decagon, this agrees with Pythagoras’s doctrines, calling odd numbers male and even female (confirmed by examining excesses and defects, since odd exceeds), so the former is reckoned masculine, the latter feminine.

To these reasons let the contemplation of concinnous intervals also be added, the least of which is the Semitone; for a succeeding Semitone always invites the voice to surmount it, because of its smallness, being like a ridge on a gentler slope; and whenever a Semitone occurs upward, it is held as a kind of goal of the song, toward which it strives, and from which, as if a ridge has been surmounted and the effort completed, it is wont frequently to return downward. Surely, if we sing RE, MI, the ear is not satisfied, but expects FA to be added as well. Since therefore the hard third, which holds the lowest place in the Eighth Tone, lacks the semitone, which finally arrives to complete the Fourth [Diatessaron]: it is rightly held active and full of strivings, whose force is gonimos [γόνιμος] and akme askhetos [ἀκμὴ ἄσχετος], seeking its end, namely the Fourth, whose semitone is to it as it were an ekkhysis [ἔκχυσις], sought with all effort. But the minor third, standing in the lowest place of the First Tone, since it embraces the semitone, after surmounting which a return is wont to occur—as if content with itself, and made by its nature for surmounting and passion—always lays itself flat upon the ground, like a hen ready for the treading cock. And behold the causes of the affects in the Genera, and in the Tones of the primary system rising from G.

Now let us contemplate the same position of the semitone with respect to all Tones indiscriminately: where I shall adapt my speech, as hitherto, to the common Tones, as I distinguished them from my principles. For concerning the remaining X species of Octave, which I added for the sake of comparison, whether they deserve to be called Tones will be for Musicians to judge.

Often it has been said that naturally, indeed, the greater among the concinnous intervals demand the lowest place for themselves, so that great things be joined to great. For deep sounds too, which are likewise below, are determined by a certain greatness, that is, by long strings, and high sounds by short ones; or, what is the same, a deep sound is produced by great and slow motion, a high sound by small and swift motion. The natural series therefore is when in a perfect Tetrachord the Major Tone holds the first place, the Minor the second, and the Semitone the third and highest. Now when all things stand according to nature, we are joyful: joyful therefore are the Tones that have the lower Tetrachord thus divided; and the Seventh and Eighth have it—which with wondrous inconsistency they think the ancients called now Phrygian, now Mixolydian, though I rather incline to believe the Ancients called them Lydian; for they testify of their Lydian that it fills minds with divine frenzy, that is, alacrity and martial spirits, such as our Seventh and Eighth.

When therefore the order is overturned so that the Semitone stands in the lowest place—which happens in the Third and Fourth Ecclesiastical Tones, the Phrygian of the ancients—with nature’s order reversed, it is fitting that something plaintive, broken, and lamentable be sounded. But when the Semitone is in the middle place, the affect is middle: of tranquility, kindliness, and pleasantness from conversations and narratives—for which the First and Second are suited, namely of the soft and feminine genus, which they think were once called Dorian. Thus indeed Vincenzo Galilei describes the Dorian: that it is by Nature stable, quiet, without violence, suited to gravity and severity—which is true of these two according to more and less. For the Tones named by an even number must always be softer—being plagal to their authentics named by an odd number, for the reason brought forward shortly before under No. IV. Thus Galilei also makes the Plagal of the First languid, tearful, fearful; for motion toward low pitches belongs to languor, toward high pitches to vigor. Yet I would prefer both to temper those Epithets and to distinguish the cause. For languor, weeping, and fear are not the property of the Dorians, but in general of excursions to low pitches and frequent descents below the root.

  1. So much on the position of the semitone; nor have we yet assigned all Tones their properties. It follows therefore that we speak also of the imperfect and adulterated consonances by which the skeletons of the octaves are articulated. And first indeed their essence as it were will have to be inspected, afterward their placement in the System.

When therefore the arrangement of the Octave system is natural and primeval, in which the third, fourth, fifth, and sixth are perfectly consonant with the lowest string, such a Tone stirs in the soul all those things that are according to nature—the soft indeed, passions; the hard, actions, or affects suited to them. This property belongs to the First and Eighth rising from G; on which account Galilei not unjustly affirmed that the Eighth coincides with the first—understand, however, with the property of each sex or genus preserved. Thus on a twofold ground the Seventh and Eighth surpass the rest, both for the position of the semitone and also for the perfection

On the contrary, the Fifth and Sixth, and the Third and fourth use below adulterated and augmented consonances—the former pair the Fourth [Diatessaron], the latter the Fifth [Diapente]: which procures for them a force of sadness and of affects departing from human moderation.

For in the Fifth and Sixth, the Fourth [Diatessaron] is indeed orthion [ὄρθιον], with the natural position of the semitone at the top, no less than in the Seventh and Eighth; but two major tones are in the lowest place, after them the semitone, from which is blown up a Fourth overflowing by a comma.

Wherefore those modes also promote greatness of affects, such as Devotion, Admiration, Amplification, Grief; again Hope, Confidence, as it were an elevation of the mind above its present lot.

In the Third and Fourth, however, besides the inverted form of the Fourth itself, this fermentation of consonances is also added, and increases sadness and languors of the soul.

Now if it is permitted to pursue the analogy beyond the customary tones, to my species III, of soft song, described from A—because that has the semitone below, yet a perfect Fourth—this then I would adapt to pleasant sadness: as when we indulge ourselves in softness of soul, in Loves and Longings, or when joy presses out tears.

So much on the Consonances by reason of themselves: now let us see what they effect by reason of place in the system.

When therefore tone [τονὴ] and petteia [πεττεία] are chiefly occupied around the fourth and sixth strings, so that it is clear the Fourth is placed in the lowest spot by them—since this is the interval of the Tetrachord, which embraces all three species of concinnous intervals, and is therefore foremost in this animation of the Tones—then this form invites the song to humility, for the same cause that I used above under No. IV. But if these parts of the song are occupied around the third and fifth, placing the Fourth higher, the song is roused and elevated, especially if it runs to the octave—such seems the ancients’ Nomos orthios [Νόμος ὄρθιος]. If finally around the third and sixth, bringing the Fourth into the middle—happening oftener in the First and Second than in the Seventh and Eighth—a middle affect too will be advanced. Frequently, however, these articulations of skeletons are mingled among themselves, so that in different parts of a song they are different, according to the variety of the text and the skill of the artist painting the text with song.

  1. Now if that is true which makes Tones of an odd number, I, III, V, VII, higher, and of an even, II, IV, VI, VIII, lower, even with respect to the Octave’s root (certainly height answers to rousing of the Soul, depth to lowliness): then we can distribute the four classes’ affects into eight classes, by as many tones: so the Seventh has anger, violence, fortitude; the Eighth mirth, alacrity, keen pleasure; the Third keen grief, longing; the Fourth weeping, love; the First festivity, weddings, revels; the Second modest cheerfulness, conversations, narratives; the Fifth Panegyrics, exclamations, confidence, hope; the Sixth devotion, great grief, etc.

f. 0170 · p. 80

And now it is time for our discourse to return to the beginning. For the philosophical reader must be reminded of this: that our Musicians express all affects indiscriminately in all Tones—and that they both do this, because any song indiscriminately produces pleasure in the listener and requires alacrity in the composer, and can do it, because the means for stirring the affects are many, partly recounted at the beginning, which can be applied indiscriminately throughout all Tones. Yet if they study to employ all aids working toward the same aim, they will not readily neglect the choices of Tones suited to the hypothesis of the affects. But let them see to what is left to their practice; let it suffice for me to have proceeded thus far Theoretically.
