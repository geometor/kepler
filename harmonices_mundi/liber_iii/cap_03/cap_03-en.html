Chapter III: Harmonic Means and the Trinity of Concordant Pitches¶

Chapter III.

On harmonic means, and the trinity of concordant sounds.

Empty is that definition of Harmonic proportion which makes it that wherein, for three numbers in natural order, the excesses of adjacent pairs match the ratio of the extremes: as in $3, 4, 6$, the greatest $6$ doubles the least $3$, and the difference $2$ between the larger pair $4, 6$ doubles the difference $1$ between the smaller pair $4, 3$.

Yet I shall set forth the method of finding numbers containing such a proportion—called Musical by the authors—since it is frequently transferred from Harmonic doctrine into Ethics and Politics. The method is this: given two coprime numbers containing the ratio of the extremes (of three musically tempered in the ancients’ view) and of their differences from the mean, multiply each by itself and both by each other; add the two smaller of the three products for the Least of the numbers sought, add the two greater for the Greatest, and double the middle for the ancients’ Musical Mean. For example, let three numbers be sought in the ancients’ Musical proportion such that the extremes hold the ratio between $3$ and $5$. Thrice $3$ is $9$; thrice $5$ is $15$; five times $5$ is $25$: thus $9, 15, 25$ are made. Add $9$ and $15$, making $24$; add $15$ and $25$, making $40$; double $15$, making $30$. These three sought are $24, 30, 40$, whose differences are $6, 10$. For as $3$ to $5$, so $24$ to $40$, and $6$ to $10$. In least coprimes: $12, 15, 20$.

This indeed is truly a harmonic proportion for me as well, because not only is the proposed ratio between $3$ and $5$ harmonic, by the Corollary to Prop. VIII, but the found Mean $15$ also forms consonant ratios with the extremes $12$ and $20$, by the same Corollary. Yet this does not always happen. For whenever, between two numbers thus proposed, the Arithmetic mean determines dissonant ratios with the extremes,

f. 0119 · p. 29

so often does this operation also yield three numbers in a proportion truly not Harmonic, even though the two initially proposed contain by themselves a harmonic ratio. So it happens in $1:6$, $1:8$, $3:4$, $4:5$, $5:6$, $2:5$, $3:8$, $5:8$. For example, between $2$ and $5$, that is $4$ and $10$, the Arithmetic mean is $7$, not harmonic, because $7$ accords with neither $4$ nor $10$, by Prop. V. Operate therefore by the Rule: the numbers $14, 20, 35$ come forth with excesses $6, 15$, where $20$ would be called a harmonic mean by the ancients, because as $14$ is to $35$ (that is, $2$ to $5$), so is $6$ to $15$. Yet the ears utterly reject $20:35$ (that is, $4:7$) and $14:20$ (that is, $7:10$).

In the harmonic sections of Chapter II, therefore, as many means offer themselves as there are sections, minus one; and in those sections Mean is taken in a stricter sense, namely as the greater Part, or the number expressing it, of a string harmonically divided into unequal parts. Thus $2$ is the harmonic Mean between $1$ and $3$; thus $3$ between $1$ and $4$, and between $2$ and $5$; thus $4$ between $1$ and $5$; thus $5$ between $1$ and $6$, and between $3$ and $8$.

Beyond these, however, there are also other Means, freed from this law of dividing the whole string into two parts, yet comprehended under our general definition, and dividing not a single string, as in the preceding chapter, but a ratio of strings into smaller consonant ratios.

First, all ratios greater than the double are resolved into their principles by removing the double ratio: as $1:24$ consists of four doubles (namely sixteenfold) and sesquialteran. Thus the Harmonic Means of this kind between $1$ and $24$ are $2, 4, 8, 16$, with the sixteenfold placed above, or $12, 6, 3, 2$, with one double placed above and three below; for it can be done variously.

Next, the double ratio is resolved into these consonances: $3:4$ and $2:3$, or $3:4$, $4:5$, and $5:6$, or $4:5$ and $5:8$, or $5:6$ and $3:5$. Finally, the sesquialteran $2:3$ is resolved into $4:5$ and $5:6$; likewise $5:8$ into $5:6$ and $3:4$; likewise $3:5$ into $3:4$ and $4:5$.

Therefore these three ratios, $3:4$, $4:5$, and $5:6$, are the least of the consonances—that is, immediate, or lacking a Harmonic mean—namely the consonant Elements of the rest.

From these it follows that one double can have two means, also consonant with one another, and this in six ways. For since the double has three least consonant Elements, their order can be varied sixfold: for $3:4$ is either in the first place from the shorter string, or in the middle, or in the last; and in each case, of the remaining Elements either the greater $4:5$ is toward the shorter string, or the lesser $5:6$.

Yet of these six pairs of harmonic means, one alone—in $10, 12, 15, 20$—admits the ancients’ definition. For $12$ is the Musical mean (in their sense) between $10$ and $15$, and $15$ between $12$ and $20$. Their excesses are $2, 3, 5$: as the extremes of one triad $10$ to $15$, so $2$ to $3$; as the other triad $12$ to $20$, so $3$ to $5$.

Since therefore the strings of the double ratio sound identically, while between them there cannot at one time be more than two means consonant both with one another and with the doubles themselves, hence arose that famous observation of Musicians, who marvel that all harmonies are completed in three voices. For however many voices are added beyond these, each returns to one of the three through the identical consonance of the double ratio.

For although a single consonance arises from all these string magnitudes $3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, 24$, yet whatever comes after strings $3, 4, 5$ all returns into one of these by identical sound: as $6$ into $3$, $8$ into $4$, $10$ into $5$; $12$ into $6$ and $3$; $16$ into $8$ and $4$; $20$ into $10$ and $5$; $24$ into $12, 6,$ and $3$.

In vain do others seek the cause of this from elsewhere: some from the threefold dimension of perfect quantity, or body, as what extends in length, breadth, and depth; some from the perfection of the number three; others from the adorable Trinity itself in the Godhead.

In vain, I say, all of them: for neither does solid quantity enter into this matter, since I have taught the origin of harmonic proportions from plane figures, and solid quantity is very far different in the cause of knowledge from plane, inasmuch as the former uses two mean proportionals, which

f. 0121 · p. 31

it is impossible to know indiscriminately; nor can any power belong to number insofar as it is considered as numbering; nor again is the origin of this Trinity immediately from the divine essence by exemplary causation, since it appeared above that the cause of the matter is compounded from the principles already explained, which in no way intend any number of voices in itself, but, fitting the voices themselves one by one to one another harmonically, and thus as though doing something else, accidentally bring about something like divine things on account of the same number—which likewise frequently happens in very many other things.

In short, this ternary number is not the efficient cause of Harmonies, but their effect, or the companion of produced harmony; it does not inform harmonies, but is the resplendence of form; it is not the matter of harmonic voices, but an offspring begotten from material necessity; it is not the end (οὗ ἕνεκα), but the boundary of the work; finally, it is nothing belonging to the Harmonic thing itself, but a secondary Being of reason and a concept of the mind of second intention. For we ought no more to ask why only three voices accord harmonically, while the fourth and all others return as it were into the same through the double ratio’s consonance, than why there are only six pairs in any Octave, six forms of triple consonances. For just as that Sixfold is not from the six days of creation, so neither is that Threefold on account of the Trinity of persons in the Godhead. Yet since the Threefold is common to divine and mundane things, wherever it occurs, the human mind coming upon it, ignorant of its causes, marvels at this concordance.
