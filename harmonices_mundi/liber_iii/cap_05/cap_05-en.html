Chapter V: Partition of Consonances into Melodic Step Intervals¶

Chapter V.

On the natural division of consonant intervals into concinnous ones, and the names thence arisen for them.

What the Concinnous [Concinna] intervals are—that is, those observable by human ears in the progression of a melody and imitable by a singer’s voice—was stated in the preceding chapter. Now we must examine with particular care into which concinnous intervals each of the Consonances is dissolved under the guidance of nature.

Taking up again, therefore, those same numbers through which all natural harmonic sections of a single string were placed together in one view, it will appear to us that the interval between the numbers $75$ and $100$, with $80$ and $90$ interposed, has passed into these three concinnous intervals: into the semitone $75:80$ or $15:16$, the major tone $80:90$, that is $8:9$, and the minor tone $90:100$, that is $9:10$.

The same thing also happened with the interval between the numbers $72$ and $96$, with the same $80$ and $90$ intervening. For $72:80$ is $9:10$, the minor tone; $80:90$, as above, is the major tone; and finally $90:96$ is $15:16$, the semitone. On both sides, moreover, between the extremes—both $75:100$ and $72:96$—the interval $3:4$ is found. Wherefore, since Nature has taught us to fit these numbers together on account of the division of the circle by demonstrable figures, Nature has therefore, by the very act of the divisions, divided two sesquitertian [$3:4$] intervals, of fixed position between the two terms of the double proportion, into three perfect concinnous intervals: the major tone, the minor tone, and the semitone. Now, for three contiguous intervals there must be four places, or voices, or strings. Hence, therefore, the sesquitertian interval began to be called the Fourth [Quarta]—that is, the fourth voice from the first, whether above or below. For the same reason the Greeks call this interval Διὰ τεσσάρων, which we also express in Latin letters, writing in the customary way Diatessaron.

It follows, therefore, that because the sesquialteran interval [$2:3$] adds over and above one complete tone (since the difference between $2:3$ and $3:4$ was $8:9$), from this very fact it is called the Fifth [Quinta], or, in imitation of the Greek pronunciation, Diapente—notwithstanding that the sesquialteran intervals have not actually been divided into so many concinnous intervals by our harmonic sections, and for that reason one number necessary for this full division is still lacking to us: a defect which God the Creator himself also expressed in the motions of the planets, as we shall hear in Book Five.

Furthermore, because in the same way both $5:8$ and $3:5$ add to the sesquialteran

$2:3$ one of the elements already mentioned—the former the semitone $15:16$, the latter the minor tone $9:10$—from this they are called Sixths [Sextæ], the former major, the latter minor.

Conversely, because both $4:5$ and $5:6$ take away from the sesquitertian [$3:4$] one of the elements already mentioned—the former the semitone $15:16$, the latter the minor tone $9:10$, as was shown in the preceding chapter—there remain to them only pairs of the concinnous elements: to the former, the major and minor tones; to the latter, the major tone and the semitone. From this some call these intervals by the Greek name Ditones [Ditoni], major and minor (or semiditone); and since two intervals require three terms or voices, they are therefore called Thirds [Tertiæ], major and minor. And of these intervals established by the natural divisions of the string, the rest have indeed been actually divided in this way, whereas the highest and the lowest have not yet been actually divided.

And since the harmonic ratios $3:5$ and $4:5$ arise from the Pentagon, whose side is inexpressible, while $5:8$ and $5:6$ also mingle something of the Pentagon’s nature, it comes about that both pairs are of a less perfect consonance; and the smaller this is, the softer and more pleasing it falls upon the ears. Now it is smaller in $5:6$ and $5:8$ because they divide the whole circle either by a more perfect figure (namely the Hexagon, whose side is expressible in length) or into parts of a continued double proportion (which is identical), namely into $6$ and $8$. Therefore $5:8$ and $5:6$ are held as the soft [minor] sixth and third; whereas $3:5$ and $4:5$ are held as the hard [major] or sharp sixth and third, and are so named.

Finally, because the interval of the double proportion, as stated above, consists of the sesquialteran and the sesquitertian—of which the former is called the Fifth and the latter the Fourth—and yet these two have in their middle one common term, which is the last of the one and the first of the other, in the same direction, in this manner:

It follows, therefore, that the last term of the latter in counting is eighth in number. And from this the interval obtained the name of Octave [Octava]. Looking to the identical consonance, the Greeks call it Διὰ πασῶν, as it is also written in Latin letters Diapason; as though, once all differently sounding pitches are exhausted, the voice of the singer returns to itself at the eighth sound, and thence a new beginning and a new series of concinnous intervals arises, similar in all respects to the former: see Proposition I.

In vain do they philosophize here about Number—why, that is, the eighth voice completes them all and returns into the same: for truly one must answer by assuming the principle, namely that it happens by nature that the interval of the double proportion, which by Chapter I is Identisonous, is divided into seven concinnous intervals determined by eight sounds, as has been proved in this chapter. They suppose this happens because the number $8$ is the first cube and the first Tessera [die]. But what has the division of a string to do with solids? And why does not the twenty-seventh voice (for $27$ is the second cube) also return to the same?

Hence indeed the name of System [Systema] also belongs properly and primarily to the interval of the double proportion, divided by its seven concinnous intervals, described by its eight voices or strings, and expressed upon instruments: of which see Chapter IX below.

In those intervals which exceed the octave or Diapason, among multiples of the octave the double octave is customarily called in Greek Δὶς διὰ πασῶν [Disdiapason], the triple Trisdiapason, and so on. In the rest, however, Diapason or its multiple is named and the excess is added, in this way: a Fifth above an octave, or an Octave above a Fifth, Diapason epidiapente, or Διὰ πέντε ἐπὶ διὰ πασῶν. Sometimes we also continue by number, calling them Ninth, Tenth, Twelfth, and so forth.

This division of consonant intervals into concinnous ones is therefore natural; and this magnitude and number of concinnous elements, not greater than three, does not rest upon the habituation of the ears alone, but the sense of hearing possesses this from natural instinct. Nor can either other intervals besides these, or another number of them, be taken as concinnous intervals into which any consonance is divided. For if you wished, for example, to add the Diesis, first it is also the offspring of dissonant concinnous intervals; next, even if you especially wished to add it precisely because it is the offspring of consonances, yet you could not add it alone: for it would bring along with it the Limma or minor semitone, which arises solely from dissonances. Yet it is fitting that the hearing distinguish the latter from the offspring of consonances, and—having admitted those in singing and among secondary intervals—reject this one as spurious.

Wherefore neither will the Diesis be among the principal intervals of the Octave.
