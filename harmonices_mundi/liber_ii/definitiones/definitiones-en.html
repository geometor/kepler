Prooemium and Definitions I–XIII: On the Congruence of Figures¶

Prooemium¶

Hitherto I have set forth the Mental or intelligible (νοητὴν) essence of individual regular figures taken singly; there follows the property of them joined together, and as it were an effect within geometry, namely congruence or unsociability. For demonstrability and congruence do not have the same scope: the former belongs to figures individually and runs on to infinity with the continual doubling of the sides of a single figure; the latter, hemmed in by fixed laws whereby several figures are summoned into a single society, checks itself by the increase of angles and quickly comes to an end. And although there is a choice of degrees in demonstrative science, and those figures which we have explained differ greatly in nobility from those which we have dismissed nameless, yet congruence does not walk quite step for step even with this nobility of demonstration; and so neither is the cause of the other, but both depend upon the same common cause (which is the aptitude of the angles of the figure), each nevertheless according to its own laws.

Now how necessary this part of our speculation also is for us may be seen from the very plan of the whole work. For since we have undertaken to explain the origin of Harmony and its most excellent effects throughout the whole world, how could we say nothing of the congruence of figures, which are the wellsprings of harmonic proportions—especially since congruere and congruentia in Latin mean the very same thing as ἁρμόττειν and ἁρμονία in Greek; since this effect of figures within geometry, and within that part of architectonics which deals with archetypes, is as it were an image and prelude of effects outside geometry and outside the conceptions of the mind, in natural and celestial things themselves; and since this property of congruence, which issues in some structure and embodiment, is of such a kind that it invites the contemplative Mind itself to make, create, and embody something externally as well—so that, lying hidden from eternity in the super-blessed divine Mind through the orders of Ideas as the highest good, communicative of itself, it could not be contained within its own abstraction

without breaking forth into the work of Creation, and making God the Creator of bodies enclosed under these same figures? Of this congruence of figures, therefore, I shall treat briefly, since the demonstrations are by no means difficult, and scarcely require any apparatus other than the drawing of the figures themselves.

On the Congruence of Regular Figures¶

Definition I. Congruence is either of a plane or in a solid. In a plane, congruence occurs when individual angles of several figures meet at a single point in such a way that no gap is left.

Definition II. This is called perfect when all the angles of every figure that meets do so in the same species of meeting, so that all junctions are mutually similar, and the order of junctions can be continued to infinity.

Definition III. Most perfect, when the figures meeting in the plane are also all of the same species.

Definition IV. Imperfect, when a larger figure is indeed fenced round on all sides by similar junctions, yet either continuation to infinity is not granted, or it is granted, but not without an admixture of diverse species of junction. Imperfect of a lower degree, when the larger figure is not fitted to meet with a similar species at all its angles.

Definition V. Solid congruence—and a solid figure—occurs when individual angles of several plane figures constitute a solid angle, and when regular or semiregular figures are fitted together, no gap remains between the edges of the figures meeting one another on the opposite side of the solid figure that cannot be closed by a figure of one of the species employed, or at least by regular figures.

Note that there is another congruence, not of plane figures to form a solid figure, but of solid figures themselves with one another to fill solid space around a single point. Of corporeal figures of this kind there are only

f. 0067 · p. 49

two: the Cube and the Rhombic Dodecahedron. For eight angles of the Cube meet at a single point and fill the entire space on all sides. The Rhombus, on the other hand, has two kinds of angles: eight obtuse three-edged angles and six acute four-edged angles. Accordingly, four of the obtuse angles are congruent to fill space, and six of the acute angles—such is the edifice built by bees with contiguous cells, where one cell is surrounded at its base by three cells with opposing bases, and on its sides by six; and three more could also surround it in front to complete the figure, were it not necessary for the doorways to remain open. With this congruence of solid figures, however, we are not concerned here.

Definition VI. A most perfect solid congruence and solid figure occurs when the planes in the congruence are also all of the same figure.

Definition VII. This is either wholly regular, when the planes are regular, and it has all its angles on the same spherical surface and mutually similar.

Definition VIII. Or it is semiregular, when the planes are semiregular (see Book I, Def. III), and it has solid angles distinct and dissimilar in the number of their edges, yet of no more than two kinds, nor ordered in more than two spherical surfaces described about one center, and having as many angles of each kind as one of the regular solids has.

It is no objection that this solid congruence is called most perfect; for the imperfection present in its planes ought not to be ascribed to its solidifying, but is accidental to it. Yet this semiregular congruence is called most perfect equivocally.

Definition IX. Perfect of a lower degree occurs when the planes are regular, and all the angles lie in the same spherical surface and are mutually similar, yet the planes are of diverse species—each kind indeed as numerous as the faces in one of the most perfect figures, namely not fewer than four, because a solid figure is bounded by at least four planes.

Definition X. Imperfect congruence or figure occurs when, other conditions remaining the same, the larger figure is found no more often than once or twice.

For the figure becomes more like a part than a whole in the former case, and more like a plane than a solid in the latter, since every solid is bounded by at least four surfaces—as in the copperplate engravings that follow at letters A and B, where the larger figure is a heptagon. These two classes run out to infinity with the number of sides of their larger figure, beginning on both sides with the triangle (which in class A belongs to the most perfect regular congruences), then passing through the square, and thus in class B we fall once more upon a most perfect regular congruence; all the rest are imperfect.

Definition XI. Semisolid is that which does not satisfy all the clauses of the fifth definition: as when, once the plane figures are fitted together, the congruence does not wholly return into itself, but leaves gaps, while in other respects observing the prescriptions of Definitions VI and VII.

Definition XII. Plane figures are called congruent which either enclose a solid figure or fill a plane without a gap, being themselves regular or semiregular figures.

XIII. Definition. Incongruent are called those regular plane figures inscribed in a circle (if indeed they are inscribable) which neither form a solid figure inscribable in a sphere, either by themselves or with others of their own or another class, except an imperfect one only; nor pave a plane, either singly among themselves, or with the stars of their own class, or with the figures and stars of another class all around.

Where note that the heptagon and such figures are excluded, notwithstanding that two parallel heptagons, assisted by either seven squares or fourteen regular triangles, completely enclose a solid; because only two heptagons concur, and the figure becomes disk-like and similar to a plane, by no means globe-like or spheroidal. See in the copperplate figure at folio 53 ff., letters A and B. So too the fifteen-gon is excluded, notwithstanding that at some of its angles it fills place in the plane together with cognate figures, because it does not do so all around at all its angles.
