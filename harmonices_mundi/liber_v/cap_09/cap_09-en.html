Chapter IX: Origin of the Eccentricities from the Procurement of Harmonies¶

CHAPTER IX. The Origin of the Eccentricities in the Individual Planets from the Procurement of Harmonies among their Motions.

Since therefore we see that the universal Harmonies of all six Planets cannot happen by chance, especially through the extremes of the motions, which we have seen all concur toward universal Harmonies, except two which concurred toward those nearest to universal; since much less could it happen by chance that all the places of the system of the Octave, established in the third book through Harmonic sections, should be marked out by the extremes of the Planetary motions; and least of all could the most subtle business of the distinction of celestial Harmonies into two genera, hard and soft, come about by chance, without the singular care of the Maker: it follows therefore that the Creator, fountain of all wisdom, perpetual approver of Order, eternal and superessential spring of Geometry and Harmonics—that this very Maker of the heavens Himself, I say, joined the Harmonic proportions arising from regular plane figures to the five regular solid figures, and out of both classes fashioned one most perfect Archetype of the heavens; in which, just as through the five solid figures there shone forth the Ideas of the orbs on which the six stars are borne, so also through the offspring of the plane figures, the Harmonies (deduced from them in the third book) should contain the measures of the Eccentricities in the individual orbs, for proportioning the motions of the bodies; and from these two things one tempering should be made, and the greater proportions of the Orbs should yield somewhat to the lesser proportions of the Eccentricities necessary for procuring the Harmonies; and conversely, out of the Harmonic proportions, those chiefly should be accommodated to the Planets which had a greater kinship with each solid figure, insofar as this could be done through Harmonies; and by this method at last both the proportions of the Orbs and the Eccentricities of each should spring forth together from the Archetype, while from the amplitude of the Orbs and the bulk of the bodies the periodic Times of each should result.

Prior Reasons.

Axiom I.

It is fitting that, wherever it could be done at all, all manner of Harmonies should have been established between the Extremes of the motions both of single Planets and of pairs, so that this variety might adorn the world.

Axiom II.

The five intervals of the six Orbs ought to correspond somewhat in magnitude to the proportion of the geometric spheres which are inscribed in and circumscribed about the five regular solid figures, and in the same order that is natural to the figures themselves.

On this see Ch. I, the Mysterium Cosmographicum, and Book IV of the Epitome of Astronomy.

Proposition III.

Between the Earth and Mars, and between the same Earth and Venus, the intervals ought to be the least in proportion to their orbs, and very nearly equal; middling and again nearly equal between Saturn and Jupiter, and between Venus and Mercury; greatest between Jupiter and Mars.

For by II, the Planets corresponding in position to the figures that make the least proportion of geometric spheres ought likewise to make the least; middling those which correspond to the figures of middling proportion, and greatest those which correspond to the figure of greatest proportion. But in whatever order the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron stand among the figures, in the same stand the pairs of planets, the one of Mars and the Earth, the other of the Earth and Venus; and in whatever order the Cube and Octahedron stand, in the same stand the pair of Saturn and Jupiter, and the pair of Venus and Mercury; and finally in whatever order the Tetrahedron stands, in the same stands the pair of Jupiter and Mars; see Ch. III. Therefore the proportion between the Planetary orbs first mentioned will be least; between those of Saturn and Jupiter, nearly equal to that between Venus and Mercury; and finally greatest between the orbs of Jupiter and Mars.

Axiom IV.

All the Planets ought to have their own Eccentricities, no less than a motion in latitude, and according to them, different distances from the Sun, the source of motion.

Just as the essence of motion consists not in BEING, but in BECOMING, so also the form or figure of the region which any Planet traverses by motion does NOT BECOME solid all at once from the beginning, but in the course of time acquires at length not only its longitude, but also its latitude and depth, the triad of dimensions being completed; and thus gradually by the weaving together and accumulation of very many revolutions it comes to represent the appearance of a hollow orb having the same center as the Sun, just as from very many interwoven and wound circles of silk thread the Silkworm’s little house is built.

Proposition V.

To each pair of neighboring Planets two different Harmonies had to be attributed.

For by IV, any planet has a longest and a shortest distance from the Sun; wherefore by Ch. III of this book it will also have a slowest and a fastest motion. There are therefore two primary comparisons of extreme motions, one of the Diverging in two planets, the other of the Converging. And it is necessary that they be different from each other, because the proportion of the diverging will be greater, that of the converging lesser. Moreover, through the different pairs of Planets, there had to be different Harmonies, so that this variety might contribute to the adornment of the World, by Ax. I; and also because the proportions of the intervals between pairs are different, by III. But to each proportion of orbs there correspond certain Harmonic proportions by quantitative kinship, as was demonstrated in Ch. V of this book.

Proposition VI.

The two least Harmonies, 4:5 and 5:6, have no place between two planets.

For 5 is to 4 as 1000 to 800, and 6 to 5 as 1000 to 833+. But the spheres circumscribed about the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron have a greater proportion to the inscribed spheres, namely that of 1000 to 795 etc., and these two proportions indicate the intervals between the nearest Planetary Orbs, or the least spaces: for in the other regular figures the spheres stand farther apart from one another. Now the proportion of the motions is still greater than the proportions of the intervals, unless the proportion of the Eccentricities to the Orbs be huge, by Number 13 of Chapter III. Therefore the least proportion of the motions is greater than 4:5 and 5:6 are. Consequently, these Harmonies, hindered indeed by the regular figures, obtain no place among the planets.

For in this house of the World I was seeking nothing but stones, of an elegant form, yet such as befits stones; unaware that the Architect had fashioned them into the most finely articulated likeness of a living body. Thus little by little, especially in these last three years, I came to the Harmonies, abandoning the Figures in the smallest differences: both because those stood on the side of Form, which the finishing hand should put on, while the figures stood on the side of Matter, which in the World is the number of bodies and the rough extent of spaces; and also because the former gave the Eccentricities, which the latter did not even promise: those namely bestowed nose, eyes, and the remaining limbs upon the statue, for which these had prescribed only the external quantity of a rough mass.

Wherefore, just as neither are the bodies of Living beings made, nor is a block of stone wont to be shaped, to the pure rule of some geometric figure, but something is taken away from the outer round figure, however elegant (while the due magnitude of the bulk nevertheless remains), so that the body may acquire the organs necessary for life, and the stone the likeness of a living being: so also the ratio which the solid figures were about to prescribe for the planetary Orbs, as inferior and regarding only body and matter, had to yield to Harmonies, so far as was necessary for them to stand alongside and adorn the motions of the globes.

The second member of the Epiphonema, concerning universal Harmonies, has a proof akin to the former. For the supreme finishing touch, as it were, of perfection is owed rather to that which more perfects the World; conversely, that must be curtailed (if one of the two must be curtailed) which holds the secondary role here. Now the universal Harmony of all perfects the World more than the single twin Harmonies of each neigh-

boring pair. For Harmony is a certain ratio of unity: therefore they are more united if all together conspire into one Harmony than if each pair separately conspires into twin Harmonies. Wherefore in a conflict between the two, one of the two single Harmonies of each pair of Planets had to yield, so that the universal Harmonies of all might stand. Rather, moreover, the Major ones, of diverging motions, had to yield than the Minor ones, of converging motions. For if the former diverge, they look toward Planets not of the proposed pair, but other neighbors; whereas if the latter converge, then the motions of either Planet incline toward the motion of the other, mutually: as in the pair of Jupiter and Mars, the aphelion of the former inclines toward Saturn, the perihelion of the latter toward the Earth; whereas the perihelion of the former inclines toward Mars, the aphelion of the latter toward Jupiter. Therefore the Harmony of the latter is more proper to Jupiter and Mars; the Harmony of the former, diverging ones, is somewhat more foreign to Jupiter and Mars. Now the principle of union, which binds each pair of neighbors together, was less injured if a Harmony more foreign and remote from them were fermented, than if their proper one—namely, that between the closer motions of neighboring Planets—were so. Nor yet was this fermentation very great: for a ratio was found whereby both the universal Harmonies of all the Planets should stand—and those of two distinct kinds, and with some latitude of tuning that at least equaled a Comma— and the twin single Harmonies of each pair of neighbors should also be maintained: those of Converging motions indeed perfect in four pairs; those of aphelia in one, and of perihelia in two, likewise perfect; while those of diverging motions in four pairs are within the discrepancy of a single Diesis, the smallest interval, by which the human voice in figured song almost perpetually errs; and in the sole pair of Jupiter and Mars, between a Diesis and a Semitone. It appears therefore that this mutual yielding is on every side very good.

Thus far then let us utter our epiphonema concerning the work of God the Creator. It remains now that at length, lifting my eyes and hands from the tablet of demonstrations and raising them toward heaven, I devoutly and humbly pray to the Father of lights: O Thou who by the light of Nature dost advance in us the longing for the light of Grace, so that through it Thou mayest translate us into the light of Glory; I give thanks to Thee, Lord Creator, because Thou hast delighted me in Thy handiwork, and in the works of Thy hands I have exulted. Behold now I have completed the work of my calling, having used as much strength of mind as Thou hast given me; I have made manifest the glory of Thy works to the men who shall read these demonstrations, so far as the narrowness of my Mind could grasp of its infinity; ready was my spirit to philosophize most faultlessly: if anything unworthy of Thy counsels has been put forth by me, a little worm born and nourished in the mire of sins, which Thou wouldst have men know, inspire that also, so that I may amend it: if by the wondrous beauty of Thy works I have been enticed into rashness, or if I have loved my own glory among men while advancing in the work destined for Thy glory, gentle and merciful, forgive it: and finally, that these demonstrations may yield to Thy glory and the salvation of Souls, and in no wise hinder them, graciously deign to bring it to pass.
