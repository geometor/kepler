Chapter IV: Consonances of Extremal Planetary Motions¶

CHAPTER IV. In what things pertaining to the motions of the Planets Harmonic proportions have been expressed by the Creator, and how?

Therefore, once the illusion of retrogradations and stations has been removed, and the proper motions of the planets in their genuine eccentric Orbits have been laid bare, there still remain these distinct things in the planets: 1. Distances from the Sun. 2. Periodic times. 3. Daily eccentric arcs. 4. Daily delays in those arcs. 5. Angles at the Sun, or daily arcs appearing as if to spectators from the Sun. And again, all these (periodic times excepted) are variable throughout the whole circuit, most indeed at the mean longitudes, but least at the extremities,

f. 0285 · p. 193

when the turns made from one of them revert toward the opposite; as when a Planet is lowest and nearest to the Sun, and therefore lingers as little as possible in one degree of the Eccentric, and in turn completes the greatest daily arc of the Eccentric in one day, and appears swiftest from the Sun: then its motion remains for a little while in this vigor, without sensible variation: until, having passed perihelion, the planet gradually begins to depart farther from the Sun in a straight line; then at the same time it also lingers longer in the degrees of the Eccentric; or if you consider one day’s motion, on the following day too it advances less, and appears much slower from the Sun: until it approaches the highest Apsis, making its distance from the Sun greatest; for then it both lingers longest in one degree of the Eccentric, or on the contrary completes its smallest arc in one day, and makes both a much smaller appearance and the smallest of its whole circuit.

Finally, all these pertain either to some single Planet through diverse times, or to diverse planets; just as, assuming an infinity of time, all the affections of the circuit of one planet can concur and be compared at the same moment of time with all the affections of the circuit of another planet: and then indeed entire Eccentrics, compared with one another, have the same proportion as their semidiameters, or mean distances; but arcs of two Eccentrics, equally denominated or by the same number, nevertheless have unequal true lengths in the proportion of the whole Eccentrics. For example, one degree in the Sphere of Saturn is nearly twice as great as one degree in the Sphere of Jupiter. And in turn, daily arcs of the Eccentrics expressed in astronomical numbers do not exhibit the proportion of the true journeys which the globes complete in one day through the ethereal air, because each unit denotes a larger portion of the journey in the wider circle of a higher Planet, and a smaller portion in the narrower circle of a lower. So that thus this sixth part of the consideration now joins in, concerning the daily Journeys of pairs of Planets.

First, therefore, let us take the second of the things laid down, namely the Periodic Times of the Planets, which contain the sums gathered from all the Delays of all the Degrees of the whole circuit, long, middling, and small. All the last numbers, as you see, shrink back from harmonic proportions, and seem like incommensurables. For let the number of Mars’s days, 687, take the measure 120, which is that of the division of the String; in this measure there will come to Saturn, for its sixteenth part, a little more than 117; to Jupiter for its eighth part, less than 95; to the Earth less than 64; to Venus for its double, more than 78; to the quadruple of Mercury, more than 61. And yet these numbers with 120 make no harmonic proportion, whereas their neighbors 60, 75, 80, 96 do. Thus in the measure in which Saturn has 120, Jupiter has nearly 97, the Earth above 65, Venus more than 80, Mercury less than 63. And in the measure in which Jupiter has 120, the Earth has less than 81, Venus less than 100, Mercury less than 78. Likewise in that in which Venus has 120, the Earth has less than 98, Mercury more than 94. Finally in that in which the Earth has 120, Mercury has less than 116. But if a free choice of proportions had prevailed here, altogether perfect Harmonies would have been taken, not exceeding and deficient ones. God the Creator is therefore not found to have willed to introduce harmonic proportions among these sums of delays gathered into Periodic Times.

And since it is a most probable conjecture (resting as it does on Geometrical demonstrations, and the doctrine concerning the causes of Planetary motions delivered in the Commentaries on Mars), that the bulks of the Planetary bodies are in the proportion of the Periodic Times—so that the globe of Saturn is roughly thirty times greater than the globe of the Earth, Jupiter twelve times, Mars less than twice, the Earth greater by half than the globe of Venus, and four times greater than the globe of Mercury—neither will these proportions of the bodies be harmonic.

Yet since God established nothing without Geometrical beauty that is not bound by a certain law of necessity from something prior, we easily gather that periodic times derive their lengths, and indeed the movable Bodies derive their bulks, from something prior in the Archetype; to express which, those seemingly disproportionate bulks and periods have been fitted to this measure. But I said that Periods are gathered from Delays, longest, middling, and slowest: therefore Geometrical harmonies must be found either in these Delays, or in something prior to them in the Mind of the Craftsman. Now the proportions of Delays are bound to the proportions of daily

f. 0287 · p. 195

arcs; because the arcs have the inverse proportion of the Delays. Again, we said that the proportions both of the Delays and of the Distances of any single Planet are the same. As regards individual Planets, therefore, one and the same will be the consideration of these three: Arcs, Delays in equal Arcs, and Remoteness of the arcs from the Sun, or Distances.

And since all these are now variable in the Planets, there can be no doubt that if they have obtained any Geometrical beauty by the sure design of the supreme Craftsman, they received it in their Extremes, namely the Aphelion and Perihelion distances; and not thus in the Means lying between. For once the proportions of the extreme distances are given, there is no further need of design for the intermediate proportions to be fitted to a fixed number; for they follow of their own accord, by the necessity of planetary motion from one Extreme, through all intermediates, to the other Extreme.

These, then, are the extreme distances, sought out through the most accurate observations of Tycho Brahe, by the method delivered in the Commentaries on Mars, with most persistent study of seventeen years.

Thus the Extreme distances of no single Planet allude to Harmonies, except those of Mars and Mercury.

Yet if you compare the extreme distances of diverse Planets among themselves, some light of Harmony already begins to shine forth. For the diverging Extremes of ♄ and ♃ make a little more than a Diapason; the Converging ones, a mean between the major and minor Sixths. Thus the Diverging Extremes of ♃ and ♂ embrace nearly a Disdiapason, the Converging ones nearly a Diapente with a diapason. But the Diverging ones of the Earth and ♂ embrace somewhat more than a major Sixth, the Converging ones an abundant Diatessaron. In the following pair, the Earth and ♀, again between the Converging ones is the same

abundant diatessaron, yet among the diverging we are left bereft of a Harmonic proportion: for it is less than a Hemidiapason (if one may speak thus), that is, less than a subduple. Finally, between the diverging extremes of Venus and Mercury lies a little less than that composed of a Diapason and a minor third; between the converging is an abundant Diapente, a little more.

Although, therefore, one interval departs a little farther from Harmonies, yet this success was an invitation to advance further. Now these were my reasonings. First, these distances, insofar as they are lengths without motion, are not aptly examined for Harmonics, because a subject more akin to Harmonies is Motion, by reason of swiftness and slowness. Secondly, in the same distances, insofar as they are diameters of the Orbs, it is credible that higher regard was had for the five regular Solids, by analogy: because the same ratio which Geometrical solid bodies bear to the celestial orbs, either enclosed on all sides with celestial matter, as antiquity holds, or to be enclosed successively by the gathering of very many revolutions: the same do Plane figures, inscribable in a circle (which figures beget Harmonies), bear to the celestial circles of motions and the remaining spaces in which motions occur. Therefore if we seek Harmonies, let us seek them not in these distances as semidiameters of the Orbs, but in them as measures of motions, that is, in the motions themselves rather. Surely no other distances can be taken for the semidiameters of the Orbs than the Mean ones; whereas here we are treating of the Extreme distances. We are not treating, therefore, of distances with respect to their orbs, but with respect to motions.

When for these reasons I had passed over to comparing extreme motions, at first the same proportions of motions remained in magnitude as had previously been those of distances, merely inverted; wherefore among the motions also some proportions were found, as before, inharmonious and foreign to Harmonies.

Yet this again I judged happened to me deservedly; inasmuch as I was comparing among themselves the arcs of Eccentrics, which are neither expressed and numbered by a measure of the same magnitude—being numbered in degrees and minutes that are of diverse magnitude in diverse planets—nor even make an appearance anywhere so great as each one’s number says, save only at the center of each Eccentric, which is supported by no body, and hence it is also incredible that there is any sense or natural instinct in that place of the world capable of this appearance; nay even impossible, since I was comparing the Eccentric arcs of diverse planets because of their appearance at their own centers, which diverse planets hold diverse: whereas diverse apparent magnitudes being compared ought to appear in one place of the World in such wise that what has the faculty of comparing them is present in that common place of appearance. Therefore I judged that either the appearance of these eccentric arcs must be removed from the mind, or it must be formed otherwise. Now if I should remove the appearance, and turn my mind to the true daily journeys of the Planets themselves, I saw that I must use that rule which I delivered in the preceding chapter, Number IX. Having therefore multiplied the daily arcs of the Eccentrics into the Mean distances of the Orbs, these journeys came forth.

As regards individual daily journeys, therefore, the proportions contained in them must be the same in magnitude as those which were previously in the distances, though inverted in kind: because Eccentric arcs, as said, have the inverse proportion of their distances from the Sun.

Yet if we consider the extreme journeys of pairs, whether diverging or converging, much less anything harmonic appears than before, when we considered the arcs themselves.

And indeed, if we weigh the matter more diligently, it will appear not very likely that the most wise Creator procured Harmonies chiefly among the Planetary journeys themselves. For if the proportions of the journeys are Harmonic, all the remaining things which the planets have will be forced and bound to the journeys, so that there is no room for procuring Harmonies elsewhere. Yet to what good are Harmonies among journeys; or who will perceive these harmonies? There are two things which lay open harmonies to us in natural things, either light or sound: the former received through the eyes, or hidden senses analogous to the eyes, the latter through the ears; occupying which images, the mind, whether by instinct (of which abundantly in Book IV) or by Astronomical or Harmonic reasoning, distinguishes the harmonious from the inharmonious.

Now there exist no sounds in the heaven, nor is the motion so turbulent that a whistling is drawn out from the friction of the celestial air. There remains light: if this must teach concerning the journeys of the Planets, it will teach either Eyes, or a sensorium analogous to them, situated in a certain place; and so that light itself may immediately teach by itself, a sense seems required to be present on the spot. There will therefore be a sense in the whole World, namely so that one and the same sense may be present at the motions of all the Planets at once. For that path—led from observations through the longest windings of Geometry and Arithmetic, through the proportions of the orbs and the rest to be learned beforehand, all the way to these journeys themselves—is too long for any natural instinct, for the sake of moving which it seems agreed that harmonies were introduced.

Gathering all things, therefore, into one view, I rightly concluded that, dismissing the true journeys of the Planets through the ethereal air, we ought to turn our eyes to the Apparent daily arcs, and indeed all appearing from one certain and signal place of the World, namely from the Solar body itself, the source of Motion of all the Planets; and we must look, not how high each Planet is from the Sun, nor through how great a space it traverses in one day—for this is a matter of reasoning and astronomy, not of instinct— but how great an angle the daily motion of each subtends at the body of the Sun itself, or how great an arc it seems to complete each day under one common circle described from the Sun, such as the Ecliptic; so that thus this appearance, borne to the body of the Sun by the benefit of light, can flow straight with the light itself into creatures partaking of this instinct: just as in the fourth book we said that the figure of the heaven flows into the fetus by the benefit of rays.

Therefore Tychonic Astronomy teaches (once the parallaxes of the annual orb, which clothe them with the show of stations and retrogradations, are removed from the proper planetary motion) that the daily motions of the Planets in their orbits (appearing as if to spectators from the Sun) are these.

That, therefore, among these extreme apparent motions of individual planets there should be Harmonies and Harmonious intervals, I was able to presume in mind even from the proportions of the daily eccentric arcs reported above: since I saw there everywhere halves of harmonic proportions reigning, while I knew that the proportion of apparent motions is the duplicate proportion of the proportion of eccentric motions. Yet it is granted to confirm what is affirmed by experience itself, even without reasoning, as you see in the nearest table. For very closely do the proportions of the apparent motions of individual planets approach harmonies; just as Saturn and Jupiter embrace very little more than Thirds, Major and Minor—the excess there being 53 to 54, here 54 to 55, or less, namely nearly sesquicommas; the Earth very little, namely 137 to 138, scarcely half a comma, more than a semitone; Mars somewhat less than a Diapente (namely 29 to 30, which approaches 34 to 35 or 35 to 36); Mercury, above a Diapason, obtains a Minor Third more closely than a Tone, namely having less by about 38 to 39, which is about two commas, namely nearly 34 to 35 or 35 to 36. Venus alone obtains something less than all harmonious intervals, and than the Diesis itself: for its proportion is between two and three commas, and surpasses two thirds of a Diesis, being about 34 to 35, nearly 35 to 36, a Diesis diminished by a comma.

Into this consideration the Moon also enters: for at Apogee in the Quadratures its hourly motion, slowest of all, is found to be 26 min. 26 sec., and at Perigee in the Syzygies, swiftest of all, 35 min. 12 sec., by which ratio a Diatessaron is most exactly formed. For the third part of 26. 26 is 8. 49, whose quadruple is 35. 15. And note that the Harmony of the Diatessaron is nowhere else found among apparent motions: note also the analogy of the Fourth in Harmonies with the Quadrature in Phases. These things, accordingly, are found in the motions of individuals.

But in the extreme motions of pairs of Planets compared with one another, the brightest sun of celestial Harmonies immediately shines forth at first glance, whether you compare the diverging extreme motions with one another, or the converging. For between the converging motions of Saturn and Jupiter, the proportion is most exactly duple, or a Diapason; between the diverging, a very little more than triple, or a Diapason with a Diapente. For of 5 min. 30 sec. the third part is 1 min. 50 sec., whereas Saturn has 1 min. 46 sec. for that. Therefore the Planetary proportion has one Diesis more, or a little less, namely 26 to 27 or 27 to 28: and with a single Second less added to the Aphelion of ♄, the excess will be 34 to 35, as great as is that of the extreme motions of ♀. Between the diverging and converging extremes of Jupiter and Mars reign the Trisdiapason and the Disdiapason epidiatriton, not perfectly. For the eighth part of 38 min. 1 sec. is 4 min. 45 sec., whereas Jupiter has 4 min. 30 sec., between which numbers there still lies 18 : 19, which is a mean between 15 to 16 and 24 to 25, Semitone and Diesis, namely very nearly the perfect Limma 128 to 125. Thus the fifth part of 26 min. 14 sec. is 5 min. 15 sec., whereas Jupiter has 5 min. 30 sec.; here therefore there is wanting to the quintuple proportion about 21 to 22, by as much as the other exceeded before, namely nearly a Diesis 24 to 25. Thus the fifth part of 164 min. is 32 min. 48 sec., which taken three times makes 98 min. 24 sec.; since Venus has 97 min. 37 sec., the Planetary therefore surpasses by about two thirds of a comma, that is 126 to 127.

These, then, are the Harmonies distributed among the Planets; nor is there any of the chief comparisons (namely of converging and diverging extreme motions) that does not come so close to some Harmony that if strings were stretched thus, the ears could scarcely discern the imperfection, except that single excess between Jupiter and Mars.

Now it follows that neither then shall we stray far from Harmonies if we compare motions of the same side. Add Jupiter’s 5 to 6 with 54 to 55, and there is gathered 5 to 12 with 54 to 55, which is between the Perihelion motions of Saturn and Jupiter. Perfect Harmonies are therefore found: between the converging motions of Saturn and Jupiter, a Diapason; between the converging motions of Jupiter and Mars, nearly a Diapason with a soft Third; between the converging motions of Mars and the Earth, a Diapente; between their perihelia, a soft Sixth; between the aphelia of the Earth and Venus, a hard Sixth, between their perihelia, a soft Sixth; between the converging extremes of Venus and Mercury, a hard Sixth; between the diverging motions, or even between the perihelia, a Disdiapason: so that without harm to Astronomy, most subtly built up from the Brahean observations, the tiny remaining discrepancy seems capable of being absorbed, especially in the motions of Venus and Mercury.

You will note, moreover, that where there is no perfect Major Harmony, as between Jupiter and Mars, there alone is found an almost perfect interposition of a solid figure, since the perihelion distance of Jupiter is nearly triple the aphelion distance of Mars: so that thus this pair strives after in distances the perfect Harmony which it does not have in motions. You will note further that the greater Planetary ratio of Saturn and Jupiter exceeds the Harmonic, namely the Triple, by a quantity nearly as great as is Venus’s own proper ratio; and nearly as much is wanting to the common greater ratio of Mars and the Earth, and as much also to the two common ratios of the extremes of the Earth and Venus, converging and diverging. You will note thirdly that in the higher planets the Harmonies are nearly established between converging motions, but in the lower between motions of the same side. Note fourthly also that between the aphelia of Saturn and the Earth there are very nearly five Diapasons; for the thirty-second part of 57 min. 3 sec. is 1 min. 47 sec., whereas Saturn’s aphelion motion has 1 min. 46 sec.

Furthermore, a great difference lies between the unfolded Harmonies of individual Planets and those of joined ones. For the former indeed cannot exist at the same moment of time, because the same Planet, dwelling in its Aphelion, cannot at the same time be also in its opposite Perihelion: whereas of two Planets, the one can be in its Aphelion and the other in its Perihelion at the same moment of time. And so the same proportion as exists between simple Song or Monody, which we call Choral Music and which alone was

known to the Ancients, and song of several voices, called Figured, the invention of recent centuries: the same is the proportion of the Harmonies which individual Planets mark out to the Harmonies of joined ones. Accordingly, in Chapters V and VI henceforth, the individual planets will be compared with the Choral Music of the ancients, and its properties will be shown in the motions of the planets: while in the following chapters, the joined planets will be demonstrated to match modern figured Music.
