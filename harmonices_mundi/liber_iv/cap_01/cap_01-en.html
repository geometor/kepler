Chapter I: Essence of Sensible and Intellectual Harmonies¶

As I am about to discourse upon the essence of Harmonies, I find myself in doubt whether it is clearer first to examine the opinions of the ancients and then compare them with my own, or to begin with an exposition of my own view. The former way of philosophizing is received by all and frequently commended by Aristotle; the latter seems better suited to the present matter. For few have touched upon disputations concerning the Essence of Harmonies in particular; and whatever they have said about the subsistence of Mathematical Genera and species that could be applied to Harmonies in particular must necessarily be full of obscurity in the philosophy of the Gentiles, and thereby leave the reader’s mind wavering in doubt between the love of most plausible doctrines and the suspicion of an invented fiction. In Christian assemblies, however, and if a man embraces with firm faith the most sacred Mystery of the Trinity and the origin of all things from the Mosaic history, the heads of the disputation can both be set forth more clearly and find the minds of readers more inclined to belief. That we may therefore make clear the method of what is to be said, we must begin from a division. For sensible Harmony, or what is analogous to it, is one thing; Harmony separated and pure from sensible things is another. The former are many, both by reason of their subjects differing in species and by reason of individuals; whereas pure Harmony, separated from sensible subjects, is one and the same in any species whatsoever. For example, there is one and the same species of Harmony which arises from the double proportion: if this resides in sounds, it is called the Diapason (octave); if in radiations, it is named the Opposite Aspect; and in a Musical system it is either higher or lower, either sharper or deeper, either of human voices or of sounds from instruments; nor is it less manifold in Meteorology, for it belongs either to Saturn and Jupiter or to another pair, and either from the equinoctial signs or from the Tropics.

Concerning each genus, therefore, it is asked how they subsist, and what each is of itself—whether in themselves or in other things. As regards sensible harmonies, then, four things concur to their Being: (1) two sensible things of the same genus, and possessed of quantity so that they can be compared with one another in respect of quantity; (2) a Soul comparing them; (3) a reception of the sensible things inward; (4) a fitting proportion, which is defined as Harmony. If any one of these is taken away, sensible Harmony is taken away. For it is easy to understand that the Nature of Harmony is not to be defined by sensible things alone, such as by a sound or by the ray of a star. For sound is one thing, and a definite order among diverse sounds is another: I mean order in this place not as caused in some physical place, nor by reason of time, but by reason of highness and lowness of pitch. There can therefore be diverse sounds, yet unless there is among them a definite order, which is defined by definite proportions—a mathematical matter—there will be no Harmony among the voices. Conversely, if sound is taken away, what audible Harmony can be imagined; and if the rays of the planets are taken away, what Harmony of configurations?

Furthermore, because musical Harmony is not a sound, but an Order of several sounds, it follows hence that it is in the predicament of Relation. For the order of which we speak here is a relation, and things ordered are related to one another. Therefore, that whereby sounds are harmony is an accident, inasmuch as it inheres in subjects, does not subsist apart, and can be absent without the destruction of the subjects. Next, just as quantity in general cannot be separated from its bodies, yet can indeed be increased and diminished along with the bodies themselves, and is nevertheless an accident; so too the Order itself of sounds, under which as a genus we place Harmony, cannot be taken away from sounds (when there are more than one) distinguished from one another by quantity of highness and lowness: for either they will cease to be more than one, or if more than one, there will be among them an order according to excess and defect—mutable indeed, if either of the related subjects be changed. Order, therefore, walks step for step with quantities, and in particular with number. Thirdly, just as Number is defined as a multitude compacted out of units—wherein Aristotle recognizes something analogous to matter, namely its units, and something formal, the concept of the mind, which recognizes one multitude of those singular units separated in some respect from the rest; and thus elsewhere the same philosopher says that if the numbering Soul be taken away, all number is taken away, yet not the single units: so that number is materially in the things themselves, nothing besides them, unless a numbering Mind comes in; for then at last number besides the things themselves, and abstracted from the things, is something diverse from them, namely a concept in the mind of the plurality of individual things—so also the very order of sounds and of other sensible things, of which we treat here, is nothing else than the several sounds themselves, unless a Mind comes in comparing the sounds differing in pitch with one another; and in general every relation, without a Mind, is nothing besides its related terms, because related terms are not what they are called unless some mind be supposed which refers the one to the other.

What therefore is true in general of order and of relation must far more especially be assumed concerning Harmony, which consists in proportion and in the numbering of equal parts of a quantity: namely, that in order for any sensible harmony to subsist and to BE, besides the two sensible terms there must also be a comparing Soul; for if this be taken away, the terms will indeed be two sensible things, but they will not be one Harmony, which is a being of reason.

We must now also consider the third requirement, namely the reception of sensible things into the soul, and its necessity and manner.

Surely Harmony is one thing; sensible terms outside the Soul are not one thing, nor can they be united anywhere except within in the soul. Yet they cannot be within unless they are received inward, which needs no proof in many words from experience. A Musician, indeed, an artificer of symphonies, can meditate within his own soul upon the Harmony of two or more voices which he does not receive inward; yet that harmony is not sensible by that act, whereas we are speaking of the essence of sensible harmony.

But of what sort is that reception? Do not sounds—to use a better-known example in place of all—remain outside beyond the ears, in the air, and before the sounds the motions of bodies, to the quantity of which the sounds correspond? Do not those motions remain in their own bodies? How, then, do they enter?

I answer: partly by acting, partly by suffering (being acted upon). By acting, insofar as struck bodies emit the species of their motion, namely sounds, and luminous bodies emit the species of their light and color, namely rays—and as we say when we declare that objects move the senses, for to move is to act. By suffering, however, not the things themselves in themselves, but through their species, which while they are sensed, remembered, and compared must always suffer something according to ordinary usage of speech. And altogether this very uniting of the two terms in the Soul, through which the Essence of sensible Harmony results—namely, their being referred and compared—is to the things taken together something of the same kind as being seen or heard is to individual things, and even less than that. For all these are certain passions, though called so equivocally, within a wide compass of signification.

Let us, however, establish the degrees of passions. First, to take an example, Water suffers when, being lukewarm or chill, it grows warm upon being brought near fire. A second signification says that moisture suffers when it itself acts in some way, namely by moistening dry things; for a part of it is drawn into the pores of what was dried out, and is mingled with it, which is to suffer: thus also water suffers when it is tasted, for the thirsty man swallows and digests a part of it. But in a third signification also, water suffers in tasting when it is merely touched by the tongue—not only because it is warmed by the heat of the tongue while itself cooling the tongue with its coldness, nor only because some of the water clings to the tongue, but simply because it is touched by the tongue, which is as it were to be very gently struck, beaten, or driven, whence arises a local motion of the parts in the whole; and to be moved is to suffer. Let a fourth signification be that in which water is said to suffer when, by emitting a vapor, it strikes the nostrils and is perceived by smell—for to be perceived is to suffer—and here indeed not the water itself suffers, but its bodily efflux, by which passion some part of it is also consumed. The fifth degree is that in which water is understood to suffer when its rushing sound is heard: for first the water itself is in motion, then an immaterial species of the water as it is moved is diffused from it into the whole surrounding space and is received into the passages of the ears; this reception is a passion not of the water itself, but of its species, or of its immaterial outflow; and in this reception something is lost through time and through the multiplicity of receptions. For a sound is duller which falls upon the ears or garments of a great multitude, or which is emitted among falling snows.

Sixthly, even when water is seen, it suffers something: for from its surface, or as it were from its colors insofar as it is colored, it sends forth rays to the eye; which rays are touched, reflected, refracted, gathered to a point, and received when brought together by the retiform tunic—all of which are passions not of the water itself, but of its immaterial species or rays. And these rays lose nothing in this reception, neither by place nor by time; for when that which touches them is removed, they travel onward wholly unimpaired, which sound does not do.

Seventhly, after these species—and indeed the body of the water itself—have first acted somewhat upon the corporeal organs of the senses (that is, have affected them in conformity with themselves and made them like themselves, so that the little membranes of the tongue and skin have perceived cold and flavor, the spirits of the nostrils have perceived odor, the auditory spirits resound with its noise, and the visual spirits shine with its light, and thus a sensory image and species of it has been formed, which the eyes surely carry about with them even when the water is taken away, often against the man’s will):
