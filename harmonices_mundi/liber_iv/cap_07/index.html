Chapter VII: Epilogue on Sublunary Nature and Earth’s Meteoric Soul¶

CHAPTER VII.

Epilogue, on sublunary Nature, and the lower faculties of the Soul, especially those upon which Astrology rests.

Many things concerning this contemplation have been said in the second chapter, many throughout this whole fourth book, and some also in the third book; and a year ago in the Prolegomena to the Ephemerides, and in Book I of the Epitome of Copernican Astronomy, fol. 125; again in the year 1610 in the Tertius Interveniens, Theses 40 to 43, and 59 to 72, and 113, etc., and in the Reply to the Objections of Röslin; and in the year 1606 in the book On the New Star, chapters VIII, IX, X, and XXIV, XXVIII, especially from fol. 171 to 175; and in the year 1604 in the Optical Part of Astronomy, fol. 26, 27, and fol. 224. For I purposely pass over the prognostics, in which I have from time to time prefaced or interspersed remarks concerning the surer foundations of Astrology, as things not widely dispersed.

But since to certain most celebrated Professors of Philosophy and Medicine I have seemed to establish a new philosophy, and that a most true one, the tender little plant (as all new things are) must be cherished and reared with every care, so that it may strike roots in the minds of those who philosophize; and neither be choked by the excessive moisture of vain sophistries, nor washed away by the torrents of vulgar opinions, or stiffen from the chills of public neglect: and if it falls to my lot to guard against this, I fear nothing for it from the winds of calumny, lest it be broken, nor from the Sun of solid criticism, lest it be scorched.

Since therefore in Chapter I I touched indeed upon the essence of the Soul, yet only for the sake of Harmonies, and since the second Chapter is not professedly about the Soul, but about Harmonies for the sake of the Soul: it pleases me now in this chapter to discourse on the Soul for its own sake, a little more generally; it pleases me to make from the Epilogue a summary of all things that pertain to the present matter; and to bring together here under one view what has been said scatteredly, incidentally, and covertly, and to explain the whole nature of the thing in an unbroken thread of discourse.

That there is a certain Soul of the whole universe, set over the motions of the stars, generation of the elements, preservation of living things and plants, and finally mutual sympathy of higher and lower things, Timaeus of Locri defends from Pythagorean tenets in Plato; [In the margin: Fol. 115. 117.] and Proclus established it, both elsewhere and especially in the words transcribed in Chapter I of this Book IV. They made this distinct from Mind: both because Mind is simple, whereas that Soul is manifold in its faculties; and because the Ideas of all sensible things are in Mind primarily through itself, pure and identical, whereas in Soul they are secondarily, on account of Mind, and received from it, inclining more toward matter: whence, employing a distinction of names, they called the Intellectual or Mental ones PARADIGMS, and the Animative ones IMAGES of those Paradigms. The sum comes to this, that any Christian may most easily understand God the creator for the Platonic Mind, and the Nature of things for the Soul.

By what winds of contemplation especially they were driven to arrive at these dogmas, I leave to others to examine: I shall speak of myself. And first indeed, concerning the Soul of the whole universe, though I do not oppose it, I shall nevertheless say nothing in this Book IV: for it seems (if there is any such) to reside in the center of the World, which for me is the Sun, and thence to be propagated into its whole amplitude [In the margin: See the Epilogue of Book V.] by the commerce of rays of light, which are in place of Spirits in an animal body.

Concerning Nature, however, which presides over the elements, and which by a customary epithet I call sublunary, twenty years ago I already began to maintain not dissimilar things; yet I was moved to this not by reading or admiring the Platonists, but solely by observing tempests, and contemplating the Aspects by which they are stirred.

[In the margin: See above fol. 123. 125. 130. 131. 132. 140. 143.] For I saw with great constancy that the state of the air was disturbed whenever the planets were either conjoined or configured in the aspects commonly celebrated by Astrologers; I saw that there was mostly calm in the air when no Aspects, or few, occurred, or when they were quickly completed and passed over. [In the margin: Drowsiness of Astrologers.] Yet I judged that this matter ought not to be considered so lightly as the common herd of Prognosticators is wont to do, who describe the influences of the stars as though they were certain Gods, ruling heaven and earth, and acting all at pleasure: utterly unconcerned by what means they accomplish each thing among us on earth, while themselves remaining in heaven, and sending nothing down to us open to the senses except rays of light. This is the chief fountainhead of the most foul Astrological superstitions. Yet I do not much marvel at Prognosticators, a race of men mostly vulgar, childish, and given to dreams: rather those celebrated professors of Philosophy seem deserving of rebuke, [In the margin: And of Philosophers.] who, while they accept this from their Aristotle, that the life of animals and plants is cherished by the virtue of the Sun, do not weigh this: that the virtue of the Sun is therefore perceived by those creatures. Altogether sluggish and drowsy is the philosophy that allows the Sun to act upon these lower things as a sculptor acts upon lifeless matter, when the Sun lacks chisels, gouges, axes, and all bodily instruments. How much more vigilant than these philosophers is the poet Virgil, and how much wiser! Who does not ascribe everything even to the rains—though they too are a part of matter—but compares the bosom of the Earth to the lap of a Spouse, and indeed a joyful one, that is, one perceiving what is done to her with pleasure, and aiding her husband with a fitting motion: all which are signs of LIFE, and presuppose a Soul in the passive body. [In the margin: The primacy of the influx of heaven lies with the sublunary Soul.] For it would not be easy for the Sun, destitute of fit soldiers, to invade this citadel of earth’s bowels, unless some soul concurred, sitting within, conspiring with the enemy, and opening the gates to him. You see most of those who grant even the least to Astrology suffering from neglect of this consideration, as from a lethargy: to such a degree that when I arose, demonstrating the manner in which weather changes follow from an Aspect, a celebrated man stood forth who, to refute me, contended with great asseveration [In the margin: Matter of rains from the Earth.] that the matter of rains (which above at fol. 134 we laughed at even in the crowd) was celestial: nor yet, even so, did he teach how it comes about that, since the rays of the planets always meet on Earth, rains fall then rather if the rays of two

f. 0249 · p. 159

Planets make an angle of 60 degrees than if 59 or 61.

But I, before all else, resolved that the fourteen books of Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, Count, against Astrology must be read by me, and the reasons which he opposed to each chapter examined: whereby it came about that I was not only confirmed in condemning very many superstitions, but also in certain matters a new light dawned upon me; while, weighing the force of his objections with strained mind, I looked more deeply into the thing itself. In fine, that book, by refuting some things, made me give credit to those things from which earlier the astrologers, by defending them, had drawn away my credit. So it was with Aspects. For when on the one hand I looked to most constant experience—not indeed gaping after snows, or winds, or thunders, and the other things which astrologers are wont to predict in particular, but* [In the margin: * Beware of ambiguity] generally observing that the state of the air was moved in some manner if there were aspects, for example [In the margin: To Meteorological Effects Aspects generally concur among the causes: yet daily Aspects do not cause a long part of the year and its continuous general quality, but act only on single days on which they fall. See below.] if Mars and Jupiter were conjoined, and was calm if not; while on the other hand I heard Pico della Mirandola asking why he should rather believe that Jupiter and Mars, when they seem to be together, do greater things than when they are apart—since by conjunction their lights are not increased, for as much as they possessed apart, so much they bring into their meeting, and if planets of diverse qualities came together, one would rather seem to be hindered by the other—here, I say, seeking an answer so as to defend Aspects, upon which I saw Meteors to follow, I began to consider more diligently first the former as cause, then the latter as effect. [In the margin: Consideration of the cause of Tempests.] For the form of an Aspect, which out of indiscriminate configuration or angulation makes an Aspect, was a qualitative quantity—nay, a Relation of such quantities, a being of reason. Therefore, to move the air, it must first move some Reason which holds in its power either the air or what disturbs it. At the same time, before my eyes stood the comparison of Aspects with Musical Concordances, handed down by Ptolemy, unfolded by Cardano, yet too rashly dismissed by Pico della Mirandola; by this analogy I was greatly aided in tracking down the causes. For most of the objections which Pico della Mirandola brought against aspects, I saw could also be brought against the tempering together of two voices. Certainly the triple or sesquialter proportion of two sounds achieves nothing in the way of sharpness; and yet the sounds are pleasing if they are in triple or sesquialter, whereas they jar if they are in septuple or sesquisexta. Since therefore the thing that distinguishes the triple from the septuple must be Rational, namely the Soul set over hearing, so too in the business of Radiations there must be a Rational creature that discerns between subtenses of 60 degrees and those of 59 or 61; whether it uses discourse for this, like a man understanding Geometry, or has it from a concreated instinct alone, like the forms of plants, which guard and always construct the certain number of petals entrusted to them from the origin of things. Here there is no force of mixture, as if weighed out to medical measures, nor any of bodily instruments; nor do sounds delight or rays animate because they are so tempered together—as when cold is mixed with hot until a quality results answering to a body about to wash—for in such mixtures the best temperature is wont to be single, and all the rest approach it according to more and less. But among configurations

f. 0250 · p. 160

and among the intervals of sounds, there are several boundary marks, and in these marks alone lies the ratio of Consonances on the one side, and of Aspects on the other; [In the margin: They are not natural.] if you depart even a little from those marks, the ratio immediately perishes utterly. For as soon as the Sun, for example, has passed the quartile of Saturn, all stimulation of Nature cools down, and ceases for thirty whole days (as regards the rays of Saturn and the Sun) until the Sun comes to the Trine of Saturn; then again tempests are stirred up on a single day, and cease again once it is passed. Not such are the alterations of bodies, which occupy the whole time from beginning to end, never intermitting, but growing with the increase of the cause and with time, and subsiding again when it is diminished. In one word, as a line stands to a Number, so commonly known bodily commotions stand to these goads of configurations. [In the margin: But Animative.] If anyone weighs these things diligently, he will be able with no difficulty to conclude with himself that, like Number, so too these momentary commotions, proceeding from an Aspect (a Being of Reason), belong not to body but to Animative faculties: and therefore there must be a Soul which, admonished and as it were roused by an Aspect, stirs up meteors and tempests.

[In the margin: A Soul in the earth.] What or of what sort this Soul is, I could gather chiefly from its seat in the world. For since the aspects at which tempests are moved [In the margin: See fol. 134. 143.] are angles of two rays—not those which are in one or the other of the Planets looking upon each other, [In the margin: Fol. 133.] nor those which are in the Sun, of a far different quantity, [In the margin: Fol. 131.] but those which are formed here on Earth—and since the planets themselves have no knowledge of the angles which their rays form here on earth, unless we make them Astronomers: it follows therefore that the Soul which stirs the air at the prescription of the Aspects is here on Earth. And since that force accompanying the Aspects is felt throughout the whole orb of the Earth, that soul will be spread equally wide. And since the matter of rains, winds, mists, thunders, and fiery chasms, which are drawn forth at the time of Aspects, [In the margin: The matter of tempests rises from the Earth.] is a moist vapor, or another spirit dry and fiery, boiling up and exhaling from the earth (for why should a Philosopher listen here to Aristotle alone, scorning Rodolphus Agricola—nay, all husbandmen, and even his own very senses, when he daily sees, as rains impend, mountain peaks vomit forth a great force of mists?): [In the margin: Fol. 133.] this soul will therefore be not only on the surface of the earth, but also within, in subterranean caverns, in the passages of mountains; [In the margin: The Earth an animal.] finally, the globe of the Earth will be such a body as is that of some animal; and what its own Soul is to an animal, this sublunary Nature which we seek, which moves tempests at the presence of aspects, will be to the Earth.

Here that very thing greatly confirmed me which could have deterred someone else; namely, [In the margin: fol. 155. Aspects often ineffectual.] that the commotions of Tempests do not always answer to Aspects by the rule; but the earth now and then seems sluggish and stubborn: at another time (namely after heavy and lasting configurations) exasperated, it indulges in evaporations even without a continuation of aspects. For Earth is not such an animal as a Dog, prompt at every nod, but such as an Ox or Elephant, slow to anger, and so much the more violent when inflamed.

When this analogy succeeded, it made me pursue it further

f. 0251 · p. 161

comparing also the bodies of Living things with the body of the Earth: I saw that nearly all things which, coming from an animal’s body, testify that a soul is in it, come also from the Earth’s body. [In the margin: Works of the Earthly Soul.] For as a body brings forth hairs on the skin’s surface, so the earth brings forth plants and trees, and among them lice are born there, here caterpillars, cicadas, and various insects and sea monsters; and as the body produces tears, mucus, and earwax, and sometimes gum from pustules on the face, so the Earth produces amber and bitumen; and as the bladder pours out urine, so the mountains pour out rivers; and as the body produces excrement of a sulfurous odor, and blasts of wind which can even be set aflame, so the Earth produces Sulfur, subterranean fires, thunders, lightnings; and as in the veins of a living thing blood is generated, and with it sweat cast out of the body, so in the veins of the earth are generated Metals and minerals, and rain-bearing vapor.

[In the margin: The Earth’s nourishment.] It is therefore necessary that, just as the other Living things draw in food and drink, so also the Earth, through certain channels, draws some matter out of which it may concoct such manifold things, because from nothing nothing is made; and it draws and drinks in seawater, which is the reason why with the endless pourings-in of so many rivers the sea never overflows. How absurd they are who ascribe metals to the working of the Sun alone, without the work of the Earth, you can gather from what was said before.

I also answered in the book On the New Star that common objection, that the Earth, if it had a soul, would seem also obliged to grow [In the margin: What species of Soul is the Earth’s.] and to have limbs fitted for motion: for according as the body is, so the soul and its faculties answer to it; since this soul exists for the sake of the Earth’s body, whereas this body is not made for the sake of the Soul, as the body of man exists for the sake of Mind, the chief faculty of the Soul: if therefore the Earth had needed increases, or any other pasture than what I have said, as by hunting, those offices too would have been entrusted to this Soul, and suitable instruments given: which was the philosophy of the dying Socrates in the Phaedo, attributing all things to deliberation concerning what is best. Therefore, if anyone argues that there are only four faculties of the Soul, none of which befits this Earthly Soul, and that therefore there is no soul in the Earth; I shall bid him add this fifth to the number, by the same precedent and the same laws of reasoning by which those four in number, namely in man, were discovered.

Now to assign a Soul to the Earth with all the greater confidence, I was moved also by other things which, repeated throughout my other books, I gathered into a bundle in the Epitome of Copernican Astronomy, fol. 125: chiefly this, that there is a formative faculty in the bowels of the Earth, which, after the manner of a pregnant woman, expresses in fissile stones human things happening outside, as if it saw them—[In the margin: Imagination of the Earth’s Soul.] such as Soldiers, Monks, Pontiffs, Kings, and whatever is in men’s mouths, new and unwonted garments. This indeed more rarely; that perpetually, [In the margin: Geometry in gems.] that in gems and fossils it expresses the five regular Geometric bodies: for the work testifies of the workman. To these let him who follows Copernicus add [In the margin: Motion of its own body.] the daily, perpetual, and most equable revolution of the Earth’s globe: which he will most rightly reckon among the offices of this Soul.

f. 0252 · p. 162

[In the margin: In the Earth a sense of Touch.] What of this too, that a certain sense either of touch or of hearing seems to be in the globe of the Earth, by this proof, which is confirmed by the constant tradition of very many provinces: if someone, having climbed to the summits of the highest mountains, casts a pebble into the deepest chasms which they have, whence a sound is wont to be aroused, or into a mountain lake (which doubtless also lack a bottom), tempests are straightway stirred up. For so too animals, if someone inserts something tickling into the tender passages of their ears or nostrils, seized with a shudder, shake their head or fling themselves into a run.

[In the margin: Diseases of the Earth.] Certain tracts of the Earth also have their own languors, and internal vicissitudes of their bowels: for sometimes they abound with excessive moisture; sometimes they labor with crudity or imperfection of concoction, when instead of seasonable rains, mere winds come forth; sometimes, as if seized by a burning fever, they sweat out no moisture, but in place of it sulfurous exhalations or pestilent damps. Therefore not unjustly in the book On the New Star, fol. 173, I admonished that all the faculties of concoction—Attractive, Retentive, Expulsive—[In the margin: The Earth’s respiration.] are to be sought in it, since those Diseases are affections of these faculties.

But what is more like the respiration of terrestrial Animals, and especially that reciprocation of Fishes as they drink in waters with the mouth and press them out by turns through the gills, than that wonderful semidiurnal flux and reflux of the Ocean? Which, although it accommodates itself to the motions of the Moon, so that it seemed probable to me, in the Preface to the Commentaries on Mars, that the waves are drawn by the Moon, as iron by a Magnet, by a corporeal virtue of the union [In the margin: Whence the flux and reflux of the Ocean?] of bodies—which also I recently repeated in the prolegomena to the Ephemerides, where I examine the opinion of David Fabricius—yet if someone argues that the Earth accommodates its own respiration as it were to the motion of the Sun and Moon, just as Animals have the same vicissitudes of Sleep and waking with those of day and night, I should judge that he ought to be heard with no unfair ears in philosophy; especially if some indication were added of flexible parts of the Earth in its depth, which might take the place of lungs or gills. For if their nature is such as is that of our air, condensable and extensible, then there will be no need for this respiration of a motion of the Earth’s surface analogous to the motion of the muscles of the diaphragm in a breathing human body.

And what fitter reason could there be for the reception of marine waters inward, as it were into the kitchen of Metals, than this very one, through those perpetual semidiurnal Straits? What else does that marvelous event lead one to suspect, when a few years before the concourse of merchants deserted Antwerp, the Flux and Reflux deserted the Ocean on a certain day (and terrified the city in no slight degree), while the Moon did not desert its course? Doubtless the Earth itself, master of this reciprocation—even though it partakes of a natural motion—held back one respiration of that day, just as Living things sometimes hold their breath, although the motion of the Diaphragm is itself also mixed with natural motion.

Although perhaps we should more rightly prove some respiration necessary to the Earth from the presence of the Soul, than the Soul from respiration.

For now, taking it as an established thing that there is a soul in the Earth, let us come to the contemplation of its essence: [In the margin: The Soul of the Earth is as a flame.] indeed it is not only some light, such as is that of fires and glowworms, depending on itself and not on illumination from the sun— by this proof, that it perceives in some way the luminous rays of the planets—but plainly it seems to be a certain flame (to be nourished, namely, by respiration or drinking in), [In the margin: Whence subterranean heat.] by the proof of perpetual and sensible subterranean heat; of which sort none endures in act in bare matter without a soul, nor is it even in potency in things going forth from the substance of Animals and plants, unless engendered by a Soul and Forms, which are something fiery. See my Optics, fol. 25, 26, 27.

[In the margin: fol. 115. 119. 121.] This we shall assign as the matter, so to speak, of the Soul of the Earth; into which there is now impressed in place of form the image of the divine countenance, [In the margin: The Soul of the Earth is a certain Zodiac.] with the Ideas both of the Circle and all its ratios, and of its sensible body, which it is set to govern, and indeed of the whole World, in which its body was to be. For God has with Himself not only geometric exemplars, but also the concepts of all sensible things to be created; all which pass together into Souls, the exemplars of God, according to each one’s capacity or use. There shines forth therefore in the Soul of the Earth a certain image of the sensible circle of the Zodiac, and indeed of the whole firmament, [In the margin: Fol. 139. 141. Cause of the sympathy of heaven and Earth.] the bond of sympathy of celestial and Terrestrial things; there shine forth much most of all in it the Archetypes of all its offices and of all the motions by which it moves its body in any direction: which others call power (δύναμις), [In the margin: fol. 122. 123. The Soul is an energy.] I would more gladly name activity (ἐνέργεια). For this is the essence of souls, this is as it were a certain streaming (ῥύσις) of this flame, that Souls themselves are always so disposed within themselves as if they were doing that for the performing of which they were made, whether they actually possess the instruments of the body or are hindered. For God is substantial Energy, and subsists by this very energy (that I may stammer in human fashion about divine things): and therefore the essence of the divine image [In the margin: fol. 123.] consists in being active (ἐν τῷ ἐνεργεῖν), as that of flame in streaming (ἐν τῷ ῥύειν): so that unless God eternally sustained it by irradiating its matter as it were, it would suddenly cease and be extinguished. Although for the principle of its individuation there is required not only the body which it is set to govern, [In the margin: Principle of individuation of the Soul.] but also this very material particle of itself (which I described earlier), distinguishing it from other Souls.

Insofar therefore as this Soul bears the idea of the Zodiac Circle, or rather of its center, it also perceives which planet at any time is under which degree of the Zodiac, and measures the angles of the radiations meeting on Earth; and insofar as from the irradiation of the Divine essence [In the margin: fol. 139. fol. 134. 135. 142. The Soul of the Earth knows Aspects by the property of its essence.] it has received the Geometric ratios of the circle and (by comparison of the circle with certain of its parts) the Archetypal Harmonies—not indeed purely geometric, but overlaid, as it were with a kind of sugar of luminous radiations, nay deeply imbued with them—it also judges the measures of angles now recognized, some as Congruous or Harmonic, others as incongruous; [In the margin: fol. 120. 124. fol. 143.] finally, insofar as the same soul embraces the ideas of its works (for each

f. 0254 · p. 164

of which it is as it were a certain operative circle), [In the margin: Fol. 141. 143.] it is also always borne into those its works; yet more remarkably then, if these three circles concur and conspire into one, that is, [In the margin: Perpetual offices of the Soul of the Earth.] if the soul, admonished of itself by Aspects, presses on with its work with some excess. For it never ceases from concocting, nor is this concoction ever without smoke and vapor: indeed out of smoke minerals are made [In the margin: Fol. 130. 131. 132.] (just as Arsenic hardens out of the fumes of metallic furnaces); while out of warm vapor, cooled within the stony crusts of the Earth and condensed into drops, rivers distill to their sources; and out of the same breathing outward above the surface of the Earth, the breathable air, which had fallen down by night gathering into dews, is daily renewed. But this work of the Soul of the Earth, though it is perpetual, yet had need of certain excesses in evaporating—not continuous throughout any whole season, but brought back to certain days—[In the margin: Why excesses by Aspects.] so that from the abundance of vapors sent out, seasonable rains—with sunshine nevertheless running between—might be supplied for refreshing and moistening the surface of the Earth, whence crops and fodder might grow up for Living things.

Someone might object to me the innermost regions of Africa and Peru, lying beneath the equinoctial line, in which places continuous rains fall throughout the whole summer: where then is here the distinction of Aspects from inefficacious configurations? [In the margin: That the Sun also without Aspects stirs up vapors.] Where the vicissitudes of Suns and showers? And does not the course of the Sun, a solitary planet, without respect to configuration with others, alone do all these things? And those not as a Being of reason, but as a natural cause, insofar namely as it intensifies heat and sultriness in those parts, so that at the parts thus parched, attenuated moisture breathes out from the bowels of the earth? I answer that this natural action is not to be denied to the Sun; yet it is not peculiar to the torrid Zone alone, but can be observed among us too. For when the Sun lingers in our Hemisphere in summer, more abundant showers mostly fall than snow in winter, when the sun is absent. [In the margin: How rivers are swelled.] And as for our rivers being swelled by melting snows, which do not seem so much swelled by falling showers—certainly not with such lasting increases—this happens partly because the single layers of snow falling bit by bit are heaped up, and preserved in a storehouse for many months by the force of cold; whereas the earth, parched by summer suns, drinks in the greatest part of summer showers—even the most bountiful—immediately: and partly because the snow in the Alps cannot be dissolved unless most abundant summer showers fall, so that thus those continuous increases of rivers are not from snows alone, but also from the joined showers. Wherefore if you weigh all things, a greater abundance of moisture mostly sweats out during summer even among us than during winter. This therefore the objection obtains, that there is a concurrence of celestial causes, of which one is natural, the heat of the Sun, and another rational, the Aspect; and there is also a concurrence of sublunary causes, so that not only does one Zone have more moisture than another, but also in the same climate one region more than another, from its own causes. Meanwhile even in the Torrid Zone it can rain more at the time of Aspects than on days free from aspects.

Now this characterization of the earthly Soul which I mentioned in the second place, made by the sensible Zodiac and indeed by the whole sphere of the fixed stars,

f. 0255 · p. 165

is also confirmed by this: that by the great agreement of all times it is established that if anything new appears in the sky—whether according to the ordinary course of heaven, [In the margin: Whence Comets and Eclipses have their effects.] such as rarer conjunctions of several stars, or more notable Eclipses of the Luminaries, or outside common nature, such as Comets or new fixed stars—at the same time sublunary Nature also is disturbed by unwonted affections; such as a huge and continuous force of rains beyond the measure of aspects, or the opposite drought and parchedness, and earthquakes accompanying them; and finally unwonted damps of the air, bringing pestilent catarrhs and other epidemic plagues especially to those places whence they breathe out with greater force, or whither more frequent winds carry them.

And further, that something like the Memory of Animals is in this Soul, I noted in the book On the New Star, Ch. X, fol. 44. For this is the Nature of all things that are kindred to light, that aroused by the light of the Sun, or at least of day, they conceive some affection lasting for a certain time. Thus in the eyes the visual Spirits, offspring of light, imbued from an unwary sight of the Sun, wherever the eyes are turned, [In the margin: Fol. 110. 111.] bear and carry this image about, even unwillingly. Thus among the secrets of the Chemists there is this marvelous and especially memorable experiment, that [In the margin: A stone kindled by daylight.] they prepare gems, as I learned very recently from an eyewitness (αὐτόπτης), which, though they lie hidden in darkness like other things devoid of light, yet if anyone exposes them to the light of mere daylight, they are kindled by it like candles, and carry brightness with them even into the dark, shining like the eyes of cats; which in a short time is extinguished again. Something of this kind happens to this soul, which I have said is kindred to light and fire: that in the region (for it is a point distinguished by regions, or a potential Zodiac circle) in which the superior planets met, or an Eclipse appeared, it conceives the character of the conjunction, lasting for some time; [In the margin: Whence the places of conjunctions have their efficacy.] and therefore as often as one of the planets, especially the Sun or Moon, crosses that place, it puts forth its own force such as it would put forth at the stimulation of the conjunction itself. All this business, I say, is like the memory of animals. For I carry about in my mind the species of a man once seen, yet I do not always have him present in my thoughts: but when he or someone like him has appeared again, [In the margin: Memory of the Soul of the Earth.] then anew that former species is drawn forth and set in the act of thinking, by recollection. Although man’s memory is nobler in this, that I am reminded of a remembered thing not only by an outward encounter, but also remind myself as often as I will: because doubtless the faculty of discourse is present in man, which is lacking to this Soul. Yet these things will be clearer in the contemplation of the human Soul.

Now one difficulty, touched upon above as well, yet lightly, must be resolved, concerning the manner and means of reception. For as to our receiving as men the species of sensible things inward into the Soul, the thing seems ready and plain: for the apertures of the pupils lie open, through which they enter; there is the eye, the former of the species; there is the crystalline humor, the temperer of the radiant Pencil [In the margin: How the soul of the Earth perceives an aspect.]; there is the Retiform tunic of the optic nerve, the receiver of the picture of external things. Nothing of the kind appears in the body of the earth, no eye here by which the Soul of the earth may see the radiations of the Planets and their angles: how then shall it feel light without vision, how

f. 0256 · p. 166

shall it perceive or receive angles without an instrument? There is some difficulty, I confess: yet if you look into the matter more deeply, [In the margin: The true manner of seeing.] it is common both here and there. But sluggishness is the death of philosophy: let us live and exercise ourselves.

[In the margin: fol. 135. 139. 140. 143.] First, the soul has obtained the ratio of a point in act (at least by reason of its binding to its body), and the figure of a circle in potency; and since it is Energy, it spreads itself from that seat of a point into a circle; for whether it must sense external things, they stand around it in a spherical manner; or govern a body, [In the margin: How the Soul of the Earth governs its body.] it also has a body cast around it, while it itself lies hidden within, rooted in a certain point of it, whence it goes forth through a species of itself into the rest of the body. But how should it go forth unless through straight lines? For this truly is to go forth; what other manner of going forth could it have, being itself both light and flame, than that in which other lights go forth from their fountains, namely in straight lines? It goes forth therefore toward the outer parts of the body by the same laws by which the surrounding lights of the firmament enter toward it residing in a point. Now since the whole surface of half the terrestrial globe, and indeed all points of the surface of the air with which it is clothed, are irradiated by one and the same Planet with infinite rays, out of them all one single ray tends toward that point which is the seat of the soul; and conversely, although the Soul goes forth to all points of the surface of its body in infinite lines (after the manner of the rays of the Sun, because it too is a certain light, and the commonly celebrated spirits seem to be nothing else than these rectilinear rays and these species of the soul), yet there is one single line out of all which tends from the center to that point of the surface which is struck by that single ray of the Planet tending toward the seat of the Soul. Let us suppose therefore that the perception of the rays of one planet consists in this, when the onset of the one and the meeting of the other coincide into one geometric straight line: by a most similar example of vision, which is perfect and accurate in the one and only perpendicular, the middle ray of the whole eye. Let us also suppose this, which in the Dioptrics, Prop. XLV, I inculcated as a singular secret of all sensations: That no sensation is made of a thing

of the outward thing, except insofar as the sensory organ is affected by that thing. For the Soul, through those outgoings of its own species, has faithful knowledge of all the members of its body, and of those things which arise anew in each of them at any time.

[In the margin: What sensation is, and how it occurs.] These things being posited, it follows that if such an impression is made by a Planet upon some point of air as does not tend toward the seat of the soul, but passes beside it, to the side, the soul indeed receives the species of that point so affected, through the return of its own species, yet disregards it as a thing of nothing; whereas if such an impression be made upon some point of the air’s surface as tends toward the seat of the Soul, this point so affected is also announced to the Soul, and this sensation, as pertaining to the Soul, is esteemed and distinguished from the rest; and thereby in that point alone the Soul imagines the star to itself, and by no means in the others just mentioned. For the ray of the Soul is actuated and as it were illuminated by the ray of the star coinciding in the same straight line, just as the color of visible things is actuated by the arrival of light, and as vision is actuated when we collect ourselves and consider that we see. Since this holds thus not in one only, but also in two planets, the soul will also find two signs in two parts of its body. Whether its body be round, or uneven and rough, the Soul has it cast around itself (sitting at one point) after the manner of a sphere, and by rays or immaterial species of itself—or, as others say, second acts—sent forth round about from itself, it breathes out as it were a perfect spherical form, as far as its body extends; and thereby examines it against the idea of a circle, and indeed of the Zodiac circle, described about its own seat as center, and measures out its distinct parts. Thus there is no need to grant that the Earth is transparent all the way to its center, nor that the soul goes out all the way to the stars; nevertheless the manner is clear by which the angles of configurations are perceived by the Soul.

[In the margin: Optical deceptions have force upon the Soul of the Earth and its works.] By this supposition indeed all the arguments are dissolved which Pico della Mirandola brought forward to overturn the doctrine of Great Conjunctions and of aspects. For since the Soul disposes (by imagining) all the bodies of the world about the point of its seat in the form of a sphere, and all the planets in the form of a circle, and that by the law of straight lines drawn out from one point, it matters nothing whether a star be large or small, whether it accomplishes much or little journey in the heaven, whether it be high or near the Earth. For all these things move the Soul as they are perceived; and they are perceived by the same laws by which they are seen by us, namely, all as equally high, and as lacking the second motion, while in the first diurnal motion equally swift, nor with any great difference of visible size. The rest that can be answered to Pico’s arguments, seek in the oft-cited place of my book On the New Star. Enough for the present concerning the Soul of the Earth, which they call sublunary Nature.

[In the margin: On Mind, fol. 140, 143, 139, 120, 121, 135, 124, 115, 117.] Concerning the chief faculty of the soul, which is called Mind, beyond those things which I brought forward above in Chapter I from Proclus, the business of Radiations admonishes nothing further that I should say. [In the margin: Mathematical Genera are otherwise in the mind.] This one thing I warn concerning Proclus’s argumentation: that it does not seem to rest on good examples, when he insists that lines, surfaces, and points must be posited in the soul because in sensible things there are no pure and separated surfaces, lines, or points. Yet neither in the Soul is there a pure line subsisting by itself; rather it inheres in a mental surface, of which it is the extremity, no less than in sensible bodily quantities a mere surface inheres in the extremity of a body, a mere line in the extremity of a surface, and a mere point at the end of a line: for the BEING of imperfect quantities is TO INHERE IN PERFECT ONES.

[In the margin: Species and the Circle are otherwise.] Namely, Mathematical GENERA are not otherwise in the Soul than the other universals and various concepts abstracted from sensible things: but among Mathematical SPECIES, that which is called the Circle is in the Soul in a far different way, not only as an Idea of external things, but also as a certain form of the Soul itself; finally as the sole storehouse of all Geometrical and Arithmetical knowledge: whereof the former is most evident in the doctrine of Sines, and the latter in the wonderful business of Logarithms; as in which, sprung from the circle, there is a kind of counting-table of all multiplications and divisions that can ever be made, as though already finished. But enough of the Chief faculty of the Soul; let us come now to the lower ones.

[In the margin: On the Vital faculty.] Therefore the vital faculty in Man has with it not only Harmonies wrapped in bright rays, but also Harmonies incrusted in the species of sounds. And sounds indeed it drinks in with the ears, as their proper object; whereas the Rays of the stars it admits not as with the eyes, but in that dimmer mode of perception explained a little above, and examines them against the innate Ideas of Harmony. For those things which are seen by the eyes serve discursive reasoning; the recognition of Harmonies is without discursive reasoning. [In the margin: Scaliger on Ideas.] Thus J. C. Scaliger rightly determines that the idea of the kite is innate in a chick, not indeed simply, but with a mark of peril to be fled.

[In the margin: What power the stars have upon the vital faculty.] Hence therefore human Souls have this property, that around the time of celestial aspects they catch chief impulses for carrying out the affairs which they have in hand. For what the goad is to the ox, the spur or cheer to the horse, the drum and trumpet to the soldier, a rousing speech to an audience, and music on pipe, bagpipe, and lute to a throng of peasants, this is the celestial configuration of suitable Planets to all, and especially when gathered into one assembly; so that both individuals are incited in their meditations and works, and all together become readier to conspire and join right hands. [In the margin: How warlike tumults come from heaven.] Thus in warfare you may commonly see battles, fights, invasions, assaults, stormings, tumults, and Panic terrors arise around the times of the Aspects of Mars and Mercury, Jupiter and Mars, the Sun and Mars, Saturn and Mars, etc.: [In the margin: How pestilences work.] In Epidemic diseases, more people take to their beds, are more grievously tormented, or even die at the time of strong Aspects, Nature namely failing in the struggle with the disease—to which struggle (though not to death) it is incited by the Aspect. All these things the heaven itself does not bring about without a medium; rather the vital faculty of the Soul, associating its own works with the celestial Harmonies, holds the primacy in this so-called influx of heaven. [In the margin: Why the chart of nativity is rather used than the chart of conception.] Now indeed the vital faculty begins to act, and is brought into act, when through birth it is inflamed within in the lamp of the heart, so that henceforth it needs breathing to nourish the flame of life. [In the margin: An exception against Astrology.] Nor yet do I wish all the aphorisms of Astrologers to be defended and confirmed by this one example; nor do I make over to heaven the helm of human affairs: how vast a gulf still separates this philosophical observation from that folly, or, if you prefer to call it, madness! [In the margin: By means of what things does a man’s fortune depend in some measure upon the stars?] There are three things upon which a man’s fortune seems to rest, as to natural things: Mind, Physiognomy of the body, and a tutelary Genius; of the first of which no one doubts, the second is more hidden and not so well known, and of the third I can bring forward nothing save conjectures. Of the first we have treated thus far—namely, of the Mind, on which Character depends; [In the margin: Character is the smith of fortune.] concerning which the common proverb is well worn, that Every man is the smith of his own fortune: in which saying the sum of this whole part of philosophy is contained. Come then, let us set before our eyes in place of all one kind of actions and manners, touched on earlier in the example of the woman. For human affairs are so arranged that there is always a great abundance of dull and popularly wise men, not only among the common herd, but also among Magistrates and in the sacred order; to whom discerning men, critics, and reformers are troublesome, and are held for pettifogging wranglers. In order, therefore, that someone may enjoy prosperous fortune—I mean that which comes from assemblies of men—though he utterly needs both right judgment in moral matters and toil and industry in his own affairs kept apart from the crowd, yet there must be some fatness mixed into his mind, his manners must be popular, and his mode of acting such as pleases the dull populace: minds too subtle and restless, unless they withdraw into some retreat of studies (by this very thing despised and held in contempt), are wont to create misfortunes for themselves.

For when the fetus is mature, the formative faculty of the soul set over generation then chiefly girds itself to thrust forth the fetus, and by that thrusting-forth to kindle a new vital faculty of the soul, when the stars, having returned to the seats of the mother’s or father’s nativity, or into the same configurations, remind the Soul of itself and of its celestial character. See the book On the New Star, fol. 43.

[In the margin: Note the natural foundation of Directions.] What of the fact that this faculty observes not only the true transits of the planets through the places of the natal chart, but also the successions of the parts of heaven which held the planets at the Nativity, into the chief places of the charts that are constituted after whole days have elapsed from birth, observing the proportion of a day to a Year (both revolutions of which are attributed to the Earth by Copernicus), and by that proportion dispenses its works throughout the whole life of the Man, so that by how much longer the Year is than the Day, by so much later the Effect follows its Significator in direction than the Significator in direction follows the birth? So that thus this celestial impression not only endures at rest, but even in enduring propagates itself by a motion of extension into longer times of life. Doubtless this faculty is not only a circle, but an ideal circular motion, and that twofold; it is finally, through motion, also an ideal Time. See above, fol. 117, the words of Proclus. An Example of this thing we can seek again from Memory, which is a slightly higher faculty. For upon a tablet things are painted only with the circumstance of place; in memory, however, deeds are preserved even with the character of time; finally, in this business of Directions, the character of situations and motions

f. 0265 · p. 175

run through in time is multiplied into operations, observing the sensible mundane proportion of the diurnal revolution to the annual translation under the Zodiac; so that the operations now stand apart in time from their stimuli. You might say that along with the Native’s very body, the time of the Genethliacal character of motions also grows.

[In the margin: Whether the length of life or the year of death can be predicted from the stars.] Whatever therefore Astrologers presume to predict from a chart concerning the Length of Life, all of it is drawn together into this head: while the preludes of Directions inform the vital faculty, as from the pulpit of the genethliacal Chart, in what year of life chiefly one or another overflowing humor ought to be driven out of the body or stirred up; [In the margin: What direction is physically.] that is a goad to Nature to accomplish her extraordinary work, which tends to prolonging life: there is no death in the stars, in the Chart, or in the Direction, much less the various forms of Death: [In the margin: The genuine causes of death.] it is the matter of the body which, either overflowing, smothers the flame of life, or failing, abandons it, which hinders the efforts of nature and twists them to destruction: it is sin which deprived the human race of the remedy of immortality once granted, and which even now drives away, hinders, and repels the guardian Angel from the protection of life against the assaults of demons, men, and the elements. But of this matter I should have to treat more fully in Astrology, and I have treated it not too concisely in the book On the Star, fol. 43, and in Tertius Interveniens, No. 65 and following; and perhaps it will come to pass that I publish the books of Giovanni Pico della Mirandola with a commentary, if I understand that I shall do a thing pleasing to students of Philosophy, and am not left destitute of the necessary means.

[In the margin: The reason and force of the Horoscope or ascending degree in a Nativity.] Now because I hasten to an end, I will subjoin a comparison of the vital faculty in man with the Soul of the Earth, for the sake of celestial influx: and this seems to be, that the Soul of the Earth is such a circle as nowhere has a beginning or end, nor is bound from any part of itself; whereas man’s vital faculty is compared to a circle as it were bound by several points: the reason is, that the Soul of the Earth has no Character of nativity, since it always remains the same, and is never born or kindled; whereas a man’s birth, recent and near to death, has received the character of its rising, which punctuates the Zodiac with fixed points of beginning and end. And to the Earth indeed, although you attribute a Genethliacal character from the first day of Creation, [In the margin: The Nativity of the World.] yet the whole expanse of heaven always confronts it, and nothing in it has the nature of beginning or end, rising or setting; to Man the surface of the Earth hides half of heaven, and distinguishes the signs of rising and setting. Thus the reason of the comparison is plain.

[In the margin: The foundation of Crises. What a disease is.] And so true is this comparison of the vital faculty set in act with a circle bound from fixed points, that even at the beginning of a disease (which is as it were a new life—namely, a new energy of the vital faculty, or an extraordinary action of it, natural yet beyond the nature of its wonted constitution, striving to drive out a noxious humor from the body) the place which the moon held flows into the Soul, and conceives a force as of some true Moon immovable in this its degree: and if the true Moon in heaven arrives at its quartile and opposite places, the Nature of the man is spurred on to wrestle with the disease: Upon this rests the doctrine of Crises, of which I treated accurately enough in Tertius Interveniens, No. 70 and following.

Let enough have been said for the present concerning the lower faculty of the human Soul also, and concerning this whole matter.

Furthermore, I have disputed these things concerning the Soul thus far, not indeed with this design, that I might wholly call away students of divine philosophy from reading Metaphysical authors, or that I might blind the eyes of so many interpreters of Plato and Aristotle of deepest inquiry—Greek, Arabic, and Latin: Plotinus, Themistius, Simplicius, Porphyry, Alexander, Averroes, and not a few of his nation whom he cites, and Boethius, and the most recent and most subtle J. C. Scaliger, when they dispute concerning the Soul and Intelligences: but rather that I might add these things drawn from my own profession and from the innermost storehouse of this Harmonic work, and not yet touched by them, so far as I know, to their considerations, as it were in place of a supplement; whence, by those who after me shall give themselves to this Metaphysical contemplation, shall read the said authors, shall compare these my experiments as it were with their reasonings, and shall test each by the file of the other with the freest judgment of reason (which I confess has been done by me at this time least of all), this part of Metaphysical science may finally be established more illustrious, richer, and more complete in all its parts: and that this may turn to the glory of the most blessed Name of God, Creator of Visible and Invisible things, and to the increase of holiness and innocence of life in contemplators, and finally to the eternal salvation of very many Souls, that very Triune God I humbly beseech.

End of Book IV.
