Chapter IV: Difference Between Book III Music & Book IV Planetary Aspects¶

What difference there is between the Harmonies considered in the fourth Book and those in the third.

Of how great moment it is for philosophizing well to distinguish the bounds of things, to compare kindred things together lest they be taken for the same, and to set opposites against each other that they may be illuminated, there is no need to preface with a longer discourse. Wherefore it will be worth the pains to set forth under one view all things useful to this end that have hitherto been spoken either scatteredly or somewhat obscurely or incidentally, and wherever there is need to illustrate them more fully, and expressly to adapt them to the prescribed title of the chapter. Fivefold, therefore, is the difference between the harmonic contemplations of this and of the preceding book: First, in the Harmonic thing itself, by reason of its amplitude; Second, in its sensible Terms; Third, in the cause which procures for Harmony its essence; Fourth, in the manner of inhering; Fifth, in the order of the causes which form the Terms of harmonic proportion.

  1. As to the thing itself which is called Harmonic proportion: those in the third book, arising first from sections of the circle demonstrable through regular plane figures, and presently transferred to straight lines and commingled and conjoined with one another, brought forth no small abundance of harmonic parts (such as Harmonic Sections, Tones, Genera, Modes, Systems, and the like) and among them a certain admirable polity; and almost all that furniture, however great it is, will also have to be scrutinized and applied below in Book V; yet now in this fourth book, although a beginning is made from the same sections of the Circle, we are not going to advance to straight lines, but shall remain within the bounds of the circle throughout the entire compass of our disputation: the cause was stated in the preceding Chapter, and will be stated more fully below in Chapter VI, where we shall also treat at greater length both of the kinship and of the effect of this difference.

  2. As to the Terms of those harmonies, or their sensible subject: in Book III that was Sounds differing in highness and lowness, and therefore they were both referred to the genus of motion and were in a certain way figured motions; whereas in this fourth book they are not voices, as was said in the preamble, nor any motions between which one might recognize harmonies; rather, they reside in the angles which two planets, by luminous rays sent forth, form on the earth, insofar as such an angle can be compared with the four right angles

surrounding a single point of concourse. And here the reader has need of some clear admonition. For I can indeed explain these terms more clearly for perception, but I cannot do so without danger of confusion in philosophizing well unless I diligently forearm the philosophical reader. For since the terms of a proportion must differ in quantity, while the quantity—that is, the measure—of angles is the arc of a circle described from the point of angular concourse, as we are taught in Geometry, in the entire space of the world, which reaches hence from the earth even to the bounds of things, we cannot anywhere describe or perceive by the senses a circle described that is more fit for measuring the angles of rays than that very sensible circle in the highest ether, expressed by the most crowded fixed stars, from which, reduced into certain forms of animals, it has gained the name of the Zodiac; since both the Planets always move beneath it, and each seems to cover one place of it for us by the interposition of its body, and in whose center the Earth, our habitation—not by its center alone, which is a point, but by the bulk of its entire body, over whose surface we humans are disposed—seems to be hidden. Nothing therefore is easier to perceive than if we say, as in the preceding chapter, that the harmonic proportions of which we are going to treat in this book are between the whole circle of the Zodiac and that arc of it which two planets, by that visible interposition of their bodies, seem to mark out, bound, or cut off.

Although this is so ordered, and most rightly pronounced from Geometrical and Astronomical Reasons, yet the Philosophical reader must take the greatest care not to presume in his mind that this harmony (the matter indeed of the fourth book) exists in the heaven itself, and in the circle of the Zodiac, or even in the planets. By no means: for this harmony resides in the parts of the Zodiac not on their own account, since the radiating planets stand at an immense interval below this circle, but because they measure the angles of the rays concurring on the Earth—nay rather, because not they themselves actually measure them, but in their stead the exact Image (Icon) of the celestial Zodiac in the sublunary Soul undergoes this office of measuring; it resides in the rays of the planets not insofar as they individually descend from their own planet, or are offspring of light (though not without this), but insofar as two of two planets joined together here on Earth form a certain harmonic angle. On both counts this subject of harmony is Terrestrial (formally, and insofar as the rays become Terms of harmonic Proportion), and by no means celestial, except only materially and in respect of its own proper BEING (esse), without consideration of Harmonies: namely insofar as the luminous rays—a thing sprung from heaven, yet already descended onto the earth—form the angles made on earth, the proper subject, that is, of this harmony. But of truly celestial harmonies we shall treat expressly in Book V. Briefly, the Terms of the harmonies in the third book were the work of Man or of Art; in this Book IV they will be the work of Nature; finally, in Book V they will be the work of God the Creator.

  1. Now as to the Rational cause which procures for harmonies their BEING (esse): the difference between the Third and Fourth books is none in genus, but only in species. For in Book III harmonies flowed into the senses by reason of

f. 0220 · p. 130

their material subject (sounds), and were received and judged, by reason of their formal essence whereby they are harmonies (that is, they were formed), by an instinct concreated with the Mind, partaking of reason without discourse. And thus far Harmonies were considered only in themselves.

Then indeed, through a hidden yet acknowledged commerce of the faculties of the soul, the harmonies received within were poured over into various affections of the heart through certain likenesses or Images (Icones) of themselves; they were also poured over into the locomotive faculty, so that man might express the species of harmony conceived in his mind not only by voice, but also imitate it by motion of the body. Thus Harmonies took on the part of some cause.

In like manner here too it is necessary to suppose a Soul, into which from the creation of things this criterion (κριτήριον) of Harmonic proportions has been instilled, and which—having received within in whatever way the angle of two radiating stars (whether that be done by something analogous to the senses, or by a property of its own essence as soul, concerning which we spoke also in the preceding chapter, and more will be said in what follows)—estimates it within itself, compares it to four right angles, distinguishes the harmonic from the non-harmonic, and thus procures for the harmony its intellectual BEING (esse), which these angles outside the threshold of the mind did not yet have.

But if it be asked what kind of soul this is, and where or in what body it is placed: I answer, first, that the souls of all men are such. Yet in the third book it was the faculty which presides over hearing, and so over the senses; here it is not the sensitive faculty, for the Eyes, whose object is Light and luminous rays, give no fit indication of the harmonic radiation of two planets. Nor is it the Ratiocinative faculty: for although from Astronomical Observations administered through the eyes reason discovers and computes what aspects exist at any time, yet it does not do this naturally, since not among all men promiscuously, but voluntarily, among a few singularly devoted to astronomy. Rather, human souls are the subject of those harmonies, first by reason of natural instinct, insofar as souls are exemplars of the Creator, as was said in Chapter IV; secondly by reason of the Vital and Natural faculties and the vital and natural motions—or in the Platonic manner, the Concupiscible and Irascible parts—by reason of the former, insofar as they are Harmonies, and by reason of the latter, insofar as the species of Harmonies are imprinted upon these faculties and become the impelling and stimulating causes of the works of Nature, both in mind and in body.

Next, the principal soul into which these Harmonies of radiations flow is that called by philosophers the sublunary Nature, diffused through the whole body of the Earth, our foster-mother, and rooted in some certain part of it not otherwise than the human soul in the heart; from which, as from a hearth, fountain, or innermost shrine, it goes forth through a species of itself into the Ocean poured around the Earth and the Air poured over both.

Just as one who listens to a Musician sweetly singing testifies by the gladness of his brow, by his voice, and by the clapping of hands or feet tempered to the measure of the Melody, that he perceives and approves what is harmonic in the melody; not otherwise does sublunary Nature, by a notable and

f. 0221 · p. 131

evident commotion of the bowels of the Earth, especially on those days when the Wandering stars are harmonically configured by their rays on Earth, testify to what we have already premised: namely, that it is no less able by a certain natural instinct to perceive the harmonic proportions of angles, than it has power by a natural faculty, like our vital one, to heat and stir up the body of the Earth and the subterranean workshops in the mountains at fixed times of Harmonies, so that they exhale a great abundance of vapors and clouds, from which, through Antiperistases of the upper cold, meteors of every kind are formed.

For in the body of the Earth must this soul be placed: because neither do harmonic angles of rays exist in any other part of the world than on the Earth, and the works of Nature which follow upon configurations of rays draw their origin from the bowels of the Earth and the caverns of the mountains.

  1. The fourth difference consists in the manners in which the diverse Harmonies reside in their subjects.

For in Book III, when they resided in Song, they resided in the whole of it—that is, throughout the entire time the song lasted. And although the intervals of voices, like all things partaking of quantity, admit continuous division, yet there was no transit there for the song from a lower sound through infinite intermediates all the way to that which was first consonant or harmonious, but with a leap and silence all intermediates were crossed over, while every vocal lingering was in sounds harmonious with one another. In the same way in Harmony, although between the Diapason and the Dihex (sixth) there lie infinite intervals, yet there was no continuous tightening of the voices through all intermediates until from the Diapason a Sixth should be made, but by a leap the voices were borne from the pure Diapason into the pure Sixth, all intermediates being passed over in silence. And in organs indeed one leaped from one pipe to another; in stringed instruments, such as the Clavier and Harp, from one string to another; or if multi-toned strings were used, as in the Clavichord, Bandora, Lute, and Cithara, from one touch to another; or in pipes, from one finger-hole to another; in the human throat, from one ring of the windpipe to another. Not so in Book IV. For the harmonies which we shall consider in it, as began to be said in the preceding chapter, do not always exist between the angle of two planetary rays and four right angles; rather, there is a continuous separation of the planets—to which indeed this is given—beneath the circle of the Zodiac, through all unharmonious (ἀνάρμοσα) intervals into harmonic ones, whose extreme is two right angles, a semicircle, or opposition; and thence in retrograde order through all inharmonious and dissonant intervals again, all the way into conjunction: no leap is made here from one harmonic angle into another, for example from a Trine into a Square, but the transit from the former to the latter is continuous, through all intermediates. Therefore inharmonious configurations of rays occupy the entire time of celestial motions, yet harmonic configurations of two, or sometimes three, or even four planets punctuate it at least at certain moments, while the rest continue in incongruity; as if seven Organ pipes should emit so many dissonant sounds by a continuous alteration of tension, and by the mutual crossing of tensions it should come about that sometimes

f. 0222 · p. 132

two or three would sound consonant together, while the rest were dissonant. Thus, speaking truly and properly, the very harmonic configurations themselves are not in time, but are perfected in indivisible moments. Yet along with this it is also true that the commotions which exist in Souls from these harmonies are not momentary; for harmonic configurations move insofar as they are in becoming (in fieri), and at the very moment in which they become perfected their goad again relaxes; whereas the works of Nature which are solicited through these goads already receive the measures of their time from the conditions of matter, and often endure far beyond the moment in which the radiation is perfected. Thus a bronze engine when discharged takes fire by the force of kindled gunpowder, nor does it immediately leave off from heat once the fuel of the fire is consumed. Thus the body of an animal, to give a more fitting example, tossed by a febrile paroxysm, although the faculty of the vital soul, author of the inflammation, relaxes from its endeavor—having indeed discharged its office, and the febrile matter being either melted down or cast out from the innermost seats toward the outer parts—is nevertheless not immediately freed from all heat; for it clings long in the matter of the body, in flesh, bones, and nerves, until it too expires with the lapse of time.

These Harmonies, therefore, are of the number of those which above in Chapter II we said are not subject to free providence, but by necessity of motions are mingled with infinite incongruous things of their own kind: for which God ordained minds that might recognize them when they occur. Yet sublunary Nature is far more fortunate in discerning these than are the ears in song. For hearing is not greatly delighted by the Harmony of two voices if five other dissonant ones make a din against them; sublunary Nature, accustomed to perpetual incongruous configurations, accounts them as nothing, because it notices nothing new, whereas it is so intent upon a harmonic angle as though it were alone. Thus when some Prognostic errs a thousand times, this is neglected; but if once it hits the mark, this is judged worthy of memory, this is celebrated in the talk of all.

From these things, therefore, it is given to understand that Harmonies in voices indeed are constituted by the choice and design of the singer, whereas in radiant angles they come forth by no design of sublunary nature, but by mere geometrical necessity of motions. For since two Planets must be separated along the whole length of a semicircle, or 180 Degrees, it necessarily happens that at their fixed moments they are also distant by its harmonic parts, namely 30, 60, 90, 120, etc. degrees. Therefore harmonies of song are sought within by the singer; harmonies of rays are awaited from without by sublunary Nature, observed when they occur, distinguished from non-harmonic ones (and thus from it they receive their BEING [esse]), and are received and applied. Briefly, configurations sing before, and sublunary Nature dances to the laws of this song.

  1. The fifth difference is in a certain manner to be joined to the first. For the Harmonies of this and the preceding book differ not only in breadth, but also in the order of the diverse respects by which Geometrical Figures generate either genus of Harmonic proportions: for in the third book the respect of Knowability (Scibilitas) was prior, whereas in this fourth book that of Congruence will be prior. But of this fifth difference we shall now treat expressly in Chapter V, in the explanation of the Axioms.
