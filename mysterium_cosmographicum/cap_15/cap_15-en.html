Chapter XV: Reconciling Distances & Variations in Prosthaphaeresis¶

f. 0061 · p. 50 f. 0065 · p. 53

Having compared the pure polyhedral sphere ratios against Copernicus's planetary distances in Chapter XIV, Kepler now asks a decisive quantitative question: if we rebuild the planetary system using the exact five-solid intervals—scaling each planet’s eccentricity proportionally—how much do the observable prosthaphaeresis angles (the heliocentric parallaxes / maximum solar elongations actually measured from Earth) deviate from Copernicus’s numbers?

Kepler reminds the reader that Copernicus’s own aim in De revolutionibus (1543) was practical astronomy rather than cosmography: he tuned his orbital numbers to predict planetary longitudes, caring less about slight shifts in absolute orb ratios so long as apparent angles stayed within observational error. To make every eccentric shift fully transparent, Kepler inserts the large engraved folding plate Tabella V (Ad cap. 15, pag. 51), plotting the exact positions of the centers of all eccentric spheres according to Copernicus and the Prutenic Tables at two epochs separated by nearly fourteen centuries:

  • Left diagram: Ad tempora Ptolemaei, circa Annum Christi 140 (showing the maximum eccentricity of Earth’s Orbis Magnus, $4170$ parts in $100{,}000$, aphelion $104{,}170$, perihelion $95{,}830$).

  • Right diagram: Ad tempora Copernici, circa Annum Christi 1525 (showing Earth’s diminished eccentricity, $3219$, along with all five planetary eccentricity circles around the true solar center $A$).

At the close of the chapter (p. 53, folio 0065), Kepler tabulates the resulting apogeal prosthaphaeresis arcs and maximum elongations side by side under three models: (1) polyhedral ratios excluding the Moon’s orb thickness from Earth’s shell, (2) Copernicus’s distances from the Sun, and (3) polyhedral ratios including the Moon’s orb inside Earth’s shell. Including the Moon slashes Mars’s discrepancy from $+2^\circ 47'$ down to $+0^\circ 20'$ and brings Jupiter within $-0^\circ 06'$—while leaving noticeable residuals on Saturn ($-0^\circ 41'$), Mercury ($-1^\circ 01'$), and Venus ($-2^\circ 18'$), setting up the investigations of Chapters XVI–XIX.
