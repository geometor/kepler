Chapter XXI: Reconciling the Motive Soul with the Five Solids¶

What is to be gathered from the defect.

Thus stands this second argument, by which it has been proved on the authority of Aristotle that the new hypotheses are superior, because through them the motions in a twofold respect—both in intensity of power and in swiftness of return—become proportional to the Copernican distances (apostemata), which could in no wise be done in the ancients’ tradition concerning the world. And this indeed ought to have been the sole intention of this treatise on motion.

Yet it is not difficult for me to conjecture that there will be some who will wish that I had omitted this final part of my little work. For (they will say) if you had established the true proportion of the heavens by the bodies, the motions would assuredly confirm it. For truth does not disagree with itself. Yet you yourself see, Kepler, how greatly the motions and the bodies—that is, the distances built up from each side—disagree with one another. Therefore you expose a bare flank to the enemy; nay, you strike yourself, nor is there need that you be slain by another’s sword.

To answer these objections, therefore, I first invert the argument, and I appeal to their own—nay, to everyone’s—judgment and conscience as to which argument they think more probable: the other concerning the bodies, or this concerning motion.

Nor does it seem likely to me that anyone will say otherwise than that this fitting of the motions to the orbs is altogether harmonious, and an admirable handiwork (cheirourgema) of God the Craftsman. Accordingly, if credit is to be given to one or the other argument, they will assent to this one before the bodies, as to a thing more evident, even though the numbers still differ somewhat from the Copernican ones. And if I have obtained this by the Reader’s confession, I shall use it as a confirmation of the bodies and an excuse for that discrepancy, since it is by many parts smaller than this dissonance in motion. For if the Reader here, because of the elegance of the discovery,

readily overlooks a great error, he will far more easily tolerate a small error there. For that divergence in the case of the bodies scarcely disturbs astronomical calculation at all, whereas this one in the case of the motions introduces something rather greater. And this is the first point—namely, a blow returned in kind.

Secondly, since the bodies disagree with the motions, as is truly objected to me, I am certainly forced to confess that one or the other is involved in error. Nevertheless, I think the error can be demonstrated in such a way that it is necessary to abandon neither discovery completely—neither that of the motions nor that of the proportion of the orbs. Which of the two discoveries is at fault is easy to conjecture from what was said above. First, the motive distances depart farther from the Copernican ones than the figural ones do. Secondly, if you compare the motive distances with the Copernican ones, each with each, and note down the defects, you will see a certain kinship of the defects with the numbers themselves, and indeed with the bodies, except in Mercury. More, clearly, in four, less in the fifth. For out of the four, the bodies are always similar in pairs, while the fifth stands solitary.

Next, bring Mercury, variable as it is, into order, and consider that something higher than the middle thickness of its orb ought to be reckoned as its mean distance—namely, as great as the orb of the Octahedron (which, as you heard above, is wider than the mean thickness)—and it will obtain 559, not 500, for its mean distance. This, therefore, will be the order of its numbers: ♀ ☿ 559 | 563 | +4. Behold, in ♄ ♃ and ♀ ☿ the smaller differences, namely 2 and 4; in ♂ Earth and Earth ♀ the larger ones, namely 26 and 43—just as the interposed bodies there, the Cube and Octahedron, and here, the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron, are similar. And observe that where there is a great difference between the inscribed and circumscribed spheres, the difference of distances is small; conversely, where the spheres ascribed to them are nearly equal, the motive distances diverge by a wide interval from the Copernican ones.

Since, therefore, there is a certain regularity in this defect, and indeed nothing ordered happens by chance, we must think that these numbers hint at the truth, though they have not yet wholly attained it. Clearly something in the theorem itself can still be polished, or the theorem indeed holds rightly, but neither operation has grasped its true sense. Although I could have suspected this right from the beginning, I did not wish to deprive the Reader of this occasion and, as it were, spur to further trial. For what if one day we should see that day when both these discoveries will be reconciled? What if from this the reason of the eccentricities could be elicited? For why I cling all the more stubbornly to this theorem on the motions as well, among other things the reason is that the ratio of one motive distance to another never strays from the whole Copernican orb, but always points a finger toward something pertaining to the thickness of the orbs. And in this there is also a certain regularity at which you may marvel. This regularity is that in the planets remote from the Earth the mean distances are very closely approached, whereas in the neighboring ones, Mars and Venus, the motive distance on both sides is nearer to the Earth than the Copernican mean.

You see also that nowhere is a body excluded from its place, nor is the order disturbed, but at the very least a sufficient gap opens between the mean distances to receive the body. So that even if someone should especially wish to accept these motive distances as best demonstrated (about which, however, there is doubt), he might perhaps remove the particular manner of interposing the bodies, yet he would not remove the interposition itself. For the motive distances almost indicate that the two similar outer bodies stand similarly between the mean distances, and the two similar inner ones between mean and extreme—namely, the Dodecahedron from the lowest of Mars to the mean of Earth, the Icosahedron from the mean of Earth to the highest of Venus. And let the Tetrahedron too enjoy its own privileges, and stand between both extremes. Yet let all these things be judged in their proper place—namely, as constructed from the uncertain numbers of the motive distances, and for no other end than that others may be spurred on to a reconciliation, toward which I have led the way.
