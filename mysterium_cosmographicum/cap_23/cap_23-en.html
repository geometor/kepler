Chapter XXIII: Astronomical Beginning & End of the World¶

On the astronomical beginning and end of the world, and the Platonic Year.

After the feast, after weariness from fullness, let us come to the sweets. I pose two noble problems: the first concerns the beginning of motion; the second, its end. Surely God did not establish the motions at random, but from one certain starting point and illustrious conjunction of the stars, and at the beginning of the Zodiac, which the Creator fashioned through the inclination of the Earth our home, because all things are for the sake of man. Therefore if the year of Christ 1595 be referred to 5572 of the world (which is commonly and by the most approved authorities reckoned 5557), Creation will fall upon an illustrious constellation at the beginning of ♈. For in the first year of the assumed count, reckoned backward on the Julian day April 27, the first day of the week, which is the day of Creation of all things, at the eleventh hour of noon in Prussia, which is the sixth hour of the evening in India, the Prutenic calculation exhibits this face of the heavens:

The authority of the ancients wills this, that the World was created in Autumn, and reason itself from Copernicus, that the Earth stand at the same beginning as the rest. Therefore the superiors will appear in ♈, the inferiors and ☉ in ♎. The Moon, since it is around the Earth, fits neither in ♈ nor in ♎, lest it disturb the threefold number of superiors and inferiors. And as the sun sets (for thus was the world founded), nowhere does it rule the night more rightly than from midheaven, which is 0° ♑. Thus too it can stand at the highest apsis of its epicycle; and because its orb is adventitious, let it also obtain an adventitious and special starting place. Its lunations too are its nobility and renown among men, and the chief of lunations is the quarter. Moreover I refer the head to Libra and the tail to Aries, so that it be in a rational position with the Moon, yet without eclipse, and so that the Moon be at its greatest northern limit. Thus the Earth will also stand middle among the stars to the eye, just as its orb obtained the middle place among orbs by God’s sure counsel, since all things are for man’s sake. Yet if you place the Sun in ♈ here too, ♄ will be in ♎, ☽ in ♋, and the rest likewise. Mean motions must be taken, for at the beginning of the course it is fitting that these are true, namely from the apsides. This palm is set in the midst; whoever wins it or its like, whether from calculation or from the restoration of Astronomy, he alone shall have Phyllis. So much for the beginning.

From one postulate I shall prove that no end to motion is established with reason, and that there will be no Platonic year. For let it be granted that eccentricity is in rational proportion with the orb; then the radii of the orbs will be mutually irrational, since they stand as those inscribed in and circumscribed about the solids, which are irrational because they follow from the ratio of the diagonal in a square, and of division in extreme and

f. 0094 · p. 82

mean ratio, which are two examples of irrationals in Geometry. Now the motions are in proportion with the radii; therefore the motions are mutually irrational, and thus they will never return to the same beginning, even should they endure for infinite ages: for never, not even in an infinite division of time, would a common measure occur, by whose repeated multiples a single bound of all motions and the mark of the Platonic year might be set. And now at last I delight to cry out with divine Copernicus: So great indeed is this divine fabric of the Best and Greatest: and with Pliny: Sacred is (the world), immense, whole in whole, nay rather itself the whole, finite and like the infinite.

Do not now forget, friendly Reader, the end of all these things, which is knowledge, wonder, and veneration of the Most Wise Maker. For it is nothing to have advanced from eyes to mind, from sight to contemplation, from the visible course to the Creator’s deepest counsel, if you wish to rest here and are not borne upward in one impulse, with all devotion of soul, into knowledge, love, and worship of the Creator. Wherefore with a pure mind and grateful heart sing with me the following Hymn to the Architect of this most perfect work.

Jehovah, Founder of the World, and our eternal power,
How great is your renown through all the orb of the lands?
How great your glory? Which, given above the ramparts of heaven,
Flies wondrously on shaken wings.
The child knows it, and satisfied, spurning the breast,
Shapes mighty arguments with lisping lips at your dictation:
Arguments whereby the swollen enemy is confounded,
Both despiser of you and despiser of right and justice:
But I, that I may trust the Deity in the spacious orb,
Shall gaze astonished upon the mighty fabric of vast heaven,
Work of the Great Artificer, marvels of a mighty right hand;
How with five rules you marked off the starry orbs,
Within which the middle Steward of Light and Soul
By what law he governs the reins of the eternal course,

What varied turns the Moon takes, what labors,
How many countless stars you scattered through the immense plain.
Greatest Maker of the World, by what reason did it move you
That a small, needy, humble dweller of so tiny a clod,
A son of Adam, should stir up cares for his own affairs?
You look upon the undeserving, raise him on high,
A race scarcely lower than the gods; such great honors you bestow,
And crown his magnificent head with a diadem,
And appoint him King over the monuments of your hands.
What is above his head, the great orbs with their motions,
You subject to his intellect; whatever is created on Earth,
Cattle born for work and fit for smoking altars,
And all the kinds of beasts that inhabit the woods,
And every tribe of birds that beats the air with light wings,
And fishes that swim the tracts of sea and the rivers,
You bid him rule all with empire and your mighty right hand.
Jehovah, Founder of the World, and our eternal power,
How great is your renown through all the orb of the lands?

FINIS.
