Chapter XVIII: Observational Errors in Copernicus & Astronomy’s Precision¶

On the disagreement in general between the prosthaphaereses from the solids and those of Copernicus, and on the exactness of Astronomy.

Above, in Chapters XIIII and XV, when I seemed almost convicted of falsehood by the evidence of the distances which Copernicus published as differing from these figural ones, I appealed to the apogee prosthaphaereses; nor did I beg off condemnation if mine should depart slightly from the Copernican. Yet after I stood similar arcs—prosthaphaereses from elongations from the Sun—as witnesses before this court near the end of Chapter XV, they seemed to testify against me. For there was not one of the Planets that kept the arc assigned by Copernicus: from Saturn I took away 41’; from Jupiter, 6’; to Mars I added 30’; from Venus indeed I subtracted a huge amount, 2° 18’, and from Mercury 61’. Those who wish to examine everything more strictly will therefore judge that, because the calculation of the solids does not agree to the fingernail with the tenets and numbers of Copernicus, all my labor has been wasted. And unless I plead an exception against this, I shall lose my case by my own sentence.

f. 0071 · p. 59

Now to Physicists or Cosmographers—the role I sustain in this little book—I owe no account of this difference. For although they borrow the arguments for their tenets from Astronomers, they do not call them back to calculation as subtly as Astronomers do, nor are they so sharp-sighted or fastidious as to be moved by this trifling difference. Wherefore I have won my case before Cosmographers.

As for the crowd of Astronomers, though I rightly fear them, yet since it is fitting that Masters preside over the judgment, I do not despair even before that tribunal of victory. And first I bid them hope well of the calculation. For although the difference is sometimes rather large, let them remember that the numbers were excerpted from the most conspicuous places of the whole circle and from the concurrence of all inequalities. Nor is the disagreement between the places assigned to the Planets from the solids and from Copernicus so great throughout the whole circle, nor equal in all revolutions. And for my part I judge that even if the Prutenic tables were most certain, and those errors were most truly committed by this interposition of the solids, yet so fitting an argument could not rightly be rejected, because that error would be in the smallest matters. Moreover, not only is it uncertain through whose fault this difference exists, but on the contrary there is strong suspicion and many arguments that the calculation itself and the Prutenic tables are at fault—so much so that it would have been a strong presumption against me had I agreed completely with the numbers of Copernicus.

Let this be the first of those arguments, that the Prutenic calculation not rarely errs in gathering the places of the Planets. Copernicus indeed restored much for us in the collapsed science of the motions, and Astronomy is far purer in our time than in the memory of our fathers. Yet if we look deeply into the matter itself, we shall surely be forced to confess that we are hardly closer to that blessed and desirable perfection than ancient Astronomy is from today’s. Long is the way, and varied the windings, to this truth. The ancients pointed it out to us, our forebears entered upon it, we have outstripped them and stand on a nearer step, yet we have not reached the goal. I do not say this in contempt of Astronomy—it is something to advance so far, if it is not granted to go further— but so that no one may rashly pass a harsher sentence upon this disagreement, and while attacking me and these

f. 0072 · p. 60

solids, insult the very foundations of Astronomy. I appeal to the observations of all Masters: from them one can see how great the difference often is between the true place and that which the calculation indicates, sometimes growing in certain cases to the length of 2 whole degrees. Since this is so, it is expedient for me to depart somewhat from the numbers of Copernicus; and henceforth it is left to the judgment of diligent observers which arcs agree more closely with the heavens, mine or those of Copernicus.

Suspect planetary eccentricities give me a second argument for transferring the blame for this difference onto the Prutenic tables themselves; and it tends to show that although my arcs are not altogether perfect and certain either (as I must confess), yet they caught this flaw from the contagion of the eccentricities. If the solids were built upon spherical surfaces at the mean distance of the planets, so that the same surface touched the centers of the circumscribed solid and the angles of the inscribed, then I would have nothing to do with the thickness of the orbs which the eccentric paths of the Planets require. Since, however, that could not be done, and the cause of the eccentricities—as also of the differences—has not yet been similarly investigated, I had to borrow the thicknesses of the orbs from Copernicus as though certain, although it is confessed that they are not most certain. For although the entire history of celestial motions is of slippery access, through long and difficult observations, this appears especially in establishing eccentricities and the places of Apogees. The Solar (or terrestrial) eccentricity ought to stand most rightly of all; for the Earth is both the nearest of stars to us inhabitants, and is carried by fewer motions than the rest. Yet in constructing the world through interposed solids, we saw above in Chapter XV how great an effect the addition or removal of the lunar ouraniskos alone—which exceeds the thickness of the terrestrial orb by a very tiny portion—has in tightening or loosening all the spheres. See then in how great difficulty even this orb—which ought to have been most certainly measured, and likely could be—is involved in Copernicus, who himself complains in De Revolutionibus, Book 3, Chapter 20, that we are forced to reason about great things through tiny and scarcely perceptible quantities, since sometimes under a single minute of difference 5 or 6 degrees pass by, and a slight error spreads into immensity. How much worse off, therefore, will the thicknesses

f. 0073 · p. 61

of orbs that are both more remote from us and subject to more varieties of motions fare! Yet if either that peche (thickness) of the orbs be most certainly ascertained, or at least probable causes be laid open why so great a measure was assigned to each by the Creator, then I pledge to produce from these solids arcs consonant in every way with the motions. For I judge that whatever still hinders us from attaining an exact knowledge of the motions, after this proportion of the heavens has been found, must all be charged to defects in the eccentricities; once these are removed, I think these five solids will be of great aid to Masters for the correction of motions that many everywhere are pondering.

What moved me to make this pledge regarding eccentricities is also this: that everywhere the dispute concerns a smaller portion than the entire pechos of an orb. For take away from all six orbs their new peche, or assign double to each: you will see the world and all prosthaphaereses collapse and swell immensely in the former case, and be torn apart and diminished in the latter. Thus the truth lies between nothing and double, nor must we fear that a Master will have excessive license to alter eccentricities if anyone tries to fit them to these figures. And so this is the second reason that can excuse me concerning the disagreement between my numbers and those of Copernicus.

A third reason is afforded me by the very numbers of the Prutenic tables, which are still coarse, and not so exact that one cannot sometimes with good leave depart from them even by half a degree. Reinhold indeed arranged everything in the Prutenic tables most diligently. Yet I would not have anyone, baited by this show of scrupulousness, disdain somewhat rough numbers in Astronomy; let him judge the matter more accurately. That minute and scrupulous care of the great man is either for certainty of calculation, or unnecessary in the fractional parts of numbers; while the whole numbers themselves, which he worked out so scrupulously, he excerpted from Copernicus just as he found them.

And how accommodating Copernicus himself is in accepting whatever numbers turn out somewhat to his wish and serve his purpose, the diligent reader of Copernicus will discover. Numbers which by force of demonstration ought to agree completely across different operations, he does not reject, even though they differ by several minutes. Observations in Walther, in Ptolemy, and elsewhere he reads in such a way

f. 0074 · p. 62

as to use them the more conveniently for constructing his calculation; hence he has no scruple sometimes to neglect or alter hours in time, or quarters of degrees and more in arcs. Elsewhere, as in the altered eccentricity of Mars and Venus, he even accepts sines differing from the truth, merely because they point slightly toward those he desires. Many things which by his own confession ought to have been amended, he draws whole and untouched from Ptolemy, even after changing other similar things; and upon them he afterwards builds the foundations of a new Astronomy. Of all these things Maestlin gave me very many proofs, which for brevity’s sake I omit to write down. Indeed he would rightly seem to incur censure, had he not done so deliberately, because it was better to have an Astronomy somewhat imperfect than none at all. For difficulties of this kind will occur as long as the stars run their courses; to overcome them, and unhindered to aspire to establishing the science with the least loss, as Copernicus dared to do, is the part of a brave man; it is for the slothful to shirk, and for the timid to despair and cast away all this care. Just as Copernicus himself neither conceals nor shamefully confesses these sphalmata (slips) of his just recounted. He fortifies himself with the example of Ptolemy and the ancients, excuses himself by the difficulty of observing, and everywhere sets an example for others of disregarding these tiny defects in confirming noble discoveries; had this not been done before, Ptolemy would never have given us that Megale Syntaxis, Copernicus his books On the Revolutions, nor Reinhold his Prutenic Tables.

Nor does that table of Maestlin inserted in Chapter XV supply me with no excuse in the fourth place. When Copernicus borrowed the eccentricities of the Planets from Ptolemy, nothing was further from his mind than this divine proportion of the heavens—so that one may rightly marvel greatly that he came so close to it— nor did he suppose that necessity would ever compel us to inquire into distances from the Sun and the places of the aphelia. What wonder then, if in this cutting to the quick and analysis of the world many crude things are found, since the master paid no heed to the tiniest details? Just as in a small painting that scarcely expresses the whole face to the senses, if anyone seeks the true proportion of an eye or pupil, he must necessarily be deceived. For the painter neglected this because of its tininess, content if he could somehow represent what is more conspicuous.

f. 0075 · p. 63

So although I approached this analysis with the best reason, compelled by the force of demonstration and the condition of the proposed matter, yet I would not have anyone persuade himself that he has brought back from it absolutely most certain numbers. For it can happen that this very dissection has been the cause of further error. Behold no slight signs: Copernicus attributes the cause why the eccentricities of Mars and Venus change to the change in the terrestrial eccentricity. Therefore their true eccentricity from the Sun does not change; you have the demonstration before your eyes in the table. If this is so, the eccentricities from the Earth in Ptolemy’s age and in ours ought to lead to the same result, and from both to conclude the same eccentricity from the Sun. Yet consult the calculation: you will see this does not happen as was fitting. For the aphelion eccentricities also come out mutually discrepant. Let the same be said of the places of the aphelia, because these are mutually connected. And this is one point.

Next, it is easily gathered from inspecting the table that since aphelia and apogees advance unequally, a great diversity of eccentricities will arise thence over the ages. Today the apsides of Saturn and Earth are nearly conjoined; hence Saturn’s distance from the terrestrial orb’s center is less than from the Sun by Earth’s whole eccentricity. When they are a quadrant apart, each distance from Sun and Earth will be equal; indeed, for Copernicus, Saturn’s eccentricity will grow until Saturn’s and Earth’s apsides are opposite. Though the world will not endure to that event, yet were Astronomy perfect, it ought to use hypotheses sufficient as for an eternal world. Yet neither Copernicus nor Reinhold warns us of any of these things. Therefore their numbers are not most perfect, nor do they unfold to us the whole spheres of the planets by which we might understand how those late motions can happen.

When these and like matters troubled me not a little, and I stood at a loss, like one unable to set in order the scattered wheels of an automaton, Maestlin consoled me—nay, dissuaded me from these subtleties: we cannot, said he, exhaust all nature’s treasures; an evil well laid to rest should not be stirred, and this, as it were, rupture of a human body should rather be endured and supported with certain reliefs than that by so exquisite an anatomy the patient should be cast into the most immediate peril of life. He brought forward

f. 0076 · p. 64

to me the example of Rheticus, whose diligence was curious to the fingernail like mine, and Copernicus reproving him on his own behalf. There is a letter of Rheticus prefixed to the Ephemeris of 1551; since it is not everywhere at hand and wonderfully aids this whole chapter in many places, I will append its chief parts as a colophon. Thus then Rheticus to the reader, among other things:

He (Copernicus) wished his investigations to be moderate, not excessive. Therefore deliberately, not from laziness or weariness of fatigue, he avoided those minute subdivisions which some have affected and some demand, such as Peuerbach’s subtlety in his Tables of Eclipses. You may see some spend all their care on these things, examining the stars’ places quite scrupulously; yet while they gape after seconds, thirds, fourths, and fifths, meanwhile they skip over whole degrees and miss them, and in the timing of phenomena often stray by hours, and sometimes even by whole days. This is surely what happens in Aesop’s fables to him who, bidden to bring back a lost ox, while intent on catching little birds, neither gets the birds and loses the ox as well. I remember when I too was driven by youthful curiosity, and longed to enter, as it were, into the inner sanctuaries of the stars. Therefore about this exactness I sometimes even wrangled with that best and greatest man, Copernicus. But he, while delighted with my mind’s honorable eagerness, used to chide me with a gentle hand, and urge me to learn also to lift my hand from the tablet: “If I,” said he, “can bring the truth within sixths of a degree, which are ten minutes, I shall exult no less in spirit than we hear Pythagoras did upon finding the rule of the square.” When I marveled and said we should strive for greater certainty, he showed that even this point would be reached with difficulty, especially for three reasons. The first of these, he said, was that he noticed most observations of the ancients were not uncorrupted, but fitted to that doctrine of motions which each had set up for himself. Therefore singular attention and diligence were needed to separate from the corrupted ones those to which the observer’s preconception had added or subtracted nothing, or very little. The second cause, he said, was that the places of the fixed stars were sought out by the ancients no further than to sixths of a degree; and yet the positions of the wandering stars must be taken chiefly by reference to these; he excepted a few in which the recorded declination of the star from the equator helped the matter, because from this the star’s own place could then be determined more certainly. The third cause he mentioned: we lack such authorities as Ptolemy had after the Babylonians and Chaldeans—those lights of the art, Hipparchus, Timocharis, Menelaus, and the rest, on whose observations and precepts we might rely. He indeed preferred to rest in those things

f. 0077 · p. 65

whose truth he could profess, rather than display sharpness of wit in a doubtful subtlety of ambiguous things. Indeed his indications would certainly be no farther, or even altogether nearer, than a sixth or a quarter of one degree from the truth; and so far was he from repenting of this shortcoming, that he rejoiced greatly to have been able to advance thus far over a long time, with immense labor, utmost exertion, and singular zeal. Mercury indeed, as in the Greek proverb, he left in the middle as common property; for he said he had neither observed it himself nor received from others anything to help him much or that he could wholly approve. Advising, suggesting, and enjoining many things upon me, he above all urged me to devote effort to observing the fixed stars, especially those that appear in the zodiac, because with them the conjunctions of the wandering stars could be noted, etc.

Thus far from Rheticus’s letter what was to the purpose. What now do you think of Copernicus, friendly Reader? Had he been advised of this matter and discovered how close he stood to it with his own calculations, what do you think he would not have attempted, what labor would he not have undertaken, to reconcile the solids with his orbs? And if this were granted, what harmony, what perfection might not be hoped for? Time will teach what others, and what Maestlin himself, God willing, will someday accomplish in this matter. Meanwhile, let no one pronounce rashly against me, and let him bear with an equable mind this adjournment of the suit.
