Chapter VI: The Tetrahedron Between Jupiter and Mars¶

Kepler next shows why the Tetrahedron (Pyramis, $\{3, 3\}$) immediately follows the Cube, occupying the vast second interval between Jupiter and Mars—thereby leaving the remaining primary solid, the Dodecahedron, necessarily in third place between Mars and Earth.

Nine geometric arguments confirm the Pyramid’s second rank:

  1. Rival to the Cube: The Tetrahedron is the sole body capable of nearly contesting primacy with the Cube itself.

  2. Pyramidal decomposition of all solids: Just as every plane polygon resolves into triangles, every polyhedron resolves into pyramids meeting at its center (the Icosahedron into 20, the Octahedron into 8, and the Dodecahedron into 12 pentagonal pyramids—which then measure cleanly against the Cube).

  3. Self-similar subdivision: Slicing through the midpoints of a Tetrahedron’s six edges resolves it cleanly into four congruent half-scale regular tetrahedra surrounding one central regular octahedron of half edge-length.

  4. Rational-square simplicity: Most of the Tetrahedron’s altitudes, edges, and circumradii belong—like the Cube’s—purely to rational square (quadrance) proportions rather than golden irrationals.

  5. Rigidity from side lengths alone: Whereas an equilateral hexahedron can shear into infinitely many oblique rhombohedra unless right angles are prescribed, specifying six equal edges locks the regular Tetrahedron rigidly into a unique shape—the very reason builders first frame posts vertically at right angles (Cube) and then brace them diagonally with triangles (Pyramid).

  6. Angle priority: Having the acute angle—simpler and further from infinitude than the obtuse—places the Tetrahedron ahead of the obtuse-angled Dodecahedron, and its face count of 4 stands immediately adjacent to the perfect number 6 of the Cube’s faces.

With the Cube first and the Tetrahedron second, the Dodecahedron takes the third primary interval between Mars and Earth without ambiguity.
