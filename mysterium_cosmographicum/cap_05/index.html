Chapter V: The Cube Between Saturn and Jupiter¶

That the cube is the first of the bodies, and between the highest planets.

Let us come now to the three primary bodies, and assign to each its own space. And the Cube indeed had to draw near to the fixed stars, and establish the first proportion, which lies between Saturn and Jupiter: because the most worthy part of the world outside the Earth are the fixed stars, like the circumference of a circle (after the center), while the Cube is the first body in its order. 1. For it alone is generated from its own base, whereas the remaining four are not generated by their faces, but are either cut out of the Cube, as the Pyramid, by casting off 4 right-angled pyramids, or augmented, as the Dodecahedron, by affixing six pentahedra. 2. It alone into homogeneous cubes without a pris-

m can be resolved. 3. It alone is everywhere alike, and stretches into three straight dimensions. For the faces of the rest are inclined, and other cubes, though they yield to two straight cuts, cheat the cutter in the remaining one. 4. Hence it is that it alone has as many faces as three-fold dimension has bounds, namely six, and twice that number of edges, namely twelve. 5. It alone has everywhere an equal angle, namely a right angle. But in a Pyramid a rule that sits flat, when applied to the middle of the faces, disagrees if you twist it toward an angle; nor do the solid angles square with that rule which measures the long lateral angle lying between. 6. Hence too to it alone belongs what Simplicius on Aristotle, De Caelo Book 1, Chapter 1, cites from Ptolemy's single-book treatise (monobiblos) as the cause of perfection in the ternary: namely, that no more than three straight lines perpendicular to one another can meet at a solid place to divide it into right solid spaces. 7. It is the simplest body of all rectilinear solids. Although this is disputed in the case of the Pyramid, yet it is easily proved from the fact that the Cube is the measure of the pyramid, and it is fitting that the measure should come first. And it is the measure not merely by human convention—since whatever solids men measure, they conceive its quantity in mind by means of little cubes—but much more by Nature. For a Right Angle is equal to that other with which it is extended into a plane. Therefore it is perpetually equal to itself, and thus unique, whereas of the others on both sides there are infinitely many; and it is fitting that a measure be one and the same, and also finite. 8. Hence so fruitful is the inscription of a right angle in a circle, without whose mediation neither a triangle, nor a pentagon, nor those derived from them can be inscribed. 9. Nor should this be passed over: that skillful nature most perfectly bestowed those same six dimensions (diastaseis) upon the most perfect living creature—no obscure proof of how highly this body is prized by her. For man himself is, as it were, a kind of cube, in whom there are six quarters as it were: above, below, before, behind, right, left.
