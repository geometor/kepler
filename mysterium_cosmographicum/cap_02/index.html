Chapter II: First Outline of the Demonstration¶

f. 0025 · p. 18

First outline of the demonstration.

Having premised these things, in order that I may come to my main purpose and prove the hypotheses of Copernicus just recounted concerning the new world by a new argument, I shall trace the matter back to the very beginning—to the “first egg,” as the saying goes—with whatever brevity can be achieved.

f. 0028 · p. 19

Body was that which God created at the beginning; and if we possess its definition, I think it will be tolerably clear why God created body at the beginning rather than anything else. I say that quantity was set before God as His purpose, to attain which everything was needed that belongs to the essence of body: so that thus the quantity of body, insofar as it is body, is a kind of form and the origin of its Definition. Now God willed quantity to exist before all else so that there might be a comparison of the Curved to the Straight. In this one matter above all, Nicholas of Cusa (De Docta Ignorantia, 1440) and others seem truly divine to me: that they esteemed so highly the mutual relationship of the Straight and the Curved, and dared to liken the Curved to God, and the Straight to creatures—so that those who have tried to liken the Creator to creatures, God to man, and divine judgments to human ones have performed scarcely more useful work than those who have striven to equate a curved line to a straight, or a circle to a square.

And even though in this alone the fitness of quantities and the nobility of the curved would have stood firmly enough with God, yet another and far greater consideration joined it: the image of the Triune God in the Spherical surface—namely, of the Father in the center, of the Son in the surface, and of the Spirit in the equality of relation (schesis) between the center-point and the circumference. For what Cusanus attributed to the circle, and others perhaps to the globe, I claim for the Spherical Surface alone. Nor can I be persuaded that among curved things anything is nobler or more perfect than the Spherical surface itself. For a globe is more than a spherical surface, and is mingled with straightness, by which alone it is filled within; whereas a circle does not exist except in a straight plane—that is, unless a spherical surface or a globe be cut by a flat plane, there will be no circle. Hence it may be seen that many properties flow down secondarily there from the Cube into the globe, and here from the square into the circle, on account of the straightness of the diameter.

But why, in fine, were the distinctions of the Curved and the Straight, and the nobility of the curved, set before God in adorning the world? Why indeed, unless because by the most perfect Maker it was altogether necessary that a most beautiful work should be fashioned? For neither is it right, nor has it ever been (as Cicero speaks from Plato's Timaeus in his book On the Universe) for him who is best to make anything except what is most beautiful. Since, then, the Creator preconceived in His mind the Idea of the world (we speak in human fashion, that as human beings we may understand), and an Idea is of a prior thing—and, as has just been said, of the best thing, so that the form of the work to be made may itself also become the best—it is manifest that by those laws which God Himself in His goodness prescribes to Himself,

f. 0029 · p. 20

He could take up the Idea of nothing else for establishing the world than of His own essence; which can be considered in a twofold way as to how preeminent and divine it is: first in itself, insofar as it is one in essence and three in persons; and secondly by comparison made with creatures.

This image, this Idea, He willed to impress upon the world, so that it might become best and most beautiful; and in order that the world might be able to receive it, He created Quantity (Quantum), and the All-Wise Maker devised quantities whose whole essence, so to speak, should fall into these two distinctions, the Straight and the Curved—of which the Curved should represent God to us in those two ways just mentioned. Nor is it to be supposed that distinctions so apt for prefiguring God came into existence by chance, as though God had not thought of these very things, but had created quantified body for other causes and by another design, and afterward the comparison of the Straight and the Curved, and this likeness to God, arose of its own accord as if fortuitously.

Nay rather, it is probable that at the beginning of all things the Curved and the Straight were chosen by God with fixed design to shadow forth in the world the divinity of the Maker; and that in order for these to exist, quantities existed; and that in order for quantity to be had, Body was created first of all.

Let us now see in what manner the supreme Creator employed these quantities in the fabric of the world, and what it is probable by our reasonings was done by the Maker, so that afterward we may seek that very design both in the Ancient and in the new hypotheses, and award the palm to him with whom it shall be found.

That the whole world, then, is enclosed by a spherical figure, Aristotle has argued abundantly enough, drawing proofs among other things from the nobility of the spherical surface; and by these even today Copernicus’s outermost sphere of the fixed stars, although devoid of motion, maintains the same figure, and receives the Sun as its center into its inmost bosom. And that the remaining orbs are round, the circular motion of the stars proves. That the Curved, therefore, was employed for the adornment of the world needs no further proof. Since, however, we see three kinds of quantities in the world—figure, number, and magnitude of bodies—we find the Curved thus far in figure alone. For there is no reason for magnitude from the fact that what is inscribed in its like (a sphere in a sphere, a circle

f. 0030 · p. 21

in a circle) around the same Center either touches everywhere or nowhere; and the Spherical itself, since it is sole and unique in its genus of quantity, cannot be the subject of any other number than the ternary. If, therefore, God had looked solely to the Curved in creation, nothing would exist in this cosmic edifice except the Sun at the center (image of the Father), the sphere of the fixed stars or the Mosaic waters on the circumference (image of the Son), and the celestial aura filling all things, or that expanse and firmament which would be the image of the Spirit—nothing, I say, besides these. Now, however, since the fixed stars are innumerable, and the catalogue of the moving stars is far from uncertain, and the sizes of the heavens are unequal to one another, we must seek the causes of all these from straightness—unless perchance we are to suppose God made anything in the world at random when the best reasons were at hand: a notion no one will persuade me to hold even concerning the fixed stars, whose placement nonetheless appears to us exceedingly confused, like a chance scattering of seed.

Let us come, then, to Straight quantities. Just as earlier the Spherical surface was taken because it was the most perfect curved quantity, so now let us pass at one leap to solids, as those among Straight things which are complete quantities and consist of three dimensions; for it is fitting that the Idea of the world be complete. Let us cast straight lines and plane surfaces out of a finite, most ordered, most beautiful world, as infinite and therefore utterly incapable of order. Again, out of solids—whose infinities are infinite in kind—let us select some by holding a census through fixed marks: for instance, those that have either edges, or angles, or faces equal to one another, whether singly or alternately or combined in any constant way, so that with good reason we may arrive at something finite. And if any kind of solid described by fixed conditions remains finite in the number of its species, yet multiplies into a vast abundance of numbers, let us apply the angles and face-centers of those solids to demonstrating the multitude, magnitude, and placement of the fixed stars, if we can; or if that labor is beyond human reach, let us put off seeking the reason for their number and placement until someone has described to us one by one how many and how great they all are. Leaving the fixed stars aside, then, and entrusting them to Him who alone counts the multitude of the stars and calls each one by name (Ps. 147), the All-Wise Artificer, let us turn our eyes to the neighboring, few, and moving bodies.

f. 0031 · p. 22

In fine, therefore, if we make a selection of solids and cast out the whole crowd of mixed ones, retaining only those all of whose faces are both equilateral and equiangular, there will remain to us these five Regular Solids, to which the Greeks gave these names: the Cube or Hexahedron, the Pyramid or Tetrahedron, the Dodecahedron, the Icosahedron, and the Octahedron. And that there cannot be more than these five, see Euclid, Book XIII, the scholium after Proposition 18 (Elements XIII.18).

Wherefore, just as the number of these solids is definite and exceedingly small, while the species of all other solids are either innumerable or infinite, so it was fitting that there be in the world two kinds of stars, distinguished from one another by a manifest boundary (such as motion and rest), of which one kind should be akin to the infinite, like the fixed stars, and the other narrow in number, like the Planets. This is not the place to dispute the causes why the latter move and the former do not; but granting that the Planets needed motion, it follows that to obtain it they had to receive round orbs.

We have the orb for the sake of motion, and the solids for the sake of number and magnitudes: what remains further, save to say with Plato, God ever geometrizes, and in this fabric of moving bodies He inscribed solids in orbs, and orbs in solids, until no solid remained left over that was not clothed both within and without by moving orbs? For from Propositions 13, 14, 15, 16, and 17 of Book XIII of Euclid it is plain how apt these solids are by their very nature for this inscription and circumscription. Wherefore, if five solids are inserted one inside another with intervening and enclosing orbs, we shall obtain the number of six orbs.

If, then, any age of the world has reasoned in this fashion about the arrangement of the world, so as to set six orbs in motion around the immobile Sun, it has assuredly handed down the true Astronomy. Now Copernicus has six such orbs, and each adjacent pair in such a proportion to one another that all five of these solids can be most fittingly placed between them—which will be the sum of what follows. Therefore he deserves to be heard at least until someone either brings forward hypotheses better suited to these philosophical truths, or shows how what has been deduced by the best reasoning from the very principles of nature could have crept by chance into numbers and into the human mind. For what could be said or imagined more admirable, or more persuasive,

f. 0032 · p. 23

than that those very things which Copernicus—from the appearances, from effects, a posteriori, steadying his step as though a blind man with a staff (as he himself used to say to Rheticus)—established by a fortunate rather than confident conjecture, and believed to be so: all those things, I say, are found to have been most rightly established by reasons deduced a priori, from causes, from the Idea of Creation?

Now if anyone should wish to receive these philosophical reasons without reasons, and mock them with mere laughter—because I, a newcomer near the end of the ages, bring forward these philosophical truths while those ancient lights of Philosophy kept silent—against him I shall bring forward a leader, author, and forerunner from remotest antiquity, Pythagoras, of whom there is frequent mention in the schools because, seeing the preeminence of the five Solids, in manifestly the same manner two thousand years ago as I do today, he judged it not unworthy of the Creator’s care to have regard to them; though having judged mathematical things physically, and by some accidental property of each, he adapted non-mathematical things to them. For he likened the Earth to the Cube, because both are stable—which yet is not properly said of the cube. To Heaven he gave the Icosahedron, because both roll readily; to Fire the Pyramid, because this is the shape of a darting tongue of flame; and the remaining two solids he distributed between air and water, because of the mutual likeness of each with its neighbors. What that man lacked, however, was a Copernicus to tell him first what actually existed in the world; had he not lacked him, there is no doubt he would have discovered why it was so, and this proportion of the heavens would now be as well known as the five solids themselves—and just as widely received as, in the course of these ages, that opinion concerning the motion of the Sun and the rest of the Earth has grown strong.

Come now, then, let us test at last whether between the orbs of Copernicus those proportions of the solids obtain. And at the outset let us weigh the matter roughly. The greatest difference of distances in Copernicus lies between Jupiter and Mars, as you see in the explanation of the hypotheses in Plate I, and below in Chapters 14 and 15; for Mars’s distance from the Sun does not equal a third part of Jupiter’s. Let us seek, therefore, the solid that makes the greatest difference between its circumscribed and inscribed orbs (let this catachresis of taking a hollow space for a solid be granted us), which is the Tetrahedron or Pyramid. Between Jupiter and Mars, therefore, stands the Pyramid. Next after these, let the distances of Jupiter and

f. 0033 · p. 24

Saturn make the greatest difference; for the former equals a little more than half the latter. A like difference appears between the innermost and outermost spheres of the cube. Saturn therefore surrounds the Cube, and the Cube surrounds Jupiter.

Nearly an equal proportion holds between Venus and Mercury, and not unlike that between the orbs of the Octahedron. Venus therefore surrounds this solid, and Mercury wears it within.

The remaining two proportions—between Venus and the Earth, and between the Earth and Mars—are the smallest, and nearly equal, namely the inner is three-quarters or two-thirds of the outer. In the Icosahedron and Dodecahedron the distances of the two spheres are likewise equal, and they employ the smallest proportion among the remaining regular solids. Wherefore it is probable that Mars surrounds the Earth through the mediation of one of these two solids, and that the Earth is separated from Venus through the mediation of the other. Hence if anyone asks me why there are only six moving orbs, I shall answer: because there ought not to be more than five proportions—as many, namely, as there are regular solids in mathematics; and six terms complete this number of proportions.

Annotation to the Second Chapter.

And that than these five…) The nobility of the Solids comes from simplicity, and from the equality of the distance of their faces from the center of the figure. For just as God is the norm and rule of creatures, so is the Sphere of solids. Now the sphere has the said properties: 1. It is simplest, because it is bounded by one limit, namely itself. 2. All its points are most equally distant from the center. Among solids, therefore, the regular ones approach nearest to the perfection of the Sphere. Their definition is this, that they have 1. all edges, 2. faces, and 3. angles each equal both in kind and in magnitude, which belongs to simplicity; and from this posited definition there follows of its own accord: 4. that the centers of all faces are equally distant from the middle; 5. that when inscribed they touch the sphere’s surface with all their vertices; 6. that they fit snugly against it; 7. that they touch the inscribed globe with all the centers of their faces; 8. that consequently the inscribed globe sits immovable; and 9. that it has the same center as the figure. By these things is wrought a second likeness to the Sphere, which arises from the equality of distance of the faces.

f. 0035 · p. 25

That Scholium, moreover, runs thus: I assert, then, that besides the said five figures no other solid figure can be constructed which is contained by equilateral and equiangular planes equal to one another. For from two triangles—nor indeed from any other two figures—will a solid angle be constructed.

But from three triangles the angle of the Pyramid consists;

from four, that of the Octahedron;

from five, that of the Icosahedron.

For from six equilateral and equiangular triangles meeting at the same point, no solid angle will be formed. Since the angle of an equilateral triangle contains two-thirds of one right angle, six such angles will be equal to four right angles—which cannot be. For every solid angle is contained by angles fewer than four right angles, by Book XI, Proposition 21.

For the very same causes, neither does such a solid angle consist of more than six planes.

But from three squares the angle of the Cube is contained.

From four none can be; for again they will be four right angles.

From three equilateral and equiangular pentagons, moreover, the angle of the Dodecahedron is contained. But from four none can be; for since the angle of an equilateral pentagon is one right angle and a fifth part of a right angle, four angles will be greater than four right angles—which cannot be.

Nor indeed from other polygonal figures will a solid angle be contained, because from this also an absurdity would follow. Wherefore it is clear that besides the said five, no other solid figure can be constructed which is contained under equilateral and equiangular planes.
