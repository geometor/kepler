Preface to the Reader: The Graz Discovery¶

It is my intention, gentle Reader, to demonstrate in this little book that the Most Good and Great Creator, in founding this mobile world and arranging the order of the heavenly orbs, looked upon those five regular bodies that have earned such celebrated renown from the time of Pythagoras and Plato down to our own day, and that to their nature He accommodated the number of the heavens, their proportions, and the law of their motions.

When I was living at Tübingen six years ago and attending the lectures of the distinguished master Michael Mästlin, I perceived how encumbered with difficulties the customary way of conceiving the world was; and I took such delight in Copernicus, whom my teacher frequently mentioned in his lectures, that I not only defended his views repeatedly in physical disputations among the students, but even composed an accurate disputation attributing the first motion of the heavens to the rotation of the Earth. Already at that time I began to inquire why things stood precisely thus and not otherwise: namely, what caused there to be six mobile orbs around the immobile Sun—neither twenty nor a hundred—and what determined the magnitudes of the intervals separating Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Earth, Venus, and Mercury.

At first I pursued numerical proportions, testing whether one orb might be double, triple, or quadruple another; when arithmetic failed, I inserted new invisible planets between Mars and Jupiter and between Venus and Mercury, and tested trigonometric chord distances along a quadrant arc (A–D–B–C), yet still no finite law emerged.

Almost an entire summer was spent in this arduous toil. At last, upon a quite slight occasion, I broke nearer to the very truth itself. On the ninth (or nineteenth, New Style) day of July in the year 1595, while showing my students at the chalkboard in Graz how the Great Conjunctions of Saturn and Jupiter leap successively across eight signs of the zodiac from one fiery trigon to the next, I inscribed a succession of equilateral triangles within the zodiacal circle and noticed that the intersection of their chords traced out an inner enveloped circle whose radius OM appeared visibly half the radius OA of the circumscribed outer circle cSAT—almost matching the true astronomical proportion between the orb of Saturn (cSAT) and the orb of Jupiter (cJUP)!

In the planar figure above, the equilateral triangle ABC inscribed within Saturn’s circle cSAT about center O touches Jupiter’s inscribed circle cJUP at midpoint M, yielding the exact radius ratio $OM : OA = 1 : 2$ and quadrance ratio $Q(O, M) / Q(O, A) = \frac{1}{4}$ ([XIII.12]).

Immediately I attempted to separate Mars and Jupiter with a square, Earth and Mars with a pentagon, and the remaining intervals with a hexagon, heptagon, and so forth. Yet I saw at once that of regular plane polygons there is an infinite multitude, whereas in the sky there are only five intervals between the six mobile Copernican orbs! Wherefore, cried I: “Why do I seek two-dimensional plane polygons for three-dimensional solid orbs? Behold, solid bodies must be interposed between solid spheres! And behold, reader, the invention and entire substance of this little treatise!” For by Euclid's final theorem ([XIII.18]), in all of three-dimensional geometry there exist five, and strictly no more than five, convex regular solid bodies!

It is a thing worthy of wonder that, although I had not yet settled the prerogatives of each body in the sequence, using a conjecture by no means subtle drawn from the known distances of the planets, I hit the mark so happily in the order

of the bodies that afterward, when I examined them by exact demonstrations, I could change nothing in them. In memory of the event, I write down for you the proposition just as it came to me, in the very words conceived at that moment:

The Earth is the circle that measures all; circumscribe a dodecahedron around it, and the circle encompassing this will be Mars. Circumscribe a tetrahedron around Mars, and the circle encompassing this will be Jupiter. Circumscribe a cube around Jupiter, and the circle encompassing this will be Saturn.

Now inscribe an icosahedron within the Earth; the circle inscribed in it will be Venus. Inscribe an octahedron within Venus; the circle inscribed in it will be Mercury. You have the reason for the number of the planets.

Such was the occasion and success of this labor; see now also my purpose in this book. How great a delight I received from this discovery I shall never express in words.

No longer did I regret the lost time, nor did I weary of the labor; I shrank from no tedium of calculation, spending days and nights in computing, until I should see whether the proposition conceived in words agreed with the orbs of Copernicus, or whether the winds would carry away my joys. And if I should find the matter to be as I supposed, I made a vow to God Most Good and Great that at the earliest opportunity I would proclaim among men in public print this admirable example of His wisdom, so that even though these things are not complete in every respect, and perhaps some points remain flowing from these principles whose discovery I could reserve to myself, yet others of strong intellect might bring forth as many things as possible alongside me at the earliest moment for the glorification of the divine Name, and sing praise with one voice to the most wise Creator. When therefore a few days later the matter succeeded, and I perceived how fittingly one body after another sat among its planets, and I reduced the whole business into the form of this present little work, and it won the approval of the celebrated mathematician Mästlin, you understand, friendly Reader, that I am bound by my vow, and cannot follow the custom of the satirist who bids us hold back books until the ninth year.

This is one reason for my haste; to remove from you every scruple of suspicion, I gladly add a second, citing that saying of Archytas from Cicero: If I had ascended to heaven itself and beheld the nature of the world and the beauty of the stars, that wonder would be joyless to me unless I had you, a fair, attentive, and eager Reader, to whom to tell it. Once you have learned these things, if you are fair, you will abstain from the reproaches that I foresee not without cause. Yet if, leaving these matters aside, you fear whether they are certain, and whether I have sung of triumph before victory, then approach these pages themselves at last, and learn the matter of which we have long been treating.

You will not find new and unknown planets interposed, as a little earlier—that boldness does not commend itself to me—but those old ones very slightly shifted, and so fortified by the interposition of rectilinear bodies, however absurd that may seem, that henceforth you can answer the rustic who asks on what hooks the heavens are suspended so they do not collapse. Farewell.
