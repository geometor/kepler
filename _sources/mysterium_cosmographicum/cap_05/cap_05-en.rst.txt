Chapter V: The Cube Between Saturn and Jupiter
==============================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0038
   :page: p. 28

Turning to the three primary solids individually, Kepler demonstrates why the **Cube** (*Hexahedron*, $\{4, 3\}$) holds first rank in its order and therefore occupies the outermost planetary interval—nearest to the sphere of the fixed stars—between **Saturn** and **Jupiter**.

Kepler establishes the Cube's primacy through a sequence of strict stereometric properties (Euclid Books XI–XIII, :cite:`Elements <euclid:elements>`):

1. **Direct generation from its base**: The Cube alone is generated simply by moving its own square base perpendicularly through space, whereas the remaining regular bodies must either be carved *out* of the Cube—as the **Tetrahedron** is obtained by slicing away four right-angled corner pyramids—or built *upon* the Cube—as the **Dodecahedron** is constructed by erecting six pentahedral roofs upon its six square faces (Euclid XIII.17).
2. **Sole regular space-filler**: Alone among the five Platonic solids, the Cube packs Euclidean three-dimensional space completely into homogeneous cells without leaving gaps or interstitial wedges, serving as the universal canonical measure of solid volume just as the square measures plane area.
3. **Orthogonal right-angle standard**: Every edge, face, and dihedral junction of the Cube meets at exact right angles ($90^\circ$), the singular unvarying mean between acute and obtuse angles.

Because the fixed stars represent the most noble boundary outside Earth—mirroring the circumference of a circle relative to its center—the Cube, prince of the primary solids, rightfully stands first between Saturn and Jupiter.
