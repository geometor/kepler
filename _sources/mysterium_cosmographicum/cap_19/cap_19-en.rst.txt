Chapter XIX: On the Remaining Discrepancy of Each Planet Individually
=====================================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0077
   :page: p. 65

*On the remaining discrepancy of each planet individually.*

These, then, were the considerations in general that can relieve my case. Now let us see in particular whether anything further can be excused. Let us begin with Saturn. To its distance (*apostema*), indeed, a large addition has been made, yet one which has caused a difference in *prosthaphaeresis* no greater than $41'$ (41 minutes). For just as its immense distance provides the readiest occasion for error in observation, so too an error in distance, however conspicuous, produces a slight diversity in *prosthaphaeresis*—smaller than one would suppose. And yet that astronomers have not measured even this star's motions with utmost certainty could be per-

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0078
   :page: p. 66

ceived from the past winter alone. For on November 2/12, in the year 1594, Saturn was seen exactly between the neck and the heart of Leo, where according to calculation it ought to have been on the preceding October 21/31—a difference in longitude of about $37'$ (37 minutes), more or less. Now if its discrepancy in *prosthaphaeresis* from :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>` does not exceed this quantity, once the distance has simply been corrected, astronomers should consider themselves abundantly satisfied.

In Jupiter nothing can rightly be found wanting; for its difference is slight, and less than a sixth of a degree ($10'$).

That in Mars, moreover, half a degree ($30'$) overflows is no wonder, nor does it trouble me; rather it moves me to wonder that the divergence is no greater. For in the preface to his Ephemeris for the year 1577, :person:`Mästlin <maestlin>` testifies that the errors of this star from calculation cannot be confined within the narrow bounds of two degrees.

Now as regards the inferior planets, Venus (♀) and Mercury (☿), although they seem to possess some advantage over the superior planets—inasmuch as it is easier to measure their orbs from greatest elongation than from acronychal observation—yet the very method of observing them is suspect to me. Though I leave this to astronomers to judge more accurately: namely, whether in these planets they are not sometimes deceived by the density of vapors and by physical parallax, which neither the Sun nor the Moon escapes. Certainly Mästlin, in his *Disputation on Eclipses* (Thesis 58), affirms concerning Venus that its distance from the Sun near the horizon has not rarely appeared notably smaller than the true distance. How much more may this be said of Mercury, which lies almost always beneath the Sun's rays, and even when it occasionally emerges, never presents itself to our sight except near the horizon through an interposed abundance of exhalations! And although the fixed stars lend aid to Venus, appearing both simultaneously and nearby, Mercury nevertheless remains more frequently at fault, being itself rarely seen, and fixed stars still more rarely near it. And since these things happen today, it is credible that they could also have happened to the ancients, however great the masters they were. For the fact that they do not warn the Reader about it only increases suspicion concerning the faulty dimensions of these planets; for this is a sign that whatever flaw arose from that cause was neither noticed nor corrected by them. Wherefore, in reading the ancients, I think one ought above all to examine whether the instruments and methods of the individual observations cited could have been subject to this error.

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0079
   :page: p. 67

Next, I fear not without reason that many things in the scheme of the hypotheses for these two planets have hitherto been left uncertain. Copernicus—as is gathered from the passage of :person:`Rheticus <rheticus>` just cited, and below from Mästlin's letter—followed the teachings of :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` more than the necessity of observations in emending the theories of the inferior planets. That he might be the less censured for this, Rheticus took care in his *Narratio*, where he admonishes that one must cling most religiously to the footsteps of the ancients, and change nothing lightly unless the extreme necessity of observations compels it. Because, therefore, such exquisite observations could not be had, that was perhaps reason enough for a most prudent master to attempt nothing further upon these planets beyond adapting them to his own principles.

Whenever, therefore, you see a great diversity of arcs in Venus, lay the blame for this—along with the general considerations I set out beforehand, which I would have you keep well in mind—upon these obstacles just alleged as well; and if you have weighed each point fairly, you will easily overcome the magnitude of the discrepancy with your equanimity. Herein it will be no small consolation to you that the Copernican number lies midway between the arcs resulting when the Moon is interposed and when it is omitted. For if you stuff the Great Orb with the Moon's system, the Icosahedron pushes Venus farther from the Earth than Copernicus handed down; whereas if, with the Moon removed, you make the Great Orb thinner, the figure admits Venus too close and allows it to be larger than it is in Copernicus. Wherefore something less than the Moon could help the matter, if Copernicus is to be retained.

Concerning Mercury, however, so much has already been said—and more can be said—that I trust you, fair Reader, even if something more were still wanting, will digest and excuse it. Nor does the diversity of its motion seem to me worth raising a great dispute over, even though it behaves better than Venus: for it makes a difference of only a single degree, which is remarkable, seeing how thoroughly deceitful its nature ever is! Assuredly this one star is what most prostitutes the reputation of astrologers and throws the whole doctrine of weather-signs into confusion. Indeed, in foretelling winds—which it most surely stirs up whenever it occupies suitable places—it often errs by so constant a number of days that I fall little short of being able then to correct its circle erroneously set forth in the Ephemeris. Therefore, if I saw any astronomer bending too anxiously over the task of pry-

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0080
   :page: p. 68

ing into the errors of this planet, I would advise him to invest that time more wisely: let him rather study the Earth and its attendant Moon—that most manifest (*enargestaton*) star—one of which we touch directly with our feet, the other almost with our eyes; let him polish away what we still get wrong in their motions and in eclipses; and only then let him transfer his labor to Mercury. Meanwhile, if errors concerning the motions of the Earth and Moon deserve pardon, far more will errors in Mercury deserve it, since it is both more remote from us and almost always lies hidden beneath the Sun.

And here again, as in the earlier chapter, I shall transcribe by way of a crowning flourish part of a letter which Mästlin sent to me—and for two reasons: first, because it warns you of a necessary matter; second, because it confirms this chapter throughout. Thus he writes:

   *So marvelous is Mercury that it very nearly deceived even me. Nor is that surprising, for I perceive it was exceedingly troublesome to Copernicus and Reinhold as well. Copernicus confesses this of himself: "With many windings and labors," he says (Book V, ch. 30), "has this star tormented us as we sought out its motions." Hence, besides citing no observations of his own made upon Mercury (☿), but borrowing them from Bernhard Walther of Nuremberg, he is not even consistent with himself in establishing the place of its apogee. For that apogee which (in ch. 26) in the first years of Antoninus, around the year of Christ 140 according to Ptolemy's observations, he finds at 10° Libra (♎), and under the starry sphere at 183° 20' from the first star of Aries (♈), that very same 183° 20' (in ch. 29) he assigns to the 21st year of Ptolemy Philadelphus—just as if this apogee of Mercury (☿) had rested motionless beneath the sphere of fixed stars for 400 years, whereas yet (at the end of ch. 30) it seems to him to have moved through one degree in 63 years (though he adds: "if indeed it was uniform"). That Reinhold was stuck in the very same difficulties is betrayed by the calculation of the Prutenic Tables, by which it is proved that Reinhold assumed the place of this apogee for that time of Philadelphus to be the same as Copernicus—namely, 183° 20' from the first star of Aries (♈); yet for Ptolemy's own time it falls in a place far removed from the manifest observations of Ptolemy and the recapitulations of Copernicus. For there its place is computed not as 183° 20' nor 10° Libra (♎), but 188° 50' under the starry orb and 15° 30' Libra (♎). And therefore those numbers of mine are accommodated indeed to Ptolemy's age, and do not—like the rest—agree in all respects with the calculation of the Prutenic Tables, but rather with Ptolemy's observations; for those Copernicus also retained and followed, and thence produced the same numbers. For our own or Copernicus's age, how-*

   .. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0081
      :page: p. 69

   *ever, I did not wish to compute these numbers, because they would turn out far different on account of the diminished eccentricity of the Great Orb, and because in Copernicus they have been investigated and confirmed by no more recent observations. I could wish, however—as you may remember my having said to you face to face—that Copernicus had taken not ancient, but new observations as the foundations of these dimensions. For that is a vast and monstrous postulate (Book V, ch. 30, fol. 169v, line 7 from the end), when he says: "We think it must be granted that the commensurations of the circles have remained the same from Ptolemy even until now." For the diminished terrestrial eccentricity itself demands other numbers. Nor is that true which Rheticus says in his Narratio, that in Mercury too, just as in Jupiter, no change of eccentricity is felt: for the side of the Sun's apogee does not close with its apogee in the same manner. To this is added that Ptolemy's observations are quite coarse and rounded to whole degrees (partiles), which ought by all means to have been corrected by more precise ones. Yet of these things it is now vain to complain. As for your own undertaking, however, if these numbers answer to you in any tolerable degree, count yourself to have discharged your office admirably, and congratulate yourself warmly—just as Copernicus did with Rheticus in his letter—relying on the surest hope that it will very soon come to pass that, on the occasion of these things which have been most ingeniously discovered by you, the rest also—which are still doubtful and not a little torment the company of astronomers—will become most plain.*
