Chapter IX: Planetary Qualities Derived from the Five Solids
============================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0043
   :page: p. 33

I cannot pass on without tasting here some few things from that part of Physics which treats of the qualities of the Planets: so that it may appear that their natural powers also observe this order, and preserve the same proportion toward one another. For if you reckon those planets which encompass the earth together with those bodies which they contain inscribed within themselves, and assign to the planets enclosed by the Earth's orb those bodies by which each is circumscribed—which I judge can be done with the best of reason—Saturn will have the Cube, Jupiter the Pyramid, Mars the Dodecahedron, Venus the Icosahedron, and Mercury the Octahedron. But the Earth, since it is nothing but the boundary, is reckoned with neither. Astrologers also distinguish the Sun and Moon by the greatest interval from the other five, so that there is no need to mention them here, and the number of bodies agrees beautifully with the 5 Planets.

Jupiter, then, beneficent in the midst of the malefics, has himself carried many away into wonder, and even spurred Ptolemy on to the investigation of causes. We see something similar in the pyramid, which, placed between two bodies partly kindred and partly repugnant to one another, differs so greatly from both that in the preceding reasonings it almost risked losing its place. Any one of the three superior planets wages hostile hatreds with the rest. With three of their bodies also there agrees nothing at all of those things which are apparent. Yet Mars conspires with Saturn in malice alone. To this I compare the inconstancy of angles, which is peculiar to them and common to both. Therefore the proof of goodness will be the contrary, namely the stability of angles in the sides alone—an argument why Jupiter, Venus, and Mercury are beneficent. The Cube, the body of Saturn, measures all the rest by its rightness; and the planet itself makes measurers, and in temperament is rigid, a guardian of what is right, yielding not a finger's breadth, inexorable, inflexible. Such is the nature of the rightness of its angle.

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0044
   :page: p. 34

The most evident kinship lies in the bases; and since Jupiter, Venus, and Mercury (I speak of the planet for the body) use the same base, we have the cause of their friendship, as above. For stability resides in the triangle first of all.

The second degree is a plane appearing with an angle as its navel. Let us no longer wonder, then, what charms lie hidden with harsh and fiery Mars, for the sake of which delicate Venus, having cheated her husband's marriage bed, conspired with Mars: for the pentagon of Mars is in Venus. So Saturn's quadrangle in Mercury reconciles the same manners to both. The third degree is when the same element of the same body exists or appears in two others; and then in those things they agree in the affairs of their common friend. Therefore in Jovian affairs Venus agrees with Mercury, because they use the common base of Jupiter. In Saturnian affairs Mercury agrees somewhat with Mars, because in the former is Saturn's square, and in the latter a hidden cube. From this it is also apparent why Venus has no kinship with Saturn—and what is strongest of all, why Mercury's versatile nature applies itself to all four, yet least of all to Mars.

Saturn is also solitary, and a lover of solitude, plainly just as the rightness of his angle cannot bear any inequality, even the least, by which it might become manifold. Conversely, Jupiter, having obtained one angle out of the class of infinite acute angles, has therefore been made sociable, yet moderately and temperately; for he is the author of more honorable friendships. So Mars and Venus are sociable too, yet excessively so; for their obtuse and prodigal angle marks intemperance. Mercury partakes of the nature of Saturn and Jupiter by reason of his angle. And scholars indeed love solitude, yet they are not inhuman; they love those who delight in the same studies, and observe measure in their intercourse—more so than Jupiter, whose every action takes place in assemblies of men and among those clad in purple.

Jupiter and Venus are fruitful: truly because Jupiter contributes to the composition of most things, while Venus is as it were the offspring of Jupiter, since one Venus contains within herself twenty diminutive Jupiters. Jupiter, moreover, is more inclined toward males, Venus toward females: whence the former is called male, the latter female. For the Pyramid is efficacious, the Icosahedron an effect and offspring. From these same principles a somewhat clearer cause is rendered why Mercury is of mixed sex, and why he is moderate in fruitfulness.

The tranquillity and constancy of character of Jupiter first, then of Saturn, and finally of Mercury arise from the paucity of their planes; the turbulence and fickleness of Venus and Mars, from the multitude of theirs. *"A woman is ever a fickle and changeable thing."* And the figure of Venus is of all the most varied and easily turned. And these are the degrees.

Whence middle Mercury—the middle string—reflects the versatile and swift nature of Mercury through the mobility of the Octahedron. For if you roll it upon two opposite angles, four continuous sides pass in a straight course across the middle of the figure. All the other figures, however you turn them, you will see have sides advancing transversely and impeded across their middle.

Mars with many sides produces fewer planes, Venus with as many sides produces more planes; and many endeavors of Mars are also in vain, while Venus, equal to him in endeavors, enjoys a more prosperous fortune. Nor ought that to seem strange. For dances are more easily arranged than wars, and it was fitting that loves should arrive sooner at their end than angers, since the latter destroy men, while the former beget them. In the same way Mercury is more fortunate than Saturn.
