Chapter XXI: Reconciling the Motive Soul with the Five Solids
=============================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0086
   :page: p. 74

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0087
   :page: p. 75 (Copernican vs. Motive Distances Table)

Anticipating a sharp objection from critics—*"Kepler, if your five solids truly established the ratios of the heavens, your new motive-period formula would confirm them rather than clash with them; truth never contradicts truth, so you have stabbed yourself with your own sword!"*—Kepler turns the critique around with characteristic candor and analytical clarity.

First, he appeals to the reader's own judgment: even with its numerical residuals, the matching of orbital periods to Copernican distances is so striking a piece of divine craftsmanship (*cheirourgema*) that anyone accepting its plausibility must *a fortiori* accept the far closer polyhedral agreement! Next, on folio ``0087`` (p. 75), he lays out the exact side-by-side table comparing adjacent planetary ratios (lower orb out of ``1000`` parts of upper orb) from **Copernicus** versus his 1596 **Motive Formula** (*Motoriae*):

* **Saturn / Jupiter (Cube)**: Copernicus ``572`` vs. Motive ``574`` (difference ``+2``).
* **Jupiter / Mars (Tetrahedron)**: Copernicus ``290`` vs. Motive ``274`` (difference ``-16``).
* **Mars / Earth (Dodecahedron)**: Copernicus ``658`` vs. Motive ``694`` (difference ``+26`` [*sic* in 1596 print, exact ``+36``]).
* **Earth / Venus (Icosahedron)**: Copernicus ``719`` vs. Motive ``762`` (difference ``+43``).
* **Venus / Mercury (Octahedron)**: Copernicus ``500`` (or ``559`` mean octahedral orb) vs. Motive ``563`` (difference ``+63``, or just ``+4`` against ``559``!).

Remarkably, the motive formula never disrupts the order or excludes a single regular solid—and its errors mirror the dual pairings of the solids themselves: tiny discrepancies (``+2``, ``+4``) at the two extreme rational-dual solids (Cube and Octahedron), larger positive excesses (``+26``, ``+43``) at the two tightly spaced golden-dual solids (Dodecahedron and Icosahedron), and a sole negative shift (``-16``) at the solitary Tetrahedron! Kepler explicitly closes by inviting future astronomers to find the exact law reconciling orbital periods and spatial distances—a challenge he himself finally solved in 1618.
