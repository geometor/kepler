Praeambulum: Preamble and Plan of Order
=======================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0195
   :page: p. 105

**JOHANNES KEPLER'S HARMONY OF THE WORLD, BOOK IV: ON THE HARMONIC CONFIGURATIONS OF SIDEREAL RAYS UPON THE EARTH, AND THEIR EFFECT IN STIRRING UP METEOROLOGICAL AND OTHER NATURAL PHENOMENA.**

:person:`Proclus Diadochus <proclus>`, *Book I of his Commentary on Book I of Euclid: On the Use of Mathematics in Natural Philosophy and Politics, which chiefly concern that Harmonic part thereof dealing with Radiations.*

   *To the contemplation of Nature it furnishes all the chief instruments, declaring the most beautiful order of Ratios according to which this entire Universe was fashioned; and the Analogy of proportions which binds all mundane things together, as Timaeus somewhere speaks, and which reconciles friendship among things at variance, and mutual responsiveness and affection among things separated by the greatest distances. And a little later:* From this source also it is possible by reasoning to track down fitting intersections of angles. *Again:* This, I suppose, Timaeus likewise wished to signify when throughout he conveys the contemplation of the Nature of the whole universe in mathematical terms, and portrays the origin of the elements by Numbers and Figures, attributing their faculties, affections, and even effects to these (figures), establishing the acuteness or obtuseness of angles, and the roughness or smoothness of sides, and the like, as the causes of manifold changes.

   *As for the doctrine called Politics, who could deny that it contributes many wonderful things, inasmuch as it measures the opportune seasons for conducting affairs, and the various revolutions of the entire universe, and the Harmonic Numbers—governors of life, or authors of incongruity, and on the whole helpers of either impulse or remission, and so forth.*

On Configurations: Preamble and Plan of Order
---------------------------------------------

In the first three books we shaped the Harmonic Proportions considered in the abstract: the first of which exhibited the Geometrical properties of individual figures; the second, the Congruences of figures joined together; and the third propagated harmonic proportions out of those figures.

It remained for us to introduce the Harmonic ratios thus far described into the World by three further books, of which the first would assign Harmonies to God the Maker of the heavens; the second, to Nature, the stewardess of manifold motions; and the third, to Man, who has mastery of the voice engendered from motion. Yet the condition of the matters to be set forth persuaded us not only to invert this order—making a beginning from human song, passing thence to the works of Nature, and thus finally to the work of Creation, which was first and most perfect of all—but also to join the end of abstract speculation with the beginning of concrete Harmonies in Song within that very same third book. A beginning of this introduction into the World having therefore been made in the preceding book, and Harmonies having been assigned to human song—which others are wont to embrace under the general name of Art—there now follows the fourth book, which in this inverted order assigns the second part of concrete Harmonics to Nature.

Now although I touched here and there in Book III upon the disputation concerning the Essence of harmonies, I nevertheless judged that a full treatment of this metaphysical inquiry ought, with the same design, to be reserved for the opening of this fourth book. For since the common sort of philosophers seek Harmonies nowhere else than in song, and it sounds like an incredible paradox to most when it is taught that sounds are one thing, and the Harmonies supposed to reside in sounds are another, some concession had to be made to the weakness of their grasp, and the origin of Harmonies had to be explained rather in Musical terms commonly known; nor was the eagerness of the reader to be disturbed by an unseasonable subtlety of metaphysical questions.

But now further, since my intention is to lay open Harmonies in Nature and in the motions of the Heavens, whereas the common sort of philosophers, at the very first mention of Harmony, immediately conceive a vocal Music of the stars to be perceived by hearing, and stand by with pricked ears like Cicero's Scipio, expecting to catch *so great and so sweet a sound*: since those unskilled in these matters are prone to argue with that same dreamer of Cicero's that *so mighty motions cannot be driven in silence*, and with the Pythagoreans out of Aristotle to render a reason why the celestial sound is not heard on Earth: since these preconceived opinions greatly hinder readers who are striving toward the inner sanctuaries of Nature, and might indeed deter many of strong judgment and followers of truth so that, scorning these Pythagorean trifles scarcely recognized from afar, they would cast the book aside unread—for these reasons this very place seemed to me the most fitting one to which to defer this supremely necessary disputation.

I was moved also by the example of :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`, who, after completing in the first two books of his *Harmonics* the Harmonic doctrine concerning song, and having now undertaken in his third book to demonstrate that all perfected natures participate in Harmonic power, himself begins his disputation from this very same question: *Under what genus of things are Nature or the Harmonic power, and its science, to be referred?* Although, therefore, I have relegated the examination and critique of Ptolemy to the Appendix of this my work, yet what ought to be answered to this question of Ptolemy from my own principles had, for the reason stated, to be set forth in advance in this fourth book. Nor does this serve merely to forearm the reader and avert perverse opinions, but also for the fundamental explication of the entire fourth book; so that, since we are about to speak of the Harmonies of sidereal rays—what and how many they are in number, and by what geometrical principles they are formed—we may first know what the essence of Harmonies is, apart from the consideration both of sounds (which have no place here) and even of the rays themselves; what is their proper subject and what their terms; whether they exist in things outside the intellect, or in the soul alone; by what medium they are perceived and received within; by what faculty they are judged; and what effect follows this perception and recognition, and by what author or prime mover. Once these matters have been unfolded both generally and by comparing particular subjects, it will afterward be easy for us to place the secrets of Metaphysics and Nature—concerning the essence and properties of Souls and of sublunary Nature itself—in a somewhat clearer light than they have stood in hitherto.

Chapters of Book IV
-------------------

* **Chapter I.** *On the Essence of Harmonic Proportions, both Sensible and Intellectual.*
* **Chapter II.** *How many and of what kind are the faculties of the Soul according to Harmonies.*
* **Chapter III.** *What are the Genera of things, whether sensible or immaterial, in which Harmonies are expressed, either by God or by Man, and in what manner?*
* **Chapter IV.** *What difference lies between the Harmonies considered in this fourth book and those considered in the third book.*
* **Chapter V.** *On the causes of efficacious Configurations, and their Number and Order of degrees.*
* **Chapter VI.** *What kinship Aspects have with Musical Consonances in Number, and its causes.*
* **Chapter VII.** *Epilogue, containing a contemplation of sublunary Nature and of the lower faculties of the Soul.*
