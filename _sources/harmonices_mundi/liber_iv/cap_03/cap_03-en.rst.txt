Chapter III: Genera of Harmonized Beings (Sensible & Immaterial)
================================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0213
   :page: p. 123

*What are the Genera of τῶν ἡρμοσμένων, that is, of things either sensible or immaterial, in which Harmonies are expressed, whether by God or by man, and in what manner?*


That these are distinct questions is easily manifest: 1. In what things Harmonies reside as in an Agent, Former, or Maker; 2. In what things they reside as in a formed work. Of the former we have treated hitherto; of the latter we must now treat further and more accurately.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0214
   :page: p. 124

All things, therefore, either are immaterial or partake of matter.
 Immaterial is the Soul (in respect, indeed, of the body), and this, as we have hitherto maintained, is wholly in respect of its essence inscribed by God into these Harmonic proportions; and the things which previously resided in the Soul as Maker—namely as it is Energy—now reside in the same as a work of God. But those things which partake of matter partake at once of Number and of Magnitude;
 and upon magnitude follows their situation in place; to which finally happens local Motion. Number therefore is something prior by nature to Harmony, because the terms of any harmony must needs be more in number than one. Yet the Number of the primary bodies of the World also has its own causes from Geometry, which below in Book V I repeat from my *Mysterium Cosmographicum*.
 Upon quantity follows figure, its inseparable property; why a spherical figure should belong to the stars and even to the World itself—namely from the Archetype of the Spherical—is wont to be explained elsewhere throughout. The figures of diverse bodies are immediately followed by a certain proportion, and that threefold: one of diameters, a second of surfaces, a third of space or bodies.
 Therefore, if no other causes of these Proportions were at hand, we could probably affirm that they were drawn from Harmonies. Yet whatever those causes are, the celestial Globes guard them without any change, inasmuch as they become neither greater nor lesser by any motion or lapse of time. It is otherwise with those Proportions which reside in bodies by reason of situation and by reason of local motions:
 for those retain the character of Motion itself; which, just as it abides in continual BECOMING (*fieri*) and never in BEING (*esse*), so also Proportions in motion are inconstant, and are different at different times. True indeed it is that if the motion of the celestial bodies did not vary their intervals from the common goal or Base of the world—that is, if there were no motion of theirs in height and depth,
 but all were in a mere concentric circle—then not only would the proportion of the intervals be constant, but it would even have been purely Harmonic, if no other causes had intervened. The same must be said of motion by reason of its essence, that is, by reason of its true swiftness through the ether: which if it were constant and perpetual for each,
 there is no doubt that these swiftnesses and slownesses of diverse bodies would have been tempered to Harmonic laws. Finally, the same must be said of the same motion also by reason of the apparent space traversed under the Zodiac. For if all the planets had had to proceed with the same apparent motion under the Zodiac, and never be separated farther one from another,
 there is no doubt that God would have established such a situation of them to one another under the Zodiac from the very beginning (if indeed their ordering had been free and not bound by other laws of necessity) as would divide the Zodiac everywhere in Harmonic ratios. So much so that even at the beginning of motions (since the beginning of time is considered without time) it is fitting
 that from a common situation and Harmonic configuration (such as could appear either from the Earth, or rather from the Sun), as from starting-gates, each ran forth into its own spaces or courses. But because the Planets move in height and depth, changing the intervals of their situation; and because upon this motion there follows by physical necessity also a real intensification and remission of motions, by reason of swiftness:
 finally, because through the unequal apparent swiftnesses of diverse bodies the space of the Zodiac, which they seem to traverse, is divided differently at different times: on these three accounts, the same thing befalls both situations and motions by reason of time as befalls quantities themselves without respect to time. For just as not in

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0215
   :page: p. 125

the whole circle or straight line, and in all its points,
 are there terms or bounds of Harmonic ratios, but only in certain ones: so here also not throughout the whole time of motion can Harmonies exist, neither in intervals, nor in swiftness of motions, nor in the spaces of the Zodiac intercepted between planets; yet they can altogether exist at certain moments of time, and they can do this without further work of God, once the beginning of motion has been given.
 For He who prescribed extremes to the motions, and granted change from one extreme to the other, the same also bestowed all the intermediate states, both the incongruous, which are infinite, and also the congruous and Harmonic, interspersed in a certain number among the incongruous. Nor indeed did God leave these mutable things without all care for Harmonic adornment; rather, some of them (such as the proper motions of the Planets)
 He reduced into Harmonic order by the very prescription of their extremes—which will be the matter of Book V. In others, where extremes are not given, but the quantity of motion is linked with itself in a circle, the Creator held it sufficient so to conform the souls set over mundane creatures that they might await, observe, and notice the harmonies coming forth throughout the whole circle at their moments of time,
 and accommodate their operations to their prescription. So it happens with the motions of the planets appearing from the Earth under the Zodiac, the proper matter of this Book IV.

Therefore, with the works of God known to us the matter stands thus; and if we now compare to these the things which Men order according to harmonic laws, partly the same things will have to be said by us, partly different.
 First, in Songs, although no less than in the heavens there is given a continuous augmentation and diminution of quantity, yet this in the motions of the heavens is necessary by fixed laws of nature, whereas in the human voice it is neither necessary nor even easy. For the throat was made with a distinction of rings for articulated voices; and more easily do we pass from high to low, and conversely,
 through intervals as it were of leaps, and discrete sounds, than if we were bidden to use a continuous tension. No wonder, therefore, that in celestial motions, together with that continuous augmentation and diminution which could not be avoided, there also remained inharmonious intervals mixed with harmonious and consonant ones; whereas in human song, all inharmonious intervals being eliminated, only harmonious and consonant ones are observed.
 Nor does Song therefore have anything wherein to boast above the celestial motions: for to those another office to perform has been entrusted, and harmonic tempering is merely accessory to them; song has nothing besides Harmonies to look to, requires nothing outside, and is intent upon the one single end of delight.

There are also other human works into which the mind introduces harmonic proportions, though more obscurely and cheaply:
 as when Song is informed not only by the quality of highness and lowness, but also moreover by the measure of beat, partaking of the duple and triple proportion; and again in the motion of bodies, when one dances in Dances, first in the proportion of equality, afterward in the duple proportion. Which same thing the poets also imitate by the framing of feet out of syllables, long and short,
 of which the former is posited to be double the latter: where the Iambus (˘-), Trochee (-˘), or Tribrach (˘˘˘) is to the Spondee (--), Dactyl (-˘˘), Anapest (˘˘-), Amphibrach (˘-˘), or Proceleusmatic (˘˘˘˘) as 3 to 4; to the Bacchii (˘--, --˘), Cretic (-˘-), and Paeons (-˘˘˘, ˘-˘˘, ˘˘-˘, ˘˘˘-) as 3 to 5; to the Molossus (---), Choriamb (-˘˘-),

   :page: p. 126

the Ionics, and their compounds as 1 to 2; to the same, the Spondee, Dactyl, and those which have the same number of times with them as 2 to 3; and these same to the Paeons as 4 to 5; while the Paeons to the Choriamb and its fellows as 5 to 6.


And so much are Poets and Grammarians delighted even with the names of proportions that by a peculiar usage they call four-syllable feet with one short syllable *Epitrites*, that is, *Sesquitertians*: because, just as in the proportion 4 to 3 expressed by parallel lines, the first three units are represented by a double line, with only the fourth exceeding by a single one, so in the foot three syllables are of two times,
 and the fourth of one only. And since the very word *Feet* alludes to the Choruses, a part of Comedies and Tragedies, it seems that actors also expressed all those proportions by the motion of their feet, no less than today the duple and triple are expressed. In Architecture, whatever proportions of length to breadth or thickness are most approved, even by spectators who are not Mathematicians,
 those are found to be as close as possible to harmonic ones. But that the proportions of sounds are more exact than all these, and that the Nature of man delights more earnestly in expressing them, happens from this: that every living creature has sounds most especially in its power, inasmuch as they are formed within itself, sent forth from the very breast, most ready at every nod of the mind
 and every motion of the heart; and, as was said before, because he has also obtained the fittest instrument, the Throat stretched out lengthwise like a string or rather a pipe, in whose rectilinear length the voice wanders up and down most swiftly.

Generally, however, in all things in which quantity, and Harmonies according to it, can be sought, those are present far more evidently through motion than without motion.
 For although there is present in any straight line its half, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, and their multiples, yet they lie hidden among other parts incommensurable to the whole, concealed in one and the same confusion with the whole, so that even if a second line equal to some one of the aliquot parts stands beside the whole—for example, a lintel-beam of three feet beside a doorpost of five—
 yet its proportion to the whole would not become known as easily as if some motion should discern, constitute, and determine comparable lengths. This is the cause: that where quantity is without motion according to it, there all things which are in the quantity are together at the same time—namely, all proportions of all parts to the whole; whereas if some quantity is traversed by a certain motion,
 then (as is the essence of motion) the proportions which have been traversed are no longer, and those which have not yet been traversed are not yet; and any single proportion is alone when the motion arrives at its traces. Thus by the succession of motions it comes to pass that harmonic proportions are shelled out from the inharmonious, and separated from their mixture, as it were set forth pure in the light,
 and held out to the senses for grasping. Indeed, not even the mind itself in a given quantity discerns harmonic proportions without some image of motion from the infinite inharmonious ones standing before and after: but having passed through (for example in a circle) infinite chords, it is busy with the chord of a third or fourth part of the circle and the like, and seeks out its demonstration;
 and it accomplishes by thought what the hand accomplishes by drawing a line, so that by this act it separates it from the infinite lines unthought

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0217
   :page: p. 127

and undrawn, in order that the mind may behold its congruence or incongruence apart from the rest.

The mind indeed can do these things, because it uses the will, and leaps at its own discretion in that infinite division of quantities,
 which is wholly present at once to the mind for thinking; whereas sensations, and other natural perceptions, and finally the motions of bodies by which perceptions are aided, are not thus in the power of the living creature so that, if infinite sounds, or the infinite angulations of two planets around the whole circuit of the circle, were confused together at the same time,
 they could withdraw themselves from the rejected ones and betake themselves into the pleasing ones alone: they have need, therefore, of motion, by whose intervention all things which would be confused by reason of quantity may be unfolded through successive times, so that each one alone may befall the senses.
 But if the eyes can do something similar to the mind, so that out of an infinite confusion of things present together without motion they choose the more excellent (as if, using the service of the hands, out of infinite possible chords of a circle they should choose and draw the chord for a third part, namely the side of a triangle):
 this itself must be judged to be done not so much by the eyes as by the mind itself through the eyes; nor is this done altogether without motion, as I said, of the hands.

In order, therefore, that we may apply this observation to our former pronouncements: it is necessary, if any thing is to be a genuine subject of harmonic proportion—it is necessary, I say,
 both that quantity—namely length—be in that thing, and that there be two marks at least, if the length is a circle, or three, if it is a straight line, of which either one or all traverse the length of the thing by some motion, and become the termini of parts of the length, between which the proportion may be: and this at the least.
 And this is done in part in configurations, the proper subject of this Book IV. For as will be said in the following chapter, Harmonies indeed in angles, which the Zodiac measures, are considered without motion: yet the angles themselves, one after another, are constituted by the motion of the radiating bodies through the Zodiac.
 Yet there are more excellent ways tending to the same end: as when bodies which have length are themselves both bounded by such marks, wandering up and down, and the segments intercepted at any turn are moved together: so that thus not now the bodies, but the very motions of the bodies, by reason of a length and shortness not temporal but corporeal (namely of embodied motions,
 or of bodies set in motion), are compared with one another. Just as the matter stands with sounds: for sound is a species sent forth from a body, both as that body is quantified, and as it is figured in a certain measure, and as it is set in motion: because according to the fitness of the figure is both the motion and the sound.


And this is now the second and more evident cause why the nature of man is devoted above all to the harmonic proportions of sounds. For this comes to pass again on account of the conformation of his body: in which the throat is that body which, according to the constriction of an upper or lower cartilaginous ring in the windpipe, is now long, now short,
 and which, upon the breath driven in and pressed out from the bellows of the lungs, puts forth by its cavity (namely its figure) a like motion, of which both the species befalls the ears and the sensation (of the windpipe itself, as it is set in motion) is present to the sentient soul: for to sense by the common sense is this—to enjoy the species of the members of the body,
 as they are variously affected and as it were formed by motions; which species, as I explained in my *Dioptrice*, are borne by the continuity of the spirits from the members

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0218
   :page: p. 128

of the body, even remote ones, to the seat of the common sense.
 Thus it comes about that because man, by frequent sensation of his own windpipe set in motion, drinks in a certain idea of the conformation of bodies sounding in any manner whatever, he all the more easily recognizes and as it were judges the conformations of bodies outside himself when moved and emitting sounds by that motion, and examines them compared with one another according to the laws of harmonic proportions.
