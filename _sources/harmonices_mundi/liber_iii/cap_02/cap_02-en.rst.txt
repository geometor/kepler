Chapter II: The Seven Harmonic Sections of a Vibrating String
=============================================================

**Chapter II.**

*On the Harmonic Section of a String.*

Thus far we have taught the twofold origin of harmonic proportions: one directly from constructible and congruent figures, the other through the double proportion on which the identity of consonances rests. Yet since harmonic proportions are infinite, and to our understanding still rough, unpolished, obscure, and unnamed, scattered like raw stones and timber,
we must proceed to polish them, give them names, and build from them the fairest edifice of the harmonic system, or musical scale—whose constitution is not arbitrary, as one might imagine, nor a mutable human invention, but a most noble and natural reason, so that God the Creator expressed it in tempering celestial motions.

Harmonic proportions are joined into a single system by harmonic sections of a string; how many these are in number must be investigated in this chapter.

**DEFINITION.**

When an entire string is cut into parts such that each is consonant both with the other and with the whole, we call this a *Harmonic Section*.

The mean of this section—musically (that is, consonantly) proportioned—is either of two equal parts, or if unequal, the greater of them; the extremes of the consonant proportion are the remaining or lesser part, and the whole string.

*Let the geometer observe the analogy of the divine proportion, or section by extremes and mean:*
*in which the whole has the same ratio to the greater part as the greater has to the lesser. What is the same ratio in that geometric section is, in our musical section, the same quality—called consonance, concord, congruence, or harmony. Yet beware of assuming consonance of the same species, just as there the ratio is unique.*

*The ancients made no mention of this section in this sense, because they did not know the true causes of consonances; of their section of a string we shall treat below.*

**PROPOSITION IX.**

The section of a string into two equal parts is harmonic.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0113
   :page: p. 23

*For since equal parts emit the same sound under any single tension, by Axiom II, while the whole is double each part, it therefore sounds identically consonant with either of them, by Prop. I. Thus there are three consonances; hence, by definition, the string is divided harmonically.*

**PROPOSITION X.**

The section of a string into two parts in double ratio is harmonic.

*For parts in this ratio sound identically consonant, by Prop. I. And since the greater part is double the lesser, the whole is triple the lesser. Thus it stands to the lesser as the circle to the consonant part cut off by the triangle's side, by the last Corollary of the preceding chapter;*
*wherefore the whole is also consonant with the lesser part, by Ax. V. It therefore sounds consonant also with its double—namely the remainder—by Prop. IV. Thus three consonances are established by this section; therefore it is harmonic.*

**PROPOSITION XI.**

The section of a string into two parts in triple ratio to one another is harmonic.

*For since parts 1 and 3 stand to one another as a consonant part of the circle to the whole, they will be consonant between themselves, by Ax. V. And since 1 and 3 make 4, part 1 will be consonant with the whole 4, by Axiom I and Prop. III.*

*Finally, since the remainder 3 is consonant with part 1, it will also be consonant with its quadruple 4, namely the whole string; hence here too there are three consonances.*

**PROPOSITION XII.**

The section of a string into two parts in quadruple ratio to one another is harmonic.

*For since the parts are in quadruple ratio, they sound identically consonant with one another, by Prop. III. And since 1 and 4 make 5, part 1 is consonant with the whole 5, by Axiom I and the said Corollary. Wherefore the whole 5 also sounds consonant with 4, the quadruple of part 1, by Prop. IV.*
*Thus three consonances arise. Therefore, etc.*

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0114
   :page: p. 24

**PROPOSITION XIII.**

The section of a string into two parts in quintuple ratio to one another is harmonic.

*For since the part is 1 and the remainder 5, they have the ratio that the whole circle has to a consonant part, by Ax. I and the said Corollary. Wherefore they are also consonant with each other, by Ax. V. And since part 1 with remainder 5 makes the whole 6,*
*therefore (by Ax. I and the Corollary) part 1 is consonant with the whole 6.*

*And since remainder 5 stands to the fourth part of the whole circle 6 (namely 1 1/2 of this division) as the whole circle 10 to the consonant part 3, by the Corollary, remainder 5 will also sound consonant with the whole 6, by Prop. VII.*
*Or, what comes to the same thing, since remainder 5 stands to 12, double the whole circle 6, as a consonant part to the whole, by the Corollary, remainder 5 will sound consonant with 12, by Ax. V—and thus also with the simple whole 6, by Prop. IV. So three consonances arise. Therefore, etc.*

**PROPOSITION XIV.**

The section of a string into two parts in sesquialter ratio (3:2) to one another is harmonic.

*For since part 2 with remainder 3 forms the sesquialter ratio, the part stands to the remainder as some consonant remainder 2 stands to the circle 3, by the Corollary; hence this part 2 will sound consonant with its remainder 3, by Ax. V.*
*And since part 2 with remainder 3 makes the whole 5, while part 1 and its remainder 4 are consonant with their whole 5, by the Corollary, the whole 5 will also sound consonant with 2, the double of the consonant part 1 (which here is our part), or with 2, the half of the consonant remainder 4, by Prop. IV.*
*The same also follows simply from the first axiomatic part of Proposition V, since the chord subtending two fifths is constructible, and therefore consonant. Finally, since the remainder 3 of part 2 stands to the fourth part of the whole 5 as the whole circle 12 to the consonant part 5, by the Corollary,*
*our remainder 3 will also sound consonant with the whole 5, by Prop. VII. Thus three consonances exist. Therefore it is harmonic.*

**PROPOSITION XV.**

The section of a string into two parts in superbipartient-third ratio, or 5 to 3, to one another is harmonic.

*For since the ratio of part 3 to remainder 5 is the same as that of a consonant remainder 3 to the whole 5, by the Corollary, therefore by Axiom V our part 3 will also sound consonant with our remainder 5. And since part 3 with remainder 5 makes the whole 8, by the Corollary part 3 will sound consonant with the whole 8.*

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0115
   :page: p. 25

*Finally, since remainder 5 stands to half of the whole 8 (which is 4) as the whole circle 5 to the consonant remainder 4, or to a fourth part of the whole 8 (which is 2) as the whole circle 5 to the consonant part 2, by the Corollary, our remainder will also sound consonant with the whole 8, by Prop. VII. Here too three consonances arise; therefore it is harmonic.*

**PROPOSITION XVI.**

If a string be cut into two expressible parts, and among them and the whole—that is, among the three terms—there is one dissonance, there must also be a second dissonance among them.

*For the cause of the dissonance will be that either the whole or a part has a number of shares of that division proper to an unconstructible figure.*
*Yet such a number joins consonantly neither with any greater number proper to a constructible figure, nor with any lesser than itself, by Axioms III and V, and Props. V and VII. Therefore that term which consists of such a number of shares is dissonant from the remaining two terms of that section; and thus two dissonances occur together.*

Similar to this proposition in geometry is this: if a straight line be cut into expressible parts, and one of them be incommensurable with some third line (not with the whole composed of both, as here), the other must also be incommensurable with that same third line.

Or, if a straight line be cut into parts incommensurable with each other, both will be incommensurable with the whole.

**PROPOSITION XVII.**

If a string be cut into two parts expressible in length, and among them and the whole—that is, among the three terms—there are two consonances, the third must also be a consonance.

*For if there are two consonances, since there are no more than three ratios, there cannot be two dissonances;*
*if not two dissonances, then not even one, by XVI inverted; therefore all three ratios will be consonant.*

Thus in geometry, if a straight line be cut into parts commensurable with each other, the whole will be commensurable with each of the parts.

**PROPOSITION XVIII.**

A section of a string into two parts expressible in length,
in which either the whole or one of the parts acquires a number of shares proper to an unconstructible figure (provided the numbers of the whole and of the parts are prime to one another), is not harmonic.

*This is proved like XVI. For of the three ratios of three terms, at least two become dissonant, contrary to the foregoing Definition.*

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0116
   :page: p. 26

Here are three examples: in the first, the greater part is seven eighths; in the last, the lesser is one ninth; in the middle, the whole takes seven parts—all dissonant.

*Places marked thus cannot be expressed by the usual notes of music.*

**PROPOSITION XIX.**

Beyond the octagonal division, no harmonic section of a string is given.

*For the subsequent sections are made either by unconstructible figures and their stars—and then, though the parts may be consonant with each other, yet they are dissonant from the whole, by Ax. III—or by figures constructible with an improper demonstration, as by the fifteen-sided figure, and the proper parts of the section are dissonant from the whole, by the Corollary to Ax. III;*
*or by figures constructible with a proper demonstration, all of which after the pentagon are even-sided (see Book I). Therefore the proper parts of such sections must be formed by an odd number of shares of the section; for if taken in an even number, the part would belong not to this section but to an earlier one:*

*for instance, when a string is divided into 10, if you take 4 or 6 shares, it is the same as if, dividing the string into 5, you took 2 or 3 shares. Since then the part is an odd number and the whole an even number, the part can indeed be consonant with the whole if it be no greater than five (by Prop. V),*
*yet one consonance does not suffice for a harmonic section, as is plain from the definition. Then, however, the remainder will be dissonant; for the whole is posited as more than 8 shares, and the definition of a remainder is that it be greater than half, namely greater than 4.*

*Therefore the least remainder in the eightfold division is 5; in more numerous divisions it is greater than 5. Thus in all sections of a string beyond the eightfold, the remainders are odd numbers greater than 5. Yet odd numbers greater than 5 belong properly to unconstructible figures, by Props. XLV and XLVII of Book I. Therefore, by Prop. XVIII of this book, these remainders cause non-harmonic sections.*

**Corollaries.**

I. The harmonic sections of a single string are seven in number, and no more.

II. The propagation of numbers that are the marks of the sections takes place in this way: at the start the whole is set in the form of a fraction, namely unity above as numerator and unity below as denominator;
then each number separately is placed above, and the sum of both on either side below, so that from any single fraction two branches spring forth, until from the sum there arises a number marking an unconstructible figure.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0117
   :page: p. 27

These seven sections of a string I first discovered with hearing as my guide—just as many, indeed, as there are harmonies no greater than a single octave; afterward, not without toil, I unearthed the causes both of the individual sections and of their total number from the deepest springs of geometry.
Let the curious reader read what I wrote about these sections twenty-two years ago in the *Mysterium Cosmographicum*, Chapter XII, and weigh how I was mistaken in that place concerning the causes of the sections and harmonies, wrongly striving to derive their number and ratios from the number of the five regular solid bodies;
whereas the truth is rather this: both the five solid figures and the musical harmonies and string sections share a common origin in the regular plane figures. And Ptolemy's *Harmonics* with Porphyry's commentary, which I mentioned in that cited passage, I later obtained in manuscript through the generosity of Johann Georg Herwart, Chancellor of Bavaria;
of whose third book I have translated the greater part into an Appendix to Books IV and V of this work. Yet I did not find the genuine causes of harmonies in them; and indeed there is no mention at all even of these sections or of their sevenfold number.

Although I noticed early enough that the causes must be sought from plane figures—the seeds of which you already see scattered in the cited Chapter XII of the *Mysterium*—nevertheless they tormented me for a very long time before all the scruples of my mind were satisfied. First, the constructible figures had to be separated from the non-constructible;
next, the cause had to be found why, though these sections came from figures, the sections stopped within the sevenfold number while the figures ran on to infinity. Thirdly, the distinction between the fifteen-sided figure and the remaining constructible ones had to be established, since I saw that figure excluded from producing harmonies, as hearing attests.
And individual chapters had their own concise safeguards, any one of which kept me occupied for a long time. Let Prop. V serve as an example, which last of all, when I was already copying out the work, I saw must be added—something I had hitherto overlooked. For unless that too stood among the principles,
and if, for instance, seven twentieths were fit to establish a harmony merely because they are constructible through three twentieths (with which they make a semicircle), then seven tenths and five sevenths, and therefore two sevenths and one seventh, would be proved to make harmonies—which both our ears and our axioms reject in every way.

Therefore, even by the sole testimony of my cited book *Mysterium Cosmographicum*, hearing is sufficiently armed against the slanders of sophists

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0118
   :page: p. 28

who would dare detract from the trustworthiness of the ears regarding such minute divisions and the subtlest discernment of concords; for the reader sees that at that time I followed the testimony of the ears
in establishing the number of sections, while I was still laboring over their causes; nor did I do here what the ancients did, who advanced a little way by the judgment of the ears, then soon spurned their guides, followed an erroneous reason for the rest of the journey, and ordered their ears, dragged as it were by force, to grow wholly deaf.
For greater assurance, however, below in Chapter VIII of this book I have taken care that anyone may have ready means to consult his own hearing on these and other divisions of a string, and weigh their testimony—so that he may be certain that what we investigate rests on the surest experience of the senses, not forged by my whim (a fault of which the Pythagoreans stand partly guilty) nor thrust forward as truth.
