Chapter XIV: On the Modes of Melodies, Which They Call Tones
============================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0156
   :page: p. 66

**Chapter XIV.**

*On the Modes of Melodies, which they call Tones.*

Being about to speak of the Modes, I first remind modern musicians that of those things which they are wont to call Modes—namely, the duple, triple, and similar proportions of the beat (such as they perhaps once made with flutes in Roman comedies)—I shall have nothing to say beyond what has already been said.
I call Modes those which they themselves with the ancients are wont to call Tones, when it is asked of what Tone a song is. For they are certain qualities or species of concinnous song, divided against one another by the two highest differences of the Genera, the Hard and the Soft.

The Greeks called them *tropoi* [Τρόπους] and *ethe* [ἤθη]: the former from the form of the System, the latter from the effect of song in man, because most legislators judged that the Modes of songs serve to shape morals.

Concerning the number and distinctions of these Tones, therefore, there are great controversies among musicians not only today, but also of old—some of which may be seen in Ptolemy, and most in Vincenzo Galilei. Yet it is not my purpose to examine or refute anyone's opinions;
therefore let the musician expect nothing else from me in this question than that one thing to which my principles hitherto explained lead me. For although Tones seem to be named from this, that one Mode begins either higher or lower than another by the interval of one Tone (or Semitone),
yet I do not distinguish Tones by the height or depth of the song itself in itself, which alone Vincenzo Galilei testifies some have observed; rather, with Ptolemy I demand that their number match the number of species of a single octave,
and I say there are just as many Tones as there can be lawful and concinnous skeletons of the Octave System, differing among themselves in Genera, in the order of concinnous intervals, and in the position of the tetrachords, or the choice of the two harmonic means.
There are, in fact, three things that vary the Tones and distinguish them from one another: Genus, sequence of concinnous intervals, and articulation of the skeleton from the lesser consonances.

First, the two Genera established in Chapter VI for the division of the Octave System constitute for us two principal Tones,
first by Nature, having arisen with the System of a single Octave itself. Next, in each Genus of the System, both hard and soft, from the composition of two octaves into one greater and perfect System, which we handed down in Chapter XI, there are given Systems of individual Octaves,
differing by the roots or boundaries by which they are enclosed; and those boundaries are different places of the natural System,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0157
   :page: p. 67

or, in today's term, various keys, which a different pitch height necessarily follows with respect to the natural System that begins from *G*;
and through it also a different position of the semitone, which is in either the third, the second-to-last, or the lowest place of the Octave. For as to height itself, considered absolutely, that of itself changes nothing in the species of a song, since the same species can be intoned either high or low.
Nor are we bound to this, that with Ptolemy we take half of the remaining Tones deeper than the principal and natural System *G*, and half higher. For we can take either all higher or all lower, since the same order of concinnous intervals follows either way—namely, if the System of the double octave, naturally composed,
begins from key *G*. But if with Ptolemy we make part lower and part higher, the natural System must be composed so that we add a fifth below and a fourth above; and thus the composite System of the double Octave will begin from *C*.

Third, finally, in Systems thus constituted,
there is given the choice of the two harmonic means toward which the parts of song *tone* [τονὴ] and *petteia* [πεττεία] aim; or, what comes to the same thing, there is given the position of the tetrachord either in the highest or lowest place of the Octave, or in the middle between two thirds.

For the sake of this diversity, therefore, there must be subjoined here before all else the square Net [*Rete*] compiled from Chapter XI, consisting of twelve orders of the twelve minimal natural intervals—not indeed so that with Aristoxenus we should make as many Tones according to their number, beginning each one higher by one such minimal interval;
but so that, once these minimal intervals are contracted into the usual concinnous ones, it may appear whether any new species of the Octave can begin from each and every minimal interval.

You see that among the twelve differences of height of the Octave, there is none that agrees with another in the order of its elements.

Let us now see how many species of Octave Systems arise from this if the first difference of Octaves, which is that of Genus, be mingled in;
that is, whether the 12 minimal intervals of all 12 orders can be concinnously contracted into the seven usual intervals of a single Octave, distinguished by eight places, and in how many ways each.

Here note that *LS* or *SL* stands for the Major Tone, *DS* or *SD* for the Minor Tone, *S* for the semitone;
and our descriptions will proceed from low to high, or from below upward.

It is certain, therefore, that in Octave *G* two species exist according to the twofold position of the semitone in the second or third place from the bottom.

*First, of the Soft Genus:* ``LS. S. DS. LS. S. SD. LS.`` or ``SD. S. LS.``

*Second, of the Hard Genus:* ``LS. SD. S. LS. SD. SL. S.`` or ``S. LS.``

But in *Ge*, as also in *Ce*, since two semitones do not compose one concinnous interval, nor are they sung melodically [ἐμμελῶς] in succession, the lowest semitone therefore stands alone, by Laws 9 and 2 of the preceding Chapter.
The distribution would therefore be:

Either such: ``S. SD. SL. S. SD. SL. SL.``—inconcinnous by Law 3.

Or in *Ge* indeed such: ``S. SD. SL. SS. DS. &c.``—and this is inconcinnous by Law 9. No concinnous species of Octave, therefore, begins from *Ge*.

In Octave *A*, according to the threefold possible position of the lower semitone, the elements will also be contracted into Tones in three ways: for either thus: ``S. DS. LS. S. DS. &c.``, which coincides with what has already been rejected; or thus, so that hence are born:

III. *Third*: ``S. DS. LS. SD. S. LS. LS.``


IV. *Fourth*: ``SD. S. LS. SD. S. LS. LS.``

V. *Fifth*: ``SD. SL. S. SD. SL. SL. S.``

V. In Octave *b*, there can be only one contraction of elements, which coincides with the fifth form, nor is any other given, namely ``DS. LS. S. DS. LS. LS. S.``, because the semitone can have no place other than the third from below.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0159
   :page: p. 69

In Octave *h* [B natural], because it is not lawful to fuse two semitones in the second place by Law 9, nor to arrange them in succession by Law 2, they must therefore be distributed into tones so that the solitary one stands either in the first or in the second place.

And if in the first place, it will follow that the other solitary one stands either in the fourth place, which is rejected by the third law, or in the sixth, and four tones in succession will follow in the lower part of the octave against the fourth law.
Therefore the semitone must be placed in the second place, so that hence arises species

VI. *Sixth*: ``SL. S. SD. SL. SL. S. SD.``

VI. In Octave *C*, for the twofold possible position of the semitone, two distributions are given: the first coincides with the 6th: ``LS. S. DS. LS. LS. S. DS.``; the other is *Seventh* in number:

VII. ``LS. SD. S. LS. LS. SD. S.``

Of one form of octave *ce* we spoke a little earlier; the other coincides with the following.

VIII. ``S. SD. SL. SL. S. SD. SL.``

In Octave *d*, for the threefold position of the semitone in the lowest Tetrachord, three species are given: *Eighth*: ``S. DS. LS. LS. S. DS. LS.``

IX. *Ninth*: ``SD. S. LS. LS. SD. S. LS.``

X. *Tenth*: ``SD. SL. S. LS. SD. SL. S.``

In Octave *de* no concinnous distribution can be made: for the semitone would be banished from the first three places, which is against the eighth law of the preceding Chapter.

In Octave *e*, again a threefold possible position of the semitone in the three lowest places gives three species, of which

XI. *Eleventh*: ``S. LS. LS. SD. S. LS. SD.``

XII. *Twelfth*: ``SL. S. LS. SD. SL. S. SD.``

XIII. *Thirteenth*: ``SL. SL. S. SD. SL. S. SD.``—it is the same as the first form of the following.

In Octave *f*, the semitone has a single place in the third seat, wherefore one species arises, namely the same biform thirteenth: ``LS. LS. S. DS. LS. S. DS.``
or ``SD. S.``

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0160
   :page: p. 70

Finally, in Octave *fe* there are two places for the semitone, either in the second-to-last seat or in the last. If therefore you arrange thus: ``SL. S. SD. SL. S. SD. SL.``, the species will be the first, as appears from comparison;
otherwise it will be the fourteenth: ``S. LS. SD. SL. S. SD. SL.``

Hence appears the reason why in Order II, IV, V, VII, IX—namely in Octaves *Ge, b, h, ce, de*—no distinct species begins.

Second, it appears that no genuine transposition can be made so that the same species of Octave remains, except these: I. from *fe* to *g*; II. from *ge* to *a*, yet of a rejected form; III. from *a* to *b*; IV. from *h* to *c*; V. from *ce* to *d*;
VI. from *d* to *de*, of a rejected form; VII. from *e* to *f*.

Yet if we defer to the common view of organists, who neglect the Comma and do not distinguish between Major and Minor Tones, then all fourteen of our species of Octaves will constitute no more than three Modes,
according to the threefold position of the semitone (as indeed Galilei says there was a time when no more than three Tones were held: Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian); yet they are multiplied into more by pitch height alone.

For as to the third cause, the articulation of the skeletons of the Octave, that is still common to us with them; on account of which also more than XIV species are born for us.

*Behold the three common orders of Octaves.*

I. That the semitone be in the lowest place, which I think was called Phrygian by the ancients; to them these from our species are equivalent:

* III. In Octave *A* | ``S. DS. LS. SD. S. LS. LS.`` (Equivalence as to the lowest Tetrachord is true.)
* VIII. In Octave *ce* | ``S. SD. SL. SL. S. SD. SL.``

* In Octave *d* | ``S. DS. LS. LS. S. DS. LS.``
* XI. In Octave *e* | ``S. LS. LS. SD. S. LS. SD.`` (Moderate equivalence.)
* XIV. In Octave *fe* | ``S. LS. SD. SL. S. SD. SL.``

II. That the semitone be in the second-to-last place, which seems to have been done in the Dorian, as called by the ancients; these from our species are commonly equivalent:

* I. In Octave *G* | ``LS. S. DS. LS. SD. S. LS.`` or ``S. DS. LS.``
* In Octave *fe* | ``SL. S. SD. SL. S. SD. SL.``

* VI. In Octave *c* | ``LS. S. DS. LS. LS. S. DS.``
* In Octave *h* | ``SL. S. SD. SL. SL. S. SD.`` or ``S. LS. SD.`` (Equivalence as to the lowest system of a fifth is true.)

* XII. In Octave *e* | ``SL. S. LS. SD. SL. S. SD.`` or ``S. LS. SD.`` (Here equivalence is least.)
* IV. In Octave *A* | ``SD. S. LS. SD. SL. S. LS.`` or ``S. LS. LS.``
* IX. In Octave *d* | ``SD. S. LS. LS. SD. S. LS.`` or ``S. DS. LS.`` (Moderate equivalence with the preceding.)

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0161
   :page: p. 71

III. That the semitone be in the third place, as I believe it was in the Lydian; these from our species are commonly equivalent:

* II. In Octave *G* | ``LS. SD. S. LS. SD. SL. S.`` or ``S. LS.``

* VII. In Octave *c* | ``LS. SD. S. LS. LS. SD. S.`` or ``S. DS.`` (Equivalence in the lowest fifth is true.)
* V. In Octave *A* | ``SD. SL. S. SD. SL. SL. S.`` or ``S. LS.``
* X. In Octave *d* | ``SD. SL. S. LS. SD. SL. S.`` or ``S. LS.`` (Lesser equivalence.)

* XIII. In Octave *e* | ``SL. SL. S. SD. SL. S. SD.``
* In Octave *f* | ``LS. LS. S. DS. LS. S. DS.`` or ``SD. S.`` (Least equivalence.)

According to the common opinion concerning the Major and Minor Tone, therefore, very many transpositions of melodies can be made from letter to letter by means of the signs ♭ and ♯.

It remains that we examine which of our XIV species of Octave can become manifold through the articulation of concinnous elements. For a fivefold distinction of species arises from the contiguity of Tones: either three tones follow one another in succession, or only two.
If three, they must stand unnaturally either in the middle of the octave, having single tones and single semitones on either side, or incline toward one extreme of the System, and then necessarily there is a semitone at that extreme, on account of Laws 8 and 3.
But if only two Tones follow one another, with semitones intervening, then there is no place for a semitone at the extremes; and the two pairs of tones stand either above or below, with the soft third in the opposite extreme, and that upright below, having its semitone above; inverted above, having its semitone below.

*[Musical examples of the five articulations: (1) Minor Third, Three Tones, Minor Third; (2) Semitone, Three Tones, Semitone, Major Third; (3) Semitone, Three Tones, Semitone, Major Third; (4) Two Tones, Semitone, Two Tones, Minor Third; (5) Minor Third, Two Tones, Semitone, Major Third.]*

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0162
   :page: p. 72

In this manner, interspersed among our XIV species there are found X others, coinciding in their first four or even five places with the preceding; where they begin to differ, the particle *or* [*vel*] has been prefixed.

And if now the final cause distinguishing the Tones be added, namely *tone* [τονὴ] and *petteia* [πεττεία], the number will be tripled, and there will become 72 species. For every Octave skeleton out of all 24 has both the third and the fourth concording with the first, and the fifth and the sixth.
If therefore these parts of the song are chiefly occupied around the fourth and sixth, then they place the fourth [*Diatessaron*] in the lowest place; if around the third and sixth, the fourth is in the middle; if finally around the third and fifth, the fourth is in the highest place.

I do not say these things because so minute a subdivision is necessary, since I know that three forms are generally mixed in one song; but so that from this number judgment may be passed on the distinctions of the Tones which the Moderns hand down,
which are in part such that it seems to me as many of them could be established as there are songs in all, the rest being of one Tone. Yet if we observe these principles, the number of Tones, however great, is nevertheless finite.
In sum, Tones distinguished by real boundaries and differences—not with respect to the pitch height of a voice on an Organ, but even in the human voice itself, which can make the beginning of the natural System from any voice high or low—are either 3 only, or 14, or 24, or 72.

With these principles of mine I thus reconcile the eight common Tones which they call Ecclesiastical, and which were among the ancients, in the opinion of some:

*I. Dorian* (*Adam the first man*) | *I. Hypodorian* (*Noah the second*)

These are two species of Soft song, from the species of octave which is First among my XIV.
Nor do I see any other difference than in mere pitch height, which the first has greater and the second less—unless you wish to distinguish them by the final cause, because the first observes this skeleton and the second that.

*Third common Phrygian* (*Third Abraham*) | *Fourth common Hypophrygian* (*Four Evangelists*)

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0163
   :page: p. 73

Nothing is more manifest than that these two of the Hard Genus belong to our species XI and are to be written from *e*; and again there is scarcely any other difference than of height, unless you wish to adapt these skeletons separately to them.

*Fifth common Lydian* (*Five books of Moses*) | *Sixth common Hypolydian* (*Six waterpots set*)

*[Marginal note on "height":] Threefold is the height of a Tone: 1. Absolute and material in a certain way, as in the human voice and also on Organs if from G the root of the System ascends to g, where the form of the System is not changed;
of this nothing now. 2. Formal, of the root itself of each Tone in the natural System composed of the octave; nor is this understood in this place, for it implies a change of Tone when the radical key of the System is altered.*

*3. Again formal, the height of a Melody with respect to its radical key,
with which remaining, the Tone indeed remains the same, yet puts on as it were another figure: as if it does not ascend to the Octave of the root, but keeps itself below the sixth, fifth, or fourth, or frequently lets itself down below the root, returning nevertheless at the end to the root.*


*Therefore these skeletons are not so to be understood as though a Melody passes through all its joints or notes depicted here: it suffices if it touches some, or their lower octaves. Those therefore that thus let themselves down below the root we could with the ancients call Plagal; the rest, Authentic.*

And these likewise are most manifestly to be written from *f*, and are of the soft genus, and are distinguished by height or by these skeletons, from our XIII.

*Seventh common Mixolydian* (*Seven liberal arts*) | *Eighth common Hypomixolydian* (*And eight are the parts.*)

Although the intention of the inventors appears, in that they wished to write the First and Second from *d*, the Third and Fourth from *e*, the Fifth and Sixth from *f*, the Seventh and Eighth from *G*,
yet it is certain from what we have argued above that these also of the Hard genus, together with the First and Second, ought to be written from the same key *G*, to which from our species II these skeletons belong.

By this application, therefore, of the eight common Tones to my species of Octave I, XI, XIII, II,
I can show a much clearer distinction among these Tones than our musicians do, because they despise the comma. For those which are written from *G* have all four legitimate consonances, of the third, fourth, fifth, and sixth with the first.
Those, however, which are from *F* and *e* use adulterated consonances, below by the major fourth *e–a* and *f–bb*, and by the major fifth *e–hh* and *f–cc*.

What then of the remaining ten species, you will ask, since all customary Tones are reduced to four?
Plainly they serve for this, that musicians may know that if we temper the Systems of the principal Octave described from *G* as nature counsels, then a song can by no means be transposed so easily and variously without changing its purity, as they hold in practice.
They are free, therefore, either to reject all the remaining 10, and with them also their transpositions, or to confess that more Forms of Melodies—that is, more Modes or Tones—can exist, and those differing from these established ones,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0164
   :page: p. 74

some more, others less, just as you see the equivalences noted in the margin above.
For if we take no account of the upper intervals of a single Octave, certainly some transpositions are given from *G* to *c* and from *A* to *d* and others.

You may ask finally, what prevents those transpositions, so often rejected by me already?
For the ears do not seem to prevent them, since it is agreed that the excess of a Comma in intervals that are disjoined in places of the System is not judged by the ears. For let the system be tuned as well as possible according to the laws of nature explained thus far, let *G–a* and *a–h* be struck,
and let all men who listen be asked which of these intervals is greater: they will say they do not know; nor will they distinguish before the intervals have been joined in the same place of the Octave—
that is, when, with the span of the string marked off by dividers, the eighty-first part of the string is cut off, and thus the two lengths 80 and 81, struck at successive moments, have been compared between themselves?

I answer that although hearing does not distinguish when only three strings of the Octave (the endpoints of two intervals) are struck,
yet when all the strings of a single Octave are struck, it thus at length distinguishes the first-born and natural Octave *G* from the Octaves of other keys. For in all species of Octave there are the same seven concinnous intervals, which once expressed by the striking of the strings immediately linger close by in memory,
so that it easily lies open to hearing in what place of the natural Octave any Tone begins. In this way the sense of the Comma will be implicit in the distinction of Octaves.
For just as Hearing tests Consonances themselves and all concinnous intervals by effect, though it counts not the string lengths that cause the concords, so the same hearing notices the effect of the comma 80:81, though it counts it not, and thus grasps it not separately by discrete sensation.
