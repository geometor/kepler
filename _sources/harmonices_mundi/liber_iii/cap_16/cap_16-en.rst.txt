Chapter XVI: On Political Proportions and the Three Means
=========================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0170

**CHAPTER XVI.**

*What Song through Harmony, or Figured Song, is.*

Although the word *Harmony* is used by the ancients for Song, we are not to understand by this name a modulation through several voices sounding harmonically together.
For that a harmony of many voices in a perpetual alternation of harmonies is a recent invention, and altogether unknown to the ancients, needs not much proof. On this matter see Vincenzo Galilei in his Italian work on Music.
Indeed, the banishment of song through Harmony from Plato's Republic is often raised as an objection, as if it had already been in use then; yet that passage refers to organs, the syrinx, the bagpipe, and the lyre,
when one voice either drones continuously or pauses, giving way to an approaching dissonant note. In that practice there was no greater artifice among the ancients than among our own bagpipers.

In these last centuries, this way of singing began to be called *Figured* because its first authors did not make diagrams as simple as those in Choral chant, but used various figures, colors, and dots;
some of these signs command silence, some sound, certain ones a long sound, others a short one; some are applied to distinguish Tones, some to distinguish mensural Modes, and others for Fugues, Repetitions, and the like.

Let harmonic polyphony therefore be defined by us thus: that there be two, three, four, or more voices—or Concinna and fit Melodies such as we described in Chapter XIII—all of the same Genus, and of the same or kindred Modes, proceeding together in such a way
that they make concords either pure or seasoned by a very brief punctuation of Concinna dissonances—not, however, identical in perpetual tenor, nor the same in immediate succession, but varied for delight by the very alternation of successions.
For just as, in proportion, the Consonance of two voices stands to the Unison, so does Harmonic Song stand to the simple Melody of a single

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0171
   :page: p. 81

voice; from which principle depend all the members of the definition, which I shall explain in turn.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0171, p. 81)

   Discantus. Altus. Tenor. Bassus.

I. First, then, regarding the plurality of voices, it occurs to note what the Craftsmen have established as most agreeable to Nature: that although many voices often sing together, yet all are called by four names only,
according to the number of a perfect harmonic mediation in one complete octave system. For they name the highest voice *Discantus*, the *Altus* that next to it, the *Tenor* the higher of the low voices, and the *Bassus* the lowest.
For between two voices sounding an Octave, there are only two means that sound harmonically together with the identisonant extremes, as explained in Chapter III.

Yet the Italian origin of those terms seems to look rather to a compound System,
so that in it the *Altus* and *Bassus* hold opposite parts—not that the *Altus* is the highest, but because these two frequently (though not at the end) make an octave, the former higher and sharper, the latter lower and deeper.
Thus to the *Tenor* the origin of the word gives the Idea and simple modulation of the song; to the *Discantus* its name seems to have been given by its perpetual volubility and wandering away from the region opposite to the *Tenor* by an octave.
Therefore if we compare the parts of Song adduced from Euclid in Chapter XIII, although all belong to all the Voices, yet the *Tenor* is occupied chiefly in *Agoge* (linear progression), the *Discantus* in *Ploke* (interweaving), the *Altus* in *Tone* (holding),
and the *Bassus* in leaping through harmonic intervals; while *Petteia* (choice of pitches) is common to all four, yet more notable in the *Altus*.

These properties of the four voices are drawn from the very Nature of things.
For since, by the definition handed down, the polyphony must continually change from one harmony into another, it was necessary that this be done so that one of the intermediate voices, in comparison with the rest, should remain within a narrow space of the System.
For if equal latitude of freedom were given to all, it must follow that the lower voice would frequently stray into the space of the upper, and vice versa; and an insane confusion would arise, and the distinction of the four Voices would be rendered void.
And it was rather one of the middle voices (*Altus* or *Tenor*) that had to occupy these narrow confines than the extremes, *Discantus* or *Bassus*.

For if any such thing had been done in the extremes, all variation of intervals would have turned toward one single direction,
namely either upward into high pitch or downward into low. Better therefore it was that the polyphony be fixed or tethered in a narrow place upon some intermediate voice, as one boundary of its parts, while the variation of intervals tends from it partly upward, partly downward.
Yet the lower of the middle voices ought not to be straitened, because the Idea of the song, assigned to the *Tenor*, the lower of the middle voices, ought both to be free and to move through the middle magnitude of the whole System,
so that the surrounding voices may be nothing else than its colors and ornaments or inlays. Now lower intervals are always greater than upper ones of the same name.
So that the *Tenor* might hold the middle place in magnitude, it had to be the lower of the middle voices; and thus the *Altus*

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0172
   :page: p. 82

had to be reduced into narrow compass, not the *Tenor*.
There remain, then, excursions for the *Discantus* and *Bassus*, yet with this distinction: that the *Bassus*, which sounds low and is expressed by large motion and long strings, wanders in slow measures and large, and therefore harmonic, intervals;
while the *Discantus*, which sounds high and flies above, and is elicited by short strings and a swift motion of narrow space, ranges everywhere in short and therefore more frequent measures, and in the smallest concinna intervals.
This, then, is the property of the voices. Now I shall explain the remaining particulars of the definition.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0172, p. 82)

   Dissonances.

II. For as regards that primary and chief seasoning of Melodic Song, Dissonance:
first, these ought to be taken not from any unmelodic interval whatsoever, but from concinna intervals. For since individual voices, concurring in a common polyphony, admit no intervals except concinna between pitches succeeding one another,
and are indeed of the same genus and Tone, as was premised in the definition: it is easily evident that, if any single harmony is established among such voices (since every harmony is resolved into concinna elements), by no wandering of the individual voices from the root of their Octave
can it ever happen that two voices between themselves make anything other than a concinna interval, even though it may not always be consonant.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0172, p. 82)

   See Giovanni Maria Artusi of Bologna, Book II on the art of composition, which is wholly about dissonances.

Secondly, although outstanding craftsmen sometimes employ greater dissonances, so that the dissonant voice stands a whole tone away from that which would make a consonance, yet this is not done except for expressing and eliciting the gravest motions of the soul.
Ordinary dissonance, however—joined with pleasantness and therefore natural in a certain measure—is produced by a semitone. The cause of this thing again, so that the last things may answer to the first, must be sought from the innermost foundations of Geometry,
from Chapter I, Axioms II and III, and from Book I and the contemplation of the Fifteen-angled figure.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0172, p. 82)

   The force of the Pentadecagon in the choice of dissonances.

For since the semitone is contained in the numbers 15 : 16,
and not only the Sixteen-angled figure has a demonstration, but also the Fifteen-angled figure: little indeed was wanting for 15 : 16 to define a harmonic proportion for us, and no less those derived from it,
15:8, 15:4, 15:2, 15:32, and 15:64, and above all 15:1—those, namely, which composers most frequently admit for forming legitimate dissonances; nor had we any other exception than this, that the demonstration of the Pentadecagon was not shaped immediately from the number of angles of the figure, like the rest,
and therefore not its own, but borrowed from different figures, the Triangle and the Pentagon, and thus derivative and of the most remote degree; likewise, that the angle of the Pentadecagon indeed fits with others to fill a plane place,
yet the whole figure does not admit congruence with all its angles. Since therefore 15:16 and its companions approach so closely to Consonances in the manner of their shaping cause, what wonder is it that they are also frequently mingled in practice among consonances:
whereas, on the contrary, the Tones 8:9 and 9:10 partake with their other terms in the Nine-angled figure, a figure utterly indemonstrable. Wherefore not sweet, but altogether harsh, are the dissonances admitted through them and their companion ratios (4:9, 2:9, 1:9, 9:16, 9:32,
so 9:5, 18:5, 36:5, 9:20, 9:40, etc.), and much harsher those that are compounded out of the Tritone, the mutilated Diapente (diminished fifth), and similar intervals: concerning which see

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0173
   :page: p. 83

Artusi: for they arise after the Major and Minor Tones and the Semitone,
namely from an unnatural composition of these as Elements: and always, by how much one departs from Nature, by so much one departs from sweetness.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0173, p. 83)

   Syncopation

Thirdly, another name for dissonances hints at the remaining laws of them,
when they are called *Syncopes*: although this is a common accident both of dissonances and of imperfect consonances, yet we are now treating of dissonances. For ordinarily they hold this rule, to use it like leaven or salt or vinegar in dishes:
namely, just as whole courses are not made out of those, so here whole songs do not abound with dissonances, except for the sake of great emphasis; nor does any single voice, beginning right on the downbeat of the measure,
strike against the rest that are consonant while itself dissonant; rather, stealthily and as it were slipping sideways, it admits dissonance while, in one place of the system which it occupied on the upbeat,
and thus beginning a certain pitch in the latter and less principal part of the measure while the remaining voices agree with it, after they have finished it lingers on longer and beyond the beginning of the following measure,
so that all the other voices, mostly lower than it (so that it, namely, by its smallness, weakness, and swiftness—being high—may clash the less), by common consent at the beginning of the following measure occupy a place dissonant from its place,
before it has departed from that place of its own, and thence drive it out as though sluggish, and thrust it down for the most part into a lower place:
from which occasion also the name of *Cadences* seems to have been born for the concords and closes that consume dissonances.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0173, p. 83)

   Cadences.

Moreover, they observe this rule, that the clash of Dissonances is ordinarily joined not so frequently at a close interval and between seconds, as between sevenths; so that the upper voice, driven in this manner from that place of its own,
may glide down through a Semitone or Tone to a sixth, or fly up to an Octave to be marked with leading voices.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0173, p. 83)

   Common dissonances.

There are also other very frequent dissonances, less artful, when a low voice continues its own pitch, remaining in unison, while a high voice runs out from the octave of that low pitch in swift notes toward some pitch consonant with it by a fifth or a fourth,
flying through all the intermediate concinna places so that it sounds consonant and dissonant by turns; yet always the first in order is consonant, and the dissonance is brief, since it is in passing, and in the pitch that is later in order:
as when a Measure of double proportion joins five notes of the Discantus with one of the Bassus, the first, third, and fifth of the Discantus will be consonant, while the second and fourth will be dissonant, or less perfectly consonant. Behold.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0173, p. 83)

   Alternation of consonances.

*[Musical example: c. im. c. dis. con. c. im. c. dis. con.]*

III. Of this rarer mixture, therefore, the natural reason is the same as that of the other most frequent and perpetual mixture, when Consonances are varied.
For just as in a simple Melody, the leading of one single voice from the root of the system through dissonant yet Concinna intervals tends toward places harmonically tempered to the Root, and lingers in them;
so melodic polyphony, beginning almost from unison, through lesser Concords—commonly called imperfect—or even through these dissonances, tends toward

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0174
   :page: p. 84

greater and perfect consonances, and for the most part (especially at the end) toward Identisonance.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0174, p. 84)

   Imperfect consonances.

IV. Again, just as in simple songs of those tongues that rest upon Rhythm and the length or shortness of syllables, little account is taken of short syllables,
while we direct long syllables and the ends of verses to places consonant with the first pitch: so also in polyphony, there are consonances called minor and imperfect, over which we skim rapidly; and there are perfect ones, toward which we press,
which for that reason are called *Clausulae* (Cadences).

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0174, p. 84)

   Cadences (*Clausulae*). — Perfect consonances not to be placed consecutively.

V. Further, just as the individual Consonances considered singly are pleasing on this account,
that they do not sound wholly identical, but are figured in a certain manner and diverse-sounding, occupying a certain qualitative breadth in hearing and drawing their nature from regular plane figures, from which also they arise:
so now Harmonic Polyphony, or a perpetual succession of several Harmonies, without variety of them loses all its grace.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0174, p. 84)

   Why not two fifths etc. consecutively.

From this explanation of the definition, causes may be given of the laws which Composers observe in fitting concords together.
For it may be asked, What is the natural cause why they deem it lawful to arrange several natural Thirds and Sixths one after another, yet do not admit several Fourths, Fifths, Octaves, and the like, of single species, in succession?
I answer, the cause is this: that Thirds or Sixths following one another in pitch are for the most part genuinely different, one major, the other minor; and although sometimes two minor Thirds follow immediately one after the other, yet they are the least and imperfect consonances.
For let the first sing *Ut Re Mi Fa*, and the second at the same instants of Time *Mi Fa Sol La*: four Thirds follow one another in turn, of which the first, *Ut Mi*, is major; the second, *Re Fa*, and third, *Mi Sol*, are minor;
the fourth, *Fa La*, is again Major. On the contrary, all Fourths are commonly held to be intervals of the same magnitude, as also Fifths, and most of all Octaves. Therefore, if several of these perfect concords of the same species were arranged consecutively,
differing only in pitch, the Song would not be varied in consonances.

Yet another principal cause prevents several Fifths from being sung in order upward, which does not prevent Octaves from being sung: concerning which Aristotle thought fit to ask in his *Problems*,
Why do they sing in Octaves (for example, Men with Women, Men with Boys), yet do not sing in Fifths and Fourths? The cause, then, is this: that whereas two Thirds make the consonance of a Fifth, and two Octaves the consonance of a Double Octave,
whereas, on the contrary, two Fifths make a Ninth, and two Fourths make a Seventh, which are dissonant intervals. Therefore a voice running there equally through Octaves and Thirds remains in its Tone or mode, observing the same species of Octave; here, if it runs through equal intervals, it markedly changes mode or Tone,
which is contrary to the definition.
VI. Concerning the choice of concords, certain things must also be noted: first, that in general great intervals express great motions of the soul (as in those works of Orlando, *Ubi est Abel* and *Tristis est Anima mea*); small and high ones, joyful motions; low and small ones, sad.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0175
   :page: p. 85

Next, since harmonic song generally ends in Identisonance, to span a large interval at the end as the text requires, not only must the Bass descend, but the Discantus also ascend, contrary to the nature of ending a song, imitating pipes and the strings of the pandora.
And then especially shines forth the power of the semitone, explained in the previous chapter: that the natural and ordinary ending of a song by ascent loves to pass over a semitone at the end, at least in those Tones that do not have a semitone immediately above the root of their Octave.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0175, p. 85)

   Why large intervals are more pleasant.

Thirdly, Galileo judged it worthy of inquiry why a Fifth above an octave (*Diapente epi diapason*) sounds more pleasantly than a simple Fifth, and so forth, why one Harmony is always more pleasant than another.
The causes he himself adduced are not worth examining, since they are obscure and uncertain. From my principles, however, the causes of this matter appear most clear. For since, by Axiom II of Chapter I, the degrees of knowledge by which the sides of figures differ are also transplanted into the proportions themselves,
the offspring of figures, it is fitting that the degrees separating the demonstration of the Part of the circle and of the Remainder also distinguish the sweetness of both Part and Remainder. Therefore, Harmonies arising immediately through the cutting of the circle itself, namely between the Whole and the cut-off Part,
are more perfect and more pleasant than those between the Remainder and the Whole, which are derived only from the former through the admixture of Identisonances from the bisection or doubling of Strings.
Thus 1:3 holds first place, and is therefore more pleasant than 2:3; and 1:6 than 5:6; and 1:5 than 4:5; and 1:4 than 3:4; and 2:5 than 3:5; and 3:8 than 5:8. For first the Part becomes consonant through itself, not because of the Remainder;
afterward, and thus more imperfectly, the Remainder is demonstrated to be consonant, namely on account of the accompanying or alien Part.

The remaining Rules of the Art of Composition I leave to the Craftsmen themselves to prove, or to render an account of them.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0175, p. 85)

   Scope of this entire work.

For me, what I have argued thus far abundantly suffices, both for illustrating the nature of Song, at least with its first and general foundations in Nature shown, and especially for the speculations of the following Book V, whose subject matter is set before me as the sole end in the whole work.
For being an Astronomer, just as I argue about regular figures not so much geometrically (except where geometry seemed still incomplete) as astronomically and metaphysically, so also I write of the proportions of song not so much musically as geometrically, physically, and finally, as before, astronomically and metaphysically;
because just as I have need of the five regular solids from Geometry, so too I have need of Proportions and the entire Harmonic apparatus from Music to explain the causes of the proportions of the celestial Orbs, of the Eccentricities, and of the motions in the Apsides.
As for the art of composing song, which is Musical practice, I by no means profess it; whoever wishes may seek it more rightly from the published books on composing Song by Artusi, named above, and my late friend Seth Calvisius—whom I name not as best, but because I have seen no others.

**On the Three Means. A Political Digression.**

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0176, p. 86)

   See a most beautiful passage in Bodin, *De Republica*.

*On folio 29, line 9, the following paragraph was omitted from its proper place because the compositors carelessly distributed the sheets of the copy.* (namely 7, 10.)
Nor yet on that account are authors to be blamed who admit proportions of this kind into the Commonwealth under the title of Harmonic.
For even if few of those thus formed define pure Harmonies, and all the rest are alien to Harmonies, yet all of this definition have this property, that they are tempered out of both proportions, the Arithmetic and the Geometric; from which tempering lies their fitness in the Commonwealth.
Conversely, in the Commonwealth, if there is any force of Harmonic proportions as Harmonic, neither can my Harmonic means be despised, which by a looser definition I hand down thus:

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0176, p. 86)

   What a Harmonic Mean truly is.

*That it is everything which comes between two consonant voices, itself consonant with each of them.* In the divisions, therefore, etc.

*Thus far what was omitted.*
*Since, however, I had mentioned Bodin in the margin, and now having finished Book Three and added the paragraph above, I judged enough labor had been given to Mathematical speculations; this thorny matter ended, I resolved to transcribe from Bodin himself the principal heads of this political disputation,*
*with the words and order shaped for the most part according to my own grasp of that passage, so that I might bring some light to a place obscure in itself—for Bodin did not bring sufficient aid from the Mathematical sciences to this speculation—*
*and to lighten the tedium of severe Mathematical demonstrations, of which the whole book consists, by interposing a popular, pleasant subject, and offer a taste of its considerable use in knowing the Commonwealth. I shall begin, however, with a fuller mathematical explanation, better suited to the non-Mathematical reader.*

Since, therefore, between 3, 6, 9, 12, and likewise between 38, 41, 44, 47, there is a continuous arithmetic progression, it comes about that of three such numbers placed in succession, the middle one is called the Arithmetic mean: as between 6 and 12 the Mean is 9,
and between 38 and 44 the Arithmetic Mean is 41.

For just as to 3 is added its triple, 9, so to 9 is added its triple, 27, which is as many times greater than 9 as 9 is greater than 3, and as 15 is greater than 5, etc. Therefore as 3 is to 12, so 9 is to 36;
and as 3 is to 9, so 12 is to 36.
Again, this proportion in this example is disjunct. Therefore a conjunct Geometric proportion, or progression, is when, starting from some number, either a like part or a like multiple is always added to it.

To the initial number is added its triple in the first two examples, and its half in the third; and again to the number born therefrom is added the same multiple or part. Therefore as 8 is to 12, so this very 12 is further to 18;
so also 18 is to 27; where 12 is the Geometric mean between 8 and 18. Thus 18 is the Geometric mean or proportional between 12 and 27, etc.

These things must be known first, so that it may be understood what Harmonic proportion is.
For it is thus defined by Bodin, that in it *the Ratios of equals and of likes are moderately blended*, that is, the former arithmetic ratios and the latter Geometric.

Further, this definition is not true.
1. For there are many ways even in non-harmonic numbers in which ratios of equals and of likes can be moderately blended; yet this does not suffice for there to be a harmonic proportion, for other dispositions of numbers must also be added, to be sought from Chapters I, II, and III.
2. Conversely, there are many harmonic couplings of numbers in which neither ratios of equals nor of likes are contained. 3. Indeed there are even harmonic proportions in which there is a simple Geometric proportion, as 1, 2, 4, although they are Identic and alone do not produce special grace:
so much so that Bodin denies they make any harmony of themselves—understand, figured harmony; conversely, there are other harmonic proportions in which there is a simple Arithmetic proportion, as 1, 2, 3, and 2, 3, 4, which Bodin himself, forgetting himself, acknowledges as harmonic, though they have nothing of Geometric analogy mixed in.
So too 3, 4, 5; 4, 5, 6; 1, 3, 5; 2, 5, 8, etc., which latter Bodin wrongly denies make harmonic concords, rising up on the authority of the ancients against the sense of hearing.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0178
   :page: p. 88

Yet the assertion is somewhat true of that proportion which the ancients, by a false persuasion, called Harmonic. I say, somewhat: if by Ratios of equals you understand Arithmetic Means transformed into other numbers, wherein is no expressed actual equality of Excesses as in the first three.
For the Harmonic Mean of the Ancients is most properly defined thus: that the proportion of the excesses among three numbers is the same as that of the extremes, as in 3, 4, 6. Whatever such three numbers are, whether truly harmonic or not, it is always true in them
that *ratios of equals* (or rather arithmetic) *and of likes* (or Geometric) *are blended* in one certain way. The manner is this: given the extremes, as 2 and 5, an arithmetic mean is given; lest it contain a fraction, take the doubles of the extremes, 4 and 10; their arithmetic mean is 7.
Now in such an arithmetic mediation 4, 7, 10 of equal excesses 3, 3, two proportions are always formed: one greater, between the lesser terms 4 and 7; the other lesser, between the greater terms 7 and 10.
Whoever now wishes to make a Harmonic mean from this Arithmetic mean, and mingle something of Geometric mediation with it, let him set the lesser proportion in the first place and the greater in the second place, thus \*, and seek a number that stands to 10 as 4 stands to 7;
the three numbers will be thus: 7, 10, 17½; or in whole numbers prime to one another, 14, 20, 35.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0178, p. 88)

   \* 7. 10. / 4. 7.

To this triad is mixed something of Arithmetic progression, yet without its expressed equality remaining. For both proportions of the Arithmetic mediation among 4, 7, 10 have been brought together here, only with their position changed: for as there the former terms, namely 4 to 7, so here the latter, 20 to 35;
and as there the latter terms, 7 to 10, so here the former, 14 to 20. In turn, something of Geometric mediation is also mixed into it: for the lesser proportion, 7 to 10, is placed at the lesser terms 14 and 20, and the greater, 4 to 7, at the greater terms, 20 and 35;
and finally it comes about that as the least term 14 is to the greatest 35, so the lesser difference 6 is to the greater 15. All these things indeed savor of a certain likeness familiar to Geometric proportion, because in it as the least term is to the middle, so is the middle to the greatest.
These things must be held fast from Mathematics, which Bodin does not seem to have sufficiently perceived when he puts forward Harmonic proportion in five numbers—3, 4, 6, 8, 12—and affirms of them *that equal and like intervals exist tempered in a certain manner.*
For seeing the excesses were 1, 2, 2, 4, he recognized expressed equality in 2, 2, and likeness in 1, 2, 4. Yet in the ancients' definition three suffice; nor does it contain any tempering of expressed equality with likeness. For in 3, 4, 6 there is no expressed equality of excesses.

Now it is worthwhile to see how Bodin, who is most copious in this subject, applies these things variously to Moral and Political affairs; to whom it generally happens that instead of Harmonic means—both those of the ancients and true ones—he seizes upon mere combinations of Arithmetic and Geometric proportions,
relying on his faulty definition: which, as it had to be stated at the outset, must often be repeated in what follows.

I. Since there are three forms of Commonwealth—Popular, Aristocratic, and Royal—

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0179
   :page: p. 89

he compares the popular to Arithmetic proportion, the aristocratic to Geometric, the royal to Harmonic.
For as in Arithmetic the increments of all numbers, great and small, are equal, so in the Commonwealth the people want burdens, benefits, honors, and magistracies equal for all, tolerating no respect of persons; for example, all must have the right of hunting, whether noble or baseborn, rich or poor.
And if there is any matter that does not admit division among many, the people wish to cast lots for it; because the lot is blind, not distinguishing noble from baseborn, rich from poor, well-deserving from undeserving, virtuous from vicious, clever from dull.
Only then does a man think himself equal to the rest, if he draws lots with them over such things, whether his lot be good or ill. In the lot's place may also stand other means of acquiring, as when choice is left to someone disinterested, or believed to regard no persons.
Thus at Rome the Consulship was open to all of a certain order, and could be sought from the people—not by bribes, lest the people be corrupted and the rich preferred to the poorer; though here, by tacit consent, virtue is regarded, and judgment on it and merit is left to the people.
On the contrary, as in Geometry Excesses of numbers match the numbers themselves—a large number having a large excess, a small one a small—so in an Aristocratic Commonwealth persons are distinguished, as are burdens, rewards, Magistracies, and offices; the highest are reserved for the Nobles, the rest left to the people.
Here it is necessary that arithmetic proportion also be admitted separately within each faction: for over those things belonging to the people, all who are among the people shall cast lots; over those belonging to the Nobles, all the Nobles.
For unless this is done, there will be perpetual degrees of Nobles even among the people, down to the lowest dregs of the populace, and perpetual degrees among the Nobles up to a Prince of the Commonwealth; by which reckoning it will not be a Republic, but a Royal kind of state.

As for the Royal state, although it is most closely likened to Geometric proportion—inasmuch as all rights of Majesty are reserved to the King, just as he surpasses all others by Noble lineage, or arms, or virtues—nevertheless the manner of governing in this state can most rightly be tempered from both kinds of Proportion.
For one King, arbiter of all, not by blind impulse like the lot, but by considerations of virtue, merit, rank, and degree, so far as lawful, dispenses all things between Nobles and people, executing all parts of distributive and commutative justice; which conjunction of both proportions suffices for Bodin to establish Harmonic proportion;
but alongside this he refers all counsels not so much to individual Orders or men as to the whole body of the State, and its safety, mutual charity, and concord—not otherwise than if in numbers, proportions somewhat deflected from equality and likeness
—so that they may even be broken if need be—are referred to the common harmony of all, in the way my Harmonic means will be used.

II. Bodin puts forward an example from the *Cyropaedia*, by which all three kinds of proportion can be explained. The boy Cyrus, when he had seen a

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0180
   :page: p. 90

tall man clad in a short tunic,

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0180, p. 90)

   Judgment of the boy Cyrus.

and a dwarf beside him in a loose tunic, judged that their tunics ought to be exchanged, so that what was fitting might fall to each; the Master declared that each should keep his own.
But if the tall man had been ordered to pay the dwarf some money, and only so the exchange were made, the Master would have been best reconciled with his pupil. Here Cyrus urged Geometric proportion, measuring garments to bodies; the Master urged arithmetic, guarding each one's own possessions;
while that third party would have looked to both at once—both the need of the bodies and each one's wealth kept intact: thus he would have asserted Harmonic proportion in Bodin's sense, as mixed of both the former; and indeed harmonic in my sense too, because he would not hesitate
to take from one what cloth was superfluous, and from the other money, so that both might be provided for—since the common advantage of both is likened to the sweetness of a concord. Moreover, I said it happens that sometimes a Geometric proportion is also harmonic, as 1, 2, 4,
just as here it is fitting that the tall man have the long robe; and sometimes an Arithmetic proportion is also Harmonic, as 2, 3, 4: just as here it is fitting that the dwarf, owning the long robe, keep his own, yet converted into money, which he can use better than excess cloth.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0180, p. 90)

   Species of Justice.

III. Since justice embraces all principles of governing, he cites from Aristotle its two parts—commutative, dealing in arithmetic equality, and distributive, in Geometric likeness; he himself urges a third species of justice forged from both of the former,
which wills both that an abundance of goods be sold to certain persons at a cheaper price than to the rest, and that great things not always be distributed to great men; and thus that either kind of justice be somewhat violated, if the safety of all demands it, or public advantage arises therefrom.
A proportion is not well forged out of two other species if it destroys both. This application is therefore fitter for my Harmonic means: as in the numbers 2, 3, 5, there is neither arithmetic equality of excesses nor Geometric progression, yet there is a Harmonic proportion among them.

To this at the end of his book he adapts the Poets, who fashion three daughters of Themis (who is justice)—*Eunomia*, *Epieikeia*, *Eirene*: Laws, Equity, and Peace—as patronesses of the three proportions, Arithmetic, Geometric, and Harmonic.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0180, p. 90)

   Marriage laws.

IV. The laws of marriage, the chief bond of the state, furnish a shining example of the three proportions. If Patricians are bidden to wed patrician women, and plebeians plebeian women, that is Geometric likeness.
But if all have the right indiscriminately to seek marriages by lot, or strive as one can by beauty, wealth, or virtue regardless of birth, with no rank forbidden, this will be arithmetic equality. Yet there citizens' minds are torn into factions, here orders are confounded—both ruinous to the Commonwealth.
Bodin therefore advises permitting poorer Patricians at times to contract wealthy plebeian marriages, and rich Plebeians to marry poor patrician women. For this is advantageous to both orders: to the nobles, indeed, so that enriched in wealth they may uphold the station they hold, and women of this order may be married;
and to the plebs, so that with the way to honors laid open, they may strive after

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0181
   :page: p. 91

virtue; and finally to the Commonwealth itself, so that the Orders may embrace one another in mutual charity.

Here again he broke Geometric Analogy to produce Harmonic, yet did not reduce it to mere equality of Arithmetic progression. As if Geometric 1, 3, 9 be broken to become 1, 3, 8: as much as this departs from 1, 3, 9, so much is taken from patricians there;
and by as much as in 1, 3, 8, whose excesses are 2, 5 (whereas before they were 2, 6), we approached the arithmetic progression 1, 3, 5 (because their excesses 2, 2 are equal), so much was given there to the plebs:
so that in numbers Concordance might be established among 1, 3, 8, and in the Commonwealth concord between Patricians and plebs, while the distinction of orders still remained. This exchange of 1:9 into 1:8 will meet us in Book V even in the heavens themselves.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0181, p. 91)

   Law of Banquets.

V. Arithmetic equality at Banquets is inept if all recline indiscriminately with no regard to sex, rank, or age; insipid in turn is mere Geometric likeness. For if the learned are joined only with the learned, what good will they do the unlearned? If women only with women, what pleasure?
if the noisy with the noisy, who shall prompt them to modesty? But if you admit neither blind equality nor peevish likeness, and instead blend both, yet moderately and with the judgment of reason, the proportion will be harmonic:
you will make Old men rejoice in youths' sight and Men in women's, youths be ruled by elders' prudence and women by men's authority, the witty enliven the morose and in turn respect them without sinking into buffoonery. Again, this is no compounding of whole species, but some violation, that Harmony may arise.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0181, p. 91)

   Seating at the games.

VI. It belongs to arithmetic equality that in antiquity the Senators used to watch the games seated indiscriminately with the plebs; to geometric proportion, that afterward the Senators were separated from the plebs.
This is true if you consider only the games. Yet if you grasp all advantages of the Commonwealth together, since most were claimed for the Senators by geometric reason with the people excluded, this very equalizing of both orders at the games belonged to that tempering—harmonic in Bodin's sense—of the whole form of government:
for by it the plebs were soothed, as boys are by trifles, and invited to love the higher Orders, without detracting from the orders' dignity. It also squares with my Means, insofar as proportion is not kept so that concord may be kept.
Formerly Analogy was set aside in certain persons of the Orders, now in certain rewards.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0181, p. 91)

   Friendship.

VII. Harmonic tempering animates friendships. For what Concordance is in proportion, that Love—the foundation of Friendship—is in the whole compass of human life.
But if mere equality of duties is brought in by arithmetic law, there will be no friendship except among equals; and if among unequals a mere and strict Geometric likeness of Duties, in neither case will there be friendship, but in the one a perpetual bargaining and trafficking of duties for one's own profit,
and in the other a necessary association of Patron and Client, no freedom to show Love, nothing spontaneous.
Although friendship cannot coexist with frequent injuries, yet it rejects laws and refers all things to the sober and sound judgment of Love, now equal,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0182
   :page: p. 92

repaying now proportional things of such kind, sometimes neither, always such as seem, according to the occasion, to serve for the preservation of Love: which also, like Harmony by dissonances and like fire by an iron poker, is stirred up by certain injuries, and renews its strength by their free pardon.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0182, p. 92)

   The Lesbian Rule.

**VIII.** Bodin finely compares Arithmetic equality to the iron Rule of Polycleitus, which could be broken sooner than bent; Geometric similarity to the leaden Lesbian Rule, which was fitted to all angles; and he shadows forth Harmonic proportion, by his definition, by a wooden Rule, which bends indeed, yet springs back immediately.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0182, p. 92)

   Laws; Equity.

**IX.** In the governance of every kind of Commonwealth, the rigor of the laws and the office of the Judge are compared to Arithmetic equality, by which not only is his own rendered to each man by a plumb line, but penalties are also inflicted equally upon offenders, without respect of persons,
and by which Judges are bound to the laws, and to what is alleged and proved, so that they must judge according to them, even if the laws seem unjust. Conversely, Equity and the pure discretion of a Magistrate, provided he be good and act from conscience, put on the nature of Geometric Analogy.
Yet Bodin, by the example of Harmonic tempering, interposes the office either of the higher Courts, or of all, or of the supreme Magistrate, to whom it is neither permitted to act from mere discretion, and yet it is allowed to bend the laws—not break them—according to the circumstances,
and to interpret them in judgments according to equity, so long as nothing is taken away from the law by individual rulings of the courts. He wishes the laws to be held like his own Rule, so that they yield indeed into a bend, yet immediately return.
Although in this place Bodin proved by his own example to be true what he had affirmed before of Jurists: that they perceive those Mathematical matters less well on account of their obscurity. For his disputation on Numbers does not square with the standard of our discipline;
since he says it cannot happen that a Geometric Proportion among four terms should perish by any transposition of the terms, and asserts the same analogy to be in 6, 3, 4, 2 as is in 3, 2, 4, 6. By no means: 3 is to 2 as 4 is to 6.
On the contrary, he thinks Harmonic Proportion is disturbed by transposition. Surely, if he acknowledges no proportion between two terms alone, he wins.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0182, p. 92)

   What proportion is to me.

For this is the subtlety of speech of some men, joined with the greatest obscurity, that what the Greeks call λόγον they wish to call *Ratio* in Latin, but *Proportion* what the Greeks call Ἀναλογίαν. I would indeed wish to be able to imitate that, as I remember having done before now:
yet among the Greeks λόγος is never taken in common usage for the word αἴτιον, whereas in Latin *Ratio* most frequently means cause or Manner. We must therefore keep the custom introduced by the Barbarous translators of the Greek Elements, and take the word *proportion* both for λόγῳ and for Ἀναλογίᾳ, which I often do in this book.

Now therefore Proportion and Concordance are distinguished, and the latter is as it were a certain quality of the former, and the former the subject of the latter. Indeed Concordance belongs to Proportions primarily and of itself, insofar as they are between two terms;
not insofar as there is among several terms a certain continuity

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0183
   :page: p. 93

of proportion. And two terms of a proportion are consonant not because of position, in which there is prior and posterior, but insofar as two strings are struck together at the same time;
nor is this concordance changed when the position of the characters in writing or of the Lute turned upside down is changed. But to a proportion among three or more terms, concordance belongs secondarily, on account of the individual pairs of terms: in which, although the Analogy can be changed when the position is changed,
namely that which is mixed into them from Geometric proportion, yet the Harmonic quality cannot be changed at the same time. Wherefore what Bodin argues—that those four things, Law, Execution of law, Equity, and Magistrate, are compared to four numbers bound together by Harmonic proportion,
and just as the consonance perishes when the numbers are transposed, so if forensic processes and execution are held superior to the Laws, and if the Magistrate is placed before equity, the harmony of the state is disturbed: this indeed is in no way to be referred to what is Harmonic in proportions,
but simply to the nature of the proportions themselves, both Geometric and Arithmetic, that is, to that genus of proportion which, tempered out of both, the Ancients on some weak persuasion called Harmonic. For this is common to all Analogies whether simple or mixed, contrary to what Bodin teaches,
that when the terms are transposed they either change into other species or are utterly destroyed. Yet I return to the subject.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0183, p. 93)

   The necessity of laws.

If a Magistrate has sheer discretion to rule without laws, he keeps neither Nature's law nor the office of a good, innocent man: he will be not a magistrate, but a Tyrant. And since good Princes are exceedingly few, it is unsafe for the Commonwealth to expose itself to this peril whenever a magistracy is renewed.
Even if a good magistrate sometimes befalls it, that state cannot endure: nor is it salutary for the Commonwealth, since however good a Ruler may be, he is not always wise enough to discern equity without a standard of laws; and even if he sees and follows right, citizens will slander even his best deeds,
unless there are laws by which he can defend his actions before the people: Citizens must be trained by the discipline of laws, and deterred from misdeeds by the image of the penalties prescribed by laws. Therefore a Commonwealth cannot do without laws.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0183, p. 93)

   And of equity.

Conversely, laws—by the Greek word Νόμοι—signify the distributive part of justice, which in a popular state follows arithmetic equality. Yet if everything is examined by the rigor of laws, injury will be done to very many. For laws lay down rules concerning deeds without any defined variety of circumstances;
whereas individual deeds are marked by their own circumstances, by which they are either mitigated or aggravated; and since the variety of these is such that they can never be comprehended by any laws. Neither principle, therefore, preserves the Commonwealth by itself alone: rather Bodin teaches that they must be blended,
so that there be laws, yet few, because a multitude of laws is the richest crop of lawsuits; and that many things be reserved to the discretion of the Magistrate—not only individual deeds, but also some entire classes of deeds, and certain other matters of laws, especially

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0184
   :page: p. 94

the heightening and remission of penalties, the interpretation of the laws themselves, and their application to the circumstances of a deed. Here we are treating not of the threefold form of a Commonwealth, but of the threefold manner of governing each one; a pure principle of equity, free of laws,
is an aristocratic manner of governing, even in a Popular and in a Royal state. The bonds of laws make a popular government, even in a Royal one. But if Laws indeed exist, yet are set aside in place and time at the discretion of a governor appointed by the supreme power,
that governor, even though appointed by the people, acts the part of a kind of petty King. Such for the most part are Governors of Provinces. He therefore either observes the laws or judges from equity, insofar as the safety of the Commonwealth permits: if otherwise, he does not hesitate to step beyond bounds.
Just as if someone were to admit the species of proportions that make harmony, such as 2, 3, 4 or 1, 2, 4, and disturb those that make dissonance, changing them into consonant ones, such as 1, 3, 9 into 1, 3, 8, and 3, 5, 7 into 3, 5, 8.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0184, p. 94)

   Fines.

**X.** In the laws themselves which concern pecuniary fines, for the most part arithmetic equality is observed, so that whoever has offended, whether rich or poor, pays the fine established by the laws. Yet one may see where Geometric similarity also exists, as when a fine is imposed on each in proportion to his estate,
or when a rash litigant loses an aliquot part of the estimated lawsuit. But if among all the wealthy some greater fine is appointed, among the poor a lighter one, and that is exacted equally from every member of each rank, that will be a tempering of the proportion called Harmonic by Bodin.
Here the species of proportions are not changed, but compounded, or rather distributed into distinct subjects belonging to the same whole.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0184, p. 94)

   Sumptuary laws.

**XI.** Laws concerning dress have most in common with Geometric proportion, so that the higher the degree of dignity in which each person stands, the costlier adornment of garments is permitted to him: Arithmetic equality is intolerable here. Yet because neither the variety of ranks alone demands distinction in clothing,
but some account must be taken here of wealth and some of merit, nor can all Ranks easily be set forth in a single law, some tempering of both proportions is wont to be inserted partly into the laws themselves, and partly left to the discretion of Rulers.
This tempering Bodin judges harmonic, because he defines Harmonies by the joining of two species of proportion. To me it is not harmonic, since another proportion is aimed at, and would be expressed perfectly if it could be.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0184, p. 94)

   Penalties.

**XII.** Almost the same must be said of those Laws concerning penalties which exempt illustrious persons from capital danger, and Nobles from hanging, changing the kind of punishment, by a custom received among very many nations. For the culprit, losing the dignity he had, is punished more severely by this disgrace
(Geometric law being observed) than if one who was in no dignity were beaten with rods publicly: therefore there can be a certain equality of dissimilar punishments, and a Harmonic distribution in the sense of Bodin, who here rejects Aristotle, who had taught that Geometric proportion should be kept in rewards, but Arithmetic in penalties.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0184, p. 94)

   Satisfaction.

**XIII.** There is also another proportion observable in penal Laws with the same tempering, which touches not the offender, but the honor and satisfaction of the injured person. For he is more severely punished who slays a Prince of the State,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0185
   :page: p. 95

or one from the College of Electors, than him who slays a farmer; more severely him who slays a freeman, than a slave. For although this variety must be referred to a Geometric similarity between the person and the satisfaction for the injury done to him, yet it is rare:
conversely, in penalties for homicide there is most of arithmetic equality, especially among Christians, from the divine law by which man is equated to man, and blood is repaid with blood. Therefore this blending of both kinds of ratios belongs to what Bodin calls Harmonic:
especially this, that in divine law all assassins are punished with death by arithmetic equality, yet the manner of death is in the power of the judge, to be inflicted by a Geometric correspondence upon deeds unlike in the variety of circumstances, as Bodin reports, I think from the Rabbis.

Nor is this inequality of penalties attributed so much to individual injured persons as to the safety of the whole commonwealth, which permits all enemies of the fatherland to be slain with impunity, and guards the safety of all citizens together in the preservation of the Prince and the Nobles and in public tranquillity.
Now it has been said often that the public Good has a certain correspondence to the sweetness of a Harmonic consonance. And if this one supreme law, mother of all laws, be observed—that whatever the safety of the Commonwealth turns upon be commanded to be sacred and lawful—
then straightway there is agreement concerning its likeness to Harmonic ratios, described even according to my own judgment, even if that law contains nothing else similar either to Geometric or to Arithmetic proportions. For neither do these numbers 15, 20, 24, 30, which contain the sweetest Harmony,
observe either the one proportion or the other: for their differences are 5, 4, 6, neither equal, nor similar in proportion to the proportions of the terms, nor even increasing in the same order with them.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0185, p. 95)

   Valuation of a fine.

**XIV.** Another example, closer to the Geometric discipline, Bodin brings forward in the interpretation of Laws concerning fines; namely that pecuniary fines established in ancient times should be valued according to the present public wealth or poverty. Here Arithmetic equality across all times is intolerable; yet Geometric proportion is not easy to observe in all cases.
Therefore the judge, according to his prudence and the circumstances of affairs, will follow a harmonic mean, and take care lest he appear either to have violated the laws, or by malicious interpretation of the laws to oppress the needy while license to sin is given to the wealthy and powerful.
Indeed, out of zeal for the same harmony he will sometimes commute a pecuniary penalty, which would be despised, into a corporal one.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0185, p. 95)

   Penalty for theft.

**XV.** The law which punishes thieves by hanging, Bodin approves insofar as it rests on the reason that thieves should not multiply,
so that sins which would otherwise be frequent are punished more severely, which savors of Geometric proportion. The same law he censures on account of Arithmetic equality. For the prosecution of theft, most frequent throughout all towns and villages, usually has plebeian judges,
who, haling all alike to execution, do not see that they introduce a most unjust inequality of penalties, inflicting the same doom on sins most unequal in circumstances. Most defendants lack that firmness of judgment to guard more against misfortune than against the magnitude of crime: whom opportunity,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0186
   :page: p. 96

hunger,
zeal for their children, confidence of escaping notice, and the notion of another's wealth make thieves; whereas the deed itself, compared with other things, is held cheap by natural judgment, which even the divine law, punishing theft only with fourfold restitution, seems to confirm.
Yet if it is equitable and holy, for the sake of the Commonwealth rather, that that portion of men—born to guile and darkness, imitating the nature of mice, digging through walls, disturbing the public security not of fortunes only but also of life—be thinned out like other rapacious beasts;
certainly regard for the Commonwealth, as has often been said, corresponds to harmonic consonance, nor ought this inquiry to be exercised without the most exact harmonic tempering; lest, while caring for the Commonwealth, life be rashly snatched away for this kind of crime from many who can give hope of a good citizen hereafter.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0186, p. 96)

   Penalty of the excused.

**XVI.** This too in inflicting penalties belongs to Geometric equity, that the punishment of a boy is milder than that of an adult, of a youth than of an old man, of a woman than of a man. Nothing is more unjust here than perpetual arithmetic equality;
yet if that be taken away from the judge's prudence to be exercised as occasion demands, Harmonic proportion will nevertheless be disturbed.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0186, p. 96)

   Relapsed offenders.

**XVII.** Bodin shows Harmonic proportion in those laws which avenge the first act of crime equally in all with a light fine,
as an admonition, punish those who relapse more severely, third-time offenders yet more severely, and finally strike with death. For arithmetic equality in the first act is mixed with Geometric proportion in a repeated, and thus heavier, crime's heavier penalty. This compounding of two into one seemed to Bodin to constitute harmonic proportion.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0186, p. 96)

   Retaliation.

**XVIII.** In general, the fact that penalties are dispensed either by laws or by the judge's discretion according to the diversity and magnitude of crimes, and so the entire law of Retaliation, must be assigned to Geometric analogy: whereas certain legislators avenge unequal kinds of sins equally:
this arithmetic rule has no place among men, and in God, whose creature we all equally are, it is not lawful to censure it with our complaints. Yet that lawmakers equalize certain crimes lighter in themselves with the gravest by the infliction of penalties for the sake of the Commonwealth,
and that in the law of retaliation not a tooth for a tooth, but instead of a tooth an estimated price—on which the litigants can agree—is repaid, nor is sheer blindness inflicted upon him who tore out one of my two eyes when he himself was one-eyed, by tearing out his own:
this belongs to what Bodin calls Harmonic tempering: for thus both individual sins have arithmetically equal penalties, and diverse ones are also distinguished by their penalties.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0186, p. 96)

   of intent.

**XIX.** Those who punish impulses of the mind with a penalty no lighter than if the outcome follows the will, and those who punish a deed from which intention was absent, establish plebeian arithmetic equality with no light fault—there in unlike attempts of the mind, here in unlike misdeeds.
He who distinguishes here among various forms of attempts—so that one who did everything a meditating mind could bring to pass has the crime's penalty, whether he achieved it or missed the mark; while one who only lay in wait for opportunities, yet did not come to

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0187
   :page: p. 97

the thing itself, by whose wicked image he could have been deterred: this man observes geometric equity. Nor any less does he who punishes bare persuasion to a crime more lightly than the crime itself in the perpetrator; and conversely, he who punishes the author and instigator more severely than the instrument, as one who committed another's crime;
and finally, he who punishes more lightly one whom a great force of lust or anger swept off course, than one who, calmer from those passions, accomplished everything with a premeditated mind. The same must be held concerning the variety of outcomes.
He deserves the same penalty who robs a traveler of three obols, if in his mind he gaped after great booty, as he who snatched a talent: conversely, he deserves a lesser penalty who, when he could have carried off the whole weight of gold, took little, than he who snatched a purse holding a few pennies.
These Geometric distinctions Bodin indeed refers to the philosophical estimation of crimes; yet penalties by which examples are publicly set, pertaining to public tranquillity, he exempts from that universal equality: so that thus deeds which run into the eyes of citizens are more frequently animadverted upon publicly;
more frequently attempts missing their mark—as being inconspicuous to the crowd—are passed over unpunished, or punished more lightly than was geometrically fitting, so that less of cruelty may be found with the judge. So many regards for the public good savor of something Harmonic.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0187, p. 97)

   Law of votes.

**XX.** In judgment and in deliberations, the reckoning of votes is Arithmetic if they are counted; Geometric if they are weighed, whether by the gravity of him who casts the vote or by the goodness of the arguments: a harmonic mixture (in Bodin's sense) existed among the Romans, whose Consulars set forth their opinion in a speech,
while the rest walked over to that opinion which they approved. To me, what was done by the law of necessity (for time would not have sufficed for hearing all) does not seem comparable to Harmonic proportion.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0187, p. 97)

   Commutative justice.

**XXI.** In commutative justice Aristotle required Arithmetic equality. Bodin says Geometric analogy is not banished even here, although the examples seem confused.
1. As if someone, by giving public scandal, has also injured a private person: he will satisfy the private person separately by arithmetic equality, nor will more be restored to him for his property by a nobleman than by a farmer;
yet the former will atone for the very wicked example in one way, the latter in another, with Geometric proportion of ranks preserved. In which place Aristotle is reconciled with Bodin.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0187, p. 97)

   Creditors.

2. Now as to the fact that the goods of one who is insolvent are distributed according to the magnitude of each debt, without respect of creditors, provided they are equal in time and quality of debt, that indeed is done by a merely geometric proportion;
which as Aristotle did not deny, so it seems omitted by him through oversight, and rightly supplied by Bodin; even though this is founded on arithmetic equality of payment and debt, if the Goods sufficed.
Nor yet is a higher magistrate wholly forbidden to use harmonic tempering, and to prefer needy creditors to some extent, as occasion arises, over the wealthier.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0187, p. 97)

   Restitution of theft.

3. That also savors of something harmonic (in Bodin's sense), that by the custom of certain nations things taken by theft are compensated not simply, but fourfold, and other things fivefold;
equally indeed by all, yet unequally for diverse things, in which the diverse magnitude of the injury, not the bare price of the thing, is estimated by a geometric ratio.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0188
   :page: p. 98

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0188, p. 98)

   Damages.

4. More evident is the harmonic ratio in the compensation of damages not inflicted with evil intent: where very much rests on the circumstances of the deeds of both contracting parties, which sometimes, either by laws or at the Magistrate's command, require an equal and arithmetic compensation,
sometimes none, and sometimes one moderated by equity and goodness.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0188, p. 98)

   Arbitrators.

Of this kind seems also that fact, that the higher magistrate does not grant to all equally to try their right among themselves at law, nor in all causes,
but according as the circumstances may be, either himself appoints arbitrators or permits their choice to the litigants, so that either the interests of both or the Commonwealth may be consulted.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0188, p. 98)

   Interest.

5. Interest also pertains to the commutative part of justice;
nor yet are equal rates permitted to every kind of men by all legislators; but by a geometric mixture, unequal for persons of diverse ranks, equal for individuals of the same rank: which compounding is harmonic to Bodin.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0188, p. 98)

   Remuneration.

6. I shall copy out in Bodin's words a notable exception from arithmetic equality of exchange.
*A physician for extracting a stone from a rich man often receives (indeed demands) 500 gold pieces, but from a poor man 10 or 5 (indeed nothing, by the formula of the oath of Hippocrates):*
*and if indeed they followed arithmetic or geometric Analogy everywhere, the one would perish of the stone, the other of hunger: but by harmonic ratio, wealth is procured for the one, health for the other (not indeed with the same certainty).*

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0188, p. 98)

   The judge's fee.

7. After this same ratio judges too have been accustomed to exact their labor and honorarium in deciding causes. For often the difficulty of the slightest lawsuits is greater than if inquiry is made concerning the weightiest matters, yet unfruitful to the judges.
It is therefore equitable that somewhat more of a fee be exacted from adjudicated claims of the greatest possessions: namely when judges do not receive a sufficient salary from the Commonwealth, but, with the design of lessening lawsuits, are bidden to seek compensation for their hired labor from the contributions of the litigating parties.
Although this Harmonic concert I leave back to its author, the Frenchman Bodin: among us Germans, the courts in the principal cities and provinces are kept far from such sordidness; nor is it lawful to exact anything beyond the prescription of the laws.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0188, p. 98)

   Division of inheritances.

8. An equal distribution of inheritances per head for both sexes is carried out by arithmetic laws: so that those equal in origin may also be made equal in their portions of the substance left by the same father.
Yet when the Commonwealth is consulted, estates pass to the males, who may be fit for necessary military service, while monies pass to the women and to persons consecrated to God; and while firstborns are preferred to the rest either by a greater portion or by possession of the whole estate, the rest are provided for
either by equal portions among themselves, or by a legacy, or by usufruct alone, so that a certain image of principality, useful to the Commonwealth, may be established in families; that is done by harmonic ratios, which are sometimes very harsh and dissonant as to individuals,
since arithmetic equality is disturbed: yet they conspire to the preservation of the whole state.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0188, p. 98)

   Forms of Commonwealth.

**XXII.** In popular rule, which is formed from Arithmetic equality, and in aristocratic rule, which is formed from Geometric similarity, nevertheless some regard for tempering is often had.
As when the People itself,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0189
   :page: p. 99

holding sway over affairs, of its own accord entrusts honors, magistracies, and priesthoods chiefly to the patricians; or when the Nobles make the plebeians sharers in certain honors, grant the most lucrative offices to plebeians alone, and most severely avenge injuries inflicted upon them by the Nobles,
grant liberty of enjoying pleasures, which of itself savors of a popular state; and permit a certain right of suffrage to the plebeians, in naming a fixed number of Candidates from the stock of the Nobles, out of which number the Nobles themselves afterward entrust Magistracies to whom they will.

This sharing, as long as it stands firm by the free will of that Order with whom the supreme rule lies, maintains the sweetest concert of harmony among the citizens; yet if it be revoked, first complaints of the fined party emerge, then discords, as if with the concert disturbed,
and finally either the rule is transferred, or the whole state succumbs to enemies. The same must be held concerning the Royal state, to which the harmonic ratio is familiar: in which, by as much as one surpasses all the rest, by so much does he alone also possess the supreme right of rule,
while the rest live under him in arithmetic equality.

In this form of Polity, therefore, there can be a manner of governing of three kinds, which we also touched upon above in No. IX; yet there we treated of the matter itself, while here we treat of persons, or Orders, the members of the Commonwealth.
For if the King should impart all offices equally, with no distinction of nobility from the plebs, the governance will be popular and similar to arithmetic, not becoming to supreme majesty: for the highest himself will be joined with the lowest dregs, with no intermediate order placed between, which yet Nature loves.
Nay more, citizens of noble character will depart the kingdom if there is no regard for birth.

But if he confers all things upon the nobility, wholly shutting out the plebs, this will be a Geometric form of ruling, yet dangerous and lacking harmonic sweetness: for the people, strong in numbers, at length offended,
will strip the Nobility of Magistracies and honors, and the King himself of rule. And although the Ruler should more subtly distinguish the Orders into several degrees, and distinguish their offices as well; yet if he preserves its own to each order alone,
as at Rome the Tribunate belonged to the plebs alone, the Consulship to the patricians only: the Orders will be alienated from one another, nor will they coalesce into one body in firm concord among themselves. For the offices of the lower orders will be despised together with the order itself:
insomuch that at Rome a patrician could not hold the Tribunate unless he abjured his nobility; and when the plebs had at length wrested away the consulship, yet those plebeian Consuls themselves, few in number, despised the plebs thereafter.

Bodin sets forth an image of these disputations in numbers: and first in these, 4, 6, 7. Here 4 represents the King, 6 the Nobility, and between these he admonishes there is harmony, while 7 represents the plebs, which number he rightly affirms is discordant with both of the former.
He could better have used these: 4, 6, 9. For as 6 makes a harmony with 9, while 4 makes none with the same 9, so association with the plebs is easier for the Nobility than for the King;
and as 6 is the Geometric mean between 4 and 9, concordant with both, so the Nobility is placed between as a bond between the King and the lowest plebs.

Now the Aristocratic form of governing a Kingdom he represents by the numbers of a disjunct Geometric proportion: 3, 6, 5, 10.
He compares the disjunction to dissensions in the State arising from an overly strict seclusion of the orders,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0190
   :page: p. 100

not ineptly. Yet this is bad, that he arraigns this disjunction as guilty of discordance. For all these numbers, 3, 6, 5, 10, make a common harmony.
For Geometric Analogy pertains not at all to the affection of Harmony, as has often been said; nor does the difference of that analogy, disjunction. And although, as if correcting himself, on the same page he subjoins another example of numbers of a disjunct proportion destined to the same intent, namely 2, 4, 9, 18:
in which the sounds marked by 2, 4 truly dissent from the sounds 9, 18, yet this musical discordance is not from that Geometric disjunction, but from its own proper causes, namely from this, that the side of the Nonagon is not among Knowable things (Ἐπιστητά).

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0190, p. 100)

   Tempering of Royal Governance.

Finally, the tempering of both forms of governing in a Royal state: namely, that some plebeians be mingled in the offices of the Nobility, yet few, and equal to the burden in means, or commended by virtue or some other quality; and that sometimes one of the poorer Nobles be set over a lucrative plebeian function,
to cast luster around it by the splendor of his birth, whereby it may thereafter be more commendable to the plebs: that a Duumvirate be established out of a Noble and a plebeian, to the consolation of both orders; that assemblies be formed of persons of all orders;
that in deliberations on the public good the poor be mingled with the wealthy; that mere cultivators of virtue and piety may not have all things, but some be reserved for the brave, some for the ingenious, some for the prudent, some for those prevailing in experience,
to each those offices to which he brings fitting gifts and the greatest support: this tempering, I say, Bodin expresses by the Numbers 4, 6, 8, 12, thinking that he puts forth a species of what he calls *continuous Analogy*, or as Geometers say, *continued proportion*.
Again he is deceived; for 4 is not to 6 as 6 is to 8: yet this is true, that of all four, 4, 6, 8, 12, or in the least terms 2, 3, 4, 6, there is a common concordance,
however differently 2 and 3 consonate, differently also 3 and 4, and differently finally 2 and 4 or 3 and 6. Whatever likeness, therefore, exists in numbers to a Commonwealth, belongs to them insofar as, apart from Music, they are associated by geometric or arithmetic progressions, or by a mixture of both;
or conversely, if Harmonic proportions of Numbers bring any light to the understanding of Polities, they do so of themselves, apart from regard to geometric proportions.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0190, p. 100)

   The true Harmonic idea of the Best Commonwealth.

And indeed, if I had acquired knowledge of the Commonwealth and were playing the Politician in this book, using a distinction of things I would recast Bodin's whole passage thus, so that both Aristotle's doctrines might remain unshaken, and Bodin might learn from this Harmonics of mine, apart from him, to philosophize better on polity.
I would say that the state of a City and its form of governing is one thing, and the administration of justice another, for they differ as part and whole; just as in Mathematics Geometric and Arithmetic proportions of numbers are one thing, and Musical concerts expressed by numbers another.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0190, p. 100)

   That Geometric and Arithmetic proportions do not pertain to Reason of state, as Harmonic ones do, but to judgments only.

Those who hold power, whether one, or a few, or all, indeed establish the courts; yet they themselves are occupied with the guardianship of the Commonwealth,
and rarely exercise judgment themselves, except when it is lawful to use discretion unbound by laws. Conversely, higher offices are rarely entrusted to Judges; rather, laws are prescribed by which they are bound.
Therefore I would relegate Geometric and Arithmetic progressions to the courts with Aristotle, without mingling regard for the harmonic concerts which befall them. For the temperings which Bodin has hitherto desired in the courts devolve at last, through appeals, to the supreme ruler of the Commonwealth himself.
And then, when he sets aside the proportions which Aristotle gave to Courts and justice,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0191
   :page: p. 101

he no longer acts as judge, but performs a higher office, guarding the Commonwealth and its individual members.
To this Ruler indeed, whether it be a king, or the Nobles, or the whole People, I would commend Harmonic proportions, with no regard to geometric Analogy, none to Arithmetic equality, but with a bare regard to concerts;
and I would bid those subtle Analogies yield in their place to Harmonic concerts, just as the rigid administration of both kinds of justice yields to supreme rule and the safety of the Commonwealth. Good God, what a harvest of disputations is here, if one wished to go through the particulars by comparing!
For consider the nature of each thing. Geometric analogy is common to Numbers and to continuous quantity, so that it enters into numbers later—then, to be sure, when configuration grants this to quantities, that they can be expressed by number. Now it happens most frequently that the terms embraced by Geometric Analogy are ineffable,
and the farther we proceed, the more it degenerates from Effability. On the contrary, every Harmonic proportion can be expressed in numbers. What else does this signify, applied to politics, than that the sentences of judges ought to be most just,
and most subtly tested by the siftings of Jurisconsults to the standard of laws and equity: whereas the governance of the Commonwealth, loosed from such strictures, ought to be accommodated according as the times may be, without great clamor, to the safety of all, at the discretion of him who rules.
Here the question would have to be divided into parts: how the rude ratio of Arithmetic progression, without any noble form, resting only upon the dimensions of matter, is likened to commerce, crafts, and plebeian livelihood;
how Geometric similarity (observing one single law, and taking no thought whether the line it beholds be the side of a pentagon or of a triangle, effable or ineffable) shadows forth the simple office of judges;
whereas Harmonic ratios, sprung from many knowable regular figures, joined into one though differing in kind, represent the many distinct offices of rule, to each of which belongs its own proper law, separate from the rest.
Here with Ptolemy (below in the appendix to Book V), from the genera of harmonies and song, the Soft would be given to Peace, the Hard to war; and the dissensions of the genera are the same as those in a state during both seasons.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0191, p. 101)

   Peace from the Soft Genus, War from the Hard.

Yet I leave these things to be cared for by others, to whose way of life they are more suited: I myself now hasten to the following subjects of my profession.
Therefore I shall first dispatch Bodin; for I am arriving at the end of his book, and at the same time at the beginning of this third book of mine.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0191, p. 101)

   The idea of a Kingdom in Numbers.

**XXIII.** For now Bodin, passing over wholly to music as it has been described by me apart from Geometric proportions, and accommodating it—as also pleases me—to Polities, disputes nothing more splendidly in his whole book than concerning the form of a Kingdom expressed by the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4,
which, as they are first by nature, and unity indeed is the principle of numbers, so are they compared in a pleasant play of wit to the principle of quantities, the Point, and thence to their three species—2 to the line, 3 to the surface, 4 to the body—
in almost the same manner as I recounted from the Pythagoreans at the beginning of this Book III. Next, unity to him is the King, the vicar of almighty God in the Commonwealth; 2, the Sacred order; 3, the Military or Equestrian order; 4, the People, and that altogether fourfold: Farmers, Scholars, Merchants, Artisans.
Here he recounts the harmonies comprehended among these numbers in the same manner that I used above: yet he has an idle cause of fear in advancing to the quaternary, as though harmony were disturbed, which is manifest to be false from this whole book.
Let other causes of stopping at the Quaternary indeed exist and prevail. Moreover, he demonstrates that this form of Commonwealth shines forth in Man himself,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0192
   :page: p. 102

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0192, p. 102)

   The Soul.

from Plato, in whom are 1, Mind, 2, the Reasoning faculty, pouring itself forth into syllogisms, 3, the Irascible, and 4, the Concupiscible faculties; whose seats also, in the brain, in the breast, and in the belly, Plato demonstrates to be stationed at harmonic intervals as well:
and to the first of them Rule is likened, to the second Counsel, to the third the Military order, and to the fourth the Plebs, and they possess most of it. Hence the four pillars of Commonwealths—Justice, Prudence, Fortitude, Temperance, truly called hinges—each commended especially to its own Order.
And so musical has Bodin become, having dismissed Geometry and Arithmetic, that he has not even forgotten dissonances; under the image of which thing, he advises sometimes not grudgingly to permit magistracies, honors, and the other rewards of virtues even to someone undeserving:
because, just as from the opposition of a single dissonance the remaining concert is so much the better discerned and so much the more eagerly drunk in, so whoever look upon and execrate this counterfeit of the Commonwealth's advantages may all grow accustomed more and more to flee like vices and pursue virtues.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0192, p. 102)

   The World.

**XXIV.** At length Bodin compares the Kingdom he has written of to the world itself, showing how God the creator, having joined the ratios of equals and similars into one Harmonic concert, adorned that work of His. In his purpose I assent to him, as much as anyone;
and what neither he himself nor preceding philosophers even touched upon, concerning the most accurate harmonic tempering of certain motions, I supply in the following books and bring forth into the light by the clearest demonstrations. Yet I do not cease, even at the heel, to gainsay Bodin when he wanders:
for true Harmonies do not arise from mixed ratios of equals and similars, as has often been said. Therefore Bodin, thinking he demonstrates Harmonies in the world, rather demonstrates geometric means, equalities, and similarities:
1. Harmonic ones indeed in the faculties of the Soul; yet those are Symbolic only, expressed with no evidence of demonstrations into any solid figure, as Mathematics loves, since it cannot be understood how quantities—the proximate and immediate subject of harmonies—are in the Soul.

.. admonition:: Marginal Note (fol. 0192, p. 102)

   Namely, in any faculty of it, that very number which Bodin assigns to it.

2. Equality between the body of the World and the whole force of matter. 3. Similarity (qualitative rather from figures than Relative of quantified terms) between the eternal Archetype and the form of the World;
in which place Bodin touches my heartstrings, the matter of my *Cosmographic Mystery* having been touched upon, albeit through ignorance.

But how much more plausibly in Book IV of my *Epitome of Copernican Astronomy* has this commixture of equals and similars been demonstrated concerning the principal parts of the World:
concerning the Sun as Mover, the sphere of the fixed stars as Locator, and the Intermediate region, which is granted to the movable bodies. For equality is established in the parts of matter, so that there is as much of it in the Sun as either in the orb of the outermost sphere or in the intermediate space.
Now to matter, as to the people, arithmetic equality is due. In turn, similarity of Geometric proportion lies in the density of these three bodies; and again another between the diameters of the moving Sun, the region of the Movables, and the immovable outermost Locating sphere.
4. Further, Geometric means after a fashion: as between two kinds of motions, one toward the east and the other toward the west, a third

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0193
   :page: p. 103

motion of Trepidation, though of these the latter two subsist only in the imagination of sight, according to the doctrines of Copernicus;
5. between earth and tufa, clay; 6. between metals and stones, ore-bearing clods; 7. between plants and stones, Corals; 8. between animals and plants, Zoophytes; 9. between Quadrupeds and fishes, Amphibians;
10. between birds and fishes, flying fishes; 11. between Men and Brutes, Apes, or Women according to Plato; 12. between beasts and Angels, Man, mortal in body like the former, immortal in soul like the latter;
13. between the celestial habitations of the blessed and the Elementary region, the starry heaven; 14. to which add, from the beginning of this book, the two means, namely Air and Water between Fire and Earth; and the rest.

Yet no little better is his admonition concerning those things which, in the governance of the world, have the character of dissonances: in the Soul, vices; in animals, monsters; in the Heaven, eclipses; in Geometry, ineffables
(which yet, since they exist by necessity of quantitative matter, will be compared far more rightly in the following books to the variety of the celestial motions); in the works of providence, Examples of divine wrath and vengeance; among Rational beings, the Devil:
all of which are directed by God, the supreme ruler, to a good end and to the most absolute Concert of all things: to Whom let every breathing creature offer the most well-deserved sacrifices of praise in unceasing exercise of piety;
and I myself too, if it shall seem good to Him, shall not die but live, and in the following books shall declare the Works of the Lord.
