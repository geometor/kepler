Chapter VIII: The Four-Part Planetary Choir (Bass, Tenor, Alto, Soprano)
========================================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0305
   :page: p. 213

*Which among the celestial consonances takes the part of the Discantus, which the Alto, which the Tenor, and which the Bass?*

Although these are names of human voices, and neither voices nor sounds exist in the heavens on account of the utter tranquility of the motions; nor indeed are the very subjects in which we perceive the Harmonies comprehended under the genus of true motion,
since we consider only the motions apparent from the Sun; and although, finally, there is no such cause in the heavens that calls forth voices in a fixed number to make a Harmony, as there is in human singing—for first there was the number of six Planets circling the Sun, from the number of the five intervals taken from the Regular figures, and only afterward (in the order of Nature, not of time)
was a determination to be made concerning the congruence of the motions—nevertheless, I know not how this wonderful congruence with human singing compels me, so that I am forced to pursue this part of the comparison as well, even without a solid natural cause. For the properties which custom attributes and nature claims for the Bass in Book III, Chapter XVI, Saturn and Jupiter in some manner hold in the heavens; those of the Tenor
we find in Mars; those of the Alto are present in the Earth and Venus; and those of the Discantus Mercury possesses, if not by equality of intervals, at least in proportion. However, indeed, the Eccentricities of each Planet are deduced from their own causes in the following chapter, and through them the proper intervals of each planet's motions, yet this wonderful result emerges therefrom—I know not whether it be a tempering procured
alongside, or merely necessitated: I. that just as the Bass is opposed to the Alto, so there are two Planets that have the nature of the Alto and two that have the nature of the Bass, as it were one on either side in whichever genus of song, and one each of the remaining individual Voices; II. that just as the Alto, nearly the highest voice, is in narrow straits from necessary and natural causes
