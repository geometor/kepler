Proposition XXVI: The Two Regular Star Polyhedra and Two Semisolid Star Congruences
===================================================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0084
   :page: p. 60

**Proposition XXVI.**

*To the most perfect regular congruences may also be added two other congruences, of twelve plane pentagonal stars; and two semisolids, of an octagonal and of a decagonal star.*

For the pentagonal stars (:term:`stella quinquangula <stella-quinquangula>`) enclose solid figures spiny on all sides: one of which is formed of twelve five-lined angles (Plate ``Ss``), and the other of twenty three-lined angles (Plate ``Tt``); the former stands upon three angles, the latter upon five at once; the former rises more gracefully upon an apex, the latter sits more squarely, resting upon five. In these, although externally no regular plane appears, but in its place an isosceles pentagonal triangle, yet five of this kind always falling together into one and the same plane surround a pentagon hidden beneath the solidity as their heart; and with it they make the said pentagonal star—called in the German tongue *Drudenfuß* (*pedem Truttae*, to Theophrastus Paracelsus the sign of health). The idea of the body is in a certain way the same as that of its plane: for just as in the latter—namely, in the pentagonal star—the sides of every pair of triangles fall into one straight line, which in its inner part becomes the base for one outer triangle, and a side for the innermost pentagon; so in the solid, the single isosceles triangles of five solid angles always fall into one plane, and the innermost marrow and heart of those five triangles or star—the pentagon—becomes the base in one surface of the solid angle, or in the other, the support of five solid angles. Moreover, so great is the kinship of these figures—the one with the Dodecahedron, the other with the Icosahedron—that the latter, especially the Dodecahedron, seem in a certain manner truncated and mutilated when compared with those spiny ones.

The octagonal and decagonal stars, on the other hand—by the sides of their rays, which always at the first and fourth (passing over two) meet in one straight line—always join two by two, and make, the former a kind of Cube, the latter a kind of Dodecahedron, not angled but eared figures: because when two plane angles are fitted together, a gap must necessarily arise which cannot be closed. Therefore by Definition XI, the congruence is only semisolid.

They are called *most perfect* congruences—the former solid, the latter semisolid—because Definition VI of this book applies to them as regards solidity; while to the planes themselves applies the definition of a perfect figure, which is the second in Book I, namely they are perfect secondarily. Nor do we speak absurdly in calling a semisolid *most perfect*, because we intimate that something is begun to which not Definition IX or X, but Definition VI would apply, if it could be completed.
