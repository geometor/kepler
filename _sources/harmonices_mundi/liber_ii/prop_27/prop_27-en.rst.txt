Proposition XXVII: The Two Most Perfect Rhombic Congruences
===========================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0085
   :page: p. 61

**Proposition XXVII.**

*Semiregular figures—namely plane rhombi—are also congruent most perfectly to form solid figures, and that only twice.*

For from twelve plane rhombi of a certain proportion of diagonals is made the :term:`solid rhombus <dodecaedron-rhombicum>`, the figure of the bee's cell as to its six sides and its three-faceted solid bottom (Plate ``Vv`` and the smaller figure here): for six rhombi congruent in such a way that obtuse angles are applied to obtuse and acute to acute have three obtuse-angled gaps, and likewise three pairs of acute angles projecting above and as many below. Therefore the three projections of three rhombi on each side, joined by their obtuse angles, fit into those gaps, and receive the projections of the former in their own gaps.

In the same manner thirty plane rhombi, of another proportion of diagonals (:term:`Divine Section <sectio-divina>`, :g:`PR_gold` to :g:`QS_gold`), make the :term:`Rhombic Triacontahedron <triacontaedron-rhombicum>` (Plate ``Xx`` and the larger figure here). For five and five rhombi, joined by their acute angles to fashion two solid shells standing in opposite regions, since they gape at the joined obtuse angles of five and five other rhombi, fill the gaps; and finally, in the midst as it were of both shells, a belt composed of ten rhombi runs round, joining the two shells together.

Now that there are no more perfect congruences of rhombi is proved thus. In a rhombus there are two acute plane angles and two obtuse, of which one acute and one obtuse always make two right angles; nor do more than three obtuse angles meet, lest they exceed four right angles. If therefore you join only three acute angles, there arises, as in the Cube, a rhombic hexahedron with only two acute solid angles, very far apart, while the remaining solid angles in the middle of the body are not so far apart: the laws of Definition VIII are therefore not observed, which does not admit two angles alone on the same sphere. Moreover, of the six obtuse solid angles, each is enclosed by two obtuse planes and one acute; which irregularity again is contrary to the definitions. Therefore three acute plane angles alone ought not to meet. Yet neither do six acute angles—namely of six rhombi—meet together. For if each has the magnitude of two thirds of one right angle, the obtuse angles will have double that magnitude, namely four thirds; and thus both three obtuse angles and six acute angles will form four right angles, and neither the former nor the latter will form a solid angle, but will pave a continuous plane, as in figure *G*. But if the acute angles be taken smaller, the obtuse will be greater, and three will exceed four right angles. Therefore there are only two most perfect rhombic congruences: one in which four acute rhombic angles meet in a solid, and the other in which five meet; to which, however, the Cube is associated as the principle of all rhombi, because its plane is of four equal sides, like that of the rhombi.
