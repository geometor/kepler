Types / person

Content of type 'person'

Albrecht Dürer

Author of Underweysung der Messung (1525) on polyhedral nets, pentagonal rosettes, and practical heptagon approximations.

Apollonius of Perga

Author of the Conics, whose focal theory of the ellipse enabled Kepler’s planetary kinematics.

Archimedes of Syracuse

Discoverer of the thirteen semi-regular convex solids reconstructed and proved complete by Kepler in Harmonices Mundi Book II.

Aristotle of Stagira

Author of Posterior Analytics, De Caelo, and Metaphysics; critically engaged by Kepler on mathematical causality and space-filling polyhedra.

Campanus of Novara

Medieval Latin commentator on Euclid, noted for star-pentagram angles and Book X irrationals.

Carl Friedrich Gauss

Characterized constructible regular $n$-gons via Fermat primes in Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801).

Christian Frisch

German philologist, mathematician, and editor of the eight-volume critical Joannis Kepleri Astronomi Opera Omnia (Frankfurt and Erlangen: Heyder & Zimmer, 1858–1871), supplying the authoritative Latin source texts for this digital edition.

Claudius Ptolemy

Author of the Almagest and the Harmonics, whose chord geometry and celestial harmonics Kepler transformed onto a heliocentric foundation.

Euclid of Alexandria

Author of the Elements (Stoicheia), Books I, II, IV, VI, X, and XIII, supplying the demonstrative backbone of Mysterium Cosmographicum and Harmonices Mundi.

Fra Luca Bartolomeo de Pacioli

Author of De Divina Proportione (1509), establishing Renaissance terminology of the Divine Proportion.

Galileo Galilei

Astronomer and physicist whose Sidereus Nuncius (1610) discoveries of Jupiter’s moons Kepler celebrated and integrated into Harmonices Mundi Book V.

Georg Joachim Rheticus

Author of Narratio Prima (1540), appended by Mästlin to the 1596 Mysterium Cosmographicum.

Harold Scott MacDonald Coxeter

Author of Regular Polytopes, systematizing Kepler’s uniform tilings, star polyhedra, and zonohedra.

Johannes Kepler

Imperial Mathematician, astronomer, and geometer. Author of Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596/1621), Astronomia Nova (1609), Strena seu de Nive Sexangula (1611), and Harmonices Mundi Libri V (1619). Kepler bridged classical Euclidean synthetic geometry and modern mathematical physics, discovering the polyhedral planetary nesting, the space-filling rhombic dodecahedron, hexagonal close-packing, the Kepler triangle, two regular star polyhedra, and the harmonic laws of planetary motion.

Johannes Matthäus Wackher von Wackenfels

Imperial Aulic Councillor at Prague and dedicatee of Strena seu de Nive Sexangula (1611).

Jost Bürgi

Swiss Imperial clockmaker and Cossic algebraist whose chord equation for the regular heptagon Kepler critiqued in Harmonices Mundi Book I Prop. XLV.

Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci)

Author of Liber Abaci (1202); Kepler independently formulated the additive law $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21\dots \to \phi$ in Strena (1611).

Louis Poinsot

Rediscovered Kepler’s two stellated dodecahedra in 1809–1810 and discovered their duals.

Michael Mästlin

Professor of Mathematics and Astronomy at the University of Tübingen, Kepler’s university mentor and lifelong correspondent. Mästlin secured the publication of the 1596 Mysterium Cosmographicum, contributed its appended trigonometric treatise on planetary distances, and received Kepler’s historic October 1597 letter formulating the Kepler Right Triangle ($1 : \sqrt{\phi} : \phi$).

Nicolaus Copernicus

Author of De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (1543), establishing the six heliocentric planetary orbs harmonized by Kepler.

Nicolaus Cusanus (Nicholas of Cusa)

Cardinal and mathematical theologian (De Docta Ignorantia, 1440), praised by Kepler as divinus mihi Cusanus for contrasting the spherical curved (Creator) with the polyhedral straight (creation).

Pappus of Alexandria

Author of the Synagoge, Book V of which preserved the hexagonal honeycomb theorem and face counts of the 13 Archimedean solids.

Petrus Hubertus Jacobus Hoenen, S.J.

Author of De Noetica Geometriae (1954) in the GEOMETOR canon.

Petrus Ramus (Pierre de la Ramée)

Humanist critic of Euclid Book X, refuted by Kepler in Harmonices Mundi Book I Prooemium.

Pierre Laurent Wantzel

Proved in 1837 that irreducible cubics (including the heptagon and nonagon) are non-constructible by ruler and compass.

Plato of Athens

Author of the Timaeus, associating the regular polyhedra with elemental structure and the dodecahedron with the cosmos.

Proclus Diadochus

Neoplatonic commentator on Euclid Book I, quoted extensively by Kepler on innate archetypal geometry.

Pythagoras of Samos

Founder of the Pythagorean harmonic and geometric tradition; honored by Kepler alongside the Divine Section as discoverer of the first “great treasure of geometry” ([I.47]).

Sir D’Arcy Wentworth Thompson

Author of On Growth and Form (1917/1942), extending Kepler’s Strena into mathematical biology.

Sir Roger Penrose

Discoverer of non-periodic pentagonal/decagonal tilings (1974) inspired directly by Kepler’s Figure Aa.

Thomas Callister Hales

Proved the 1611 Kepler Sphere-Packing Conjecture (1998/2005) and led its Flyspeck formal verification (2014).

Tycho Brahe

Imperial observational astronomer at Prague whose decades of high-precision planetary observations provided the empirical foundation for Kepler’s discovery of the elliptical orbits (Astronomia Nova, 1609) and the planetary harmonic consonances (Harmonices Mundi, 1619).

Willebrord Snellius

Dutch mathematician at Leiden addressed by Kepler in Harmonices Mundi Book I concerning Euclid Book X.
