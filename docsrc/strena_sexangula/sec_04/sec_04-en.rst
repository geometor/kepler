Pentamerous Blossoms & the Six-Cornered Snowflake
=================================================

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0034
   :page: p. 12

On the other hand, if one asks why virtually all trees and shrubs—or certainly most of them—unfold their blossoms in a pentagonal form, that is, with a fivefold number of petals,
 which blossom in apples and pears is followed by the arrangement of the fruit in the same or a kindred number, fivefold or tenfold: five inner chambers for holding the seeds, ten filaments—which also holds in cucumbers and others of that kind—here, I say,
 there is room for speculation on the beauty or property of the figure that has stamped the soul of these plants. And along the way I will disclose my own thoughts on this matter.

There are two regular solids, the dodecahedron and the icosahedron, of which the former is explicitly shaped from pentagons, and the latter from triangles indeed, yet fitted together into the form of a pentagon. The construction of both of these solids—and indeed of the pentagon itself—cannot be completed without that proportion which modern geometers call :term:`Divine <sectio-divina>` (:just:`II.11`, :just:`XIII.5`).
 Now it is so constituted that the two smaller terms of a continuous proportion, when joined together, make the third; and always the two nearest terms added together make the one immediately following, with the same proportion enduring forever, onward to infinity. In whole numbers it is impossible to give a perfect example;
 yet the farther we advance from unity, the more perfect the example becomes. Let the smallest terms be $1$ and $1$, which you may imagine unequal; add them, making $2$; to which add the larger $1$, making $3$; to which add $2$, making $5$; to which add $3$, making $8$; to which add $5$, making $13$; to which add $8$, making $21$. For always, as $5$ is to $8$, so is $8$ to $13$ very nearly, and as $8$ is to $13$, so is $13$ to $21$ very nearly.

.. picture:: /_static/figures/fig05_fibonacci_golden_phyllotaxis.svg
   :alt: Pentamerous Blossom Pentagram and Self-Propagating Fibonacci Golden Spiral

After the likeness of this self-propagating proportion, I hold that the seminal faculty is fashioned: and thus in the flower is displayed the authentic standard of the seminal faculty, the pentagon. I pass over other things that might be adduced to confirm this by most delightful contemplation, since a more fitting place is owed to them.

Now I have set these things before you merely by way of example, so that in probing the figure of the six-cornered snow we may be better instructed and more practiced.

For when I had proposed to inquire into the origin of this figure in snow among extrinsic and intrinsic causes, among the external causes cold first presented itself.
 Condensation surely comes from cold; and through condensation vapor gathers into the figure of a star; therefore cold seemed to bestow upon it the figure of a star. Then I passed on to another consideration: whether cold is a certain

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0035
   :page: p. 13

nature, like the heat of physicians?
 For it seemed to be a mere privation, possessing neither mind, nor the power to craft a hexagon, nor any proper operation at all.

Yet lest we confuse questions, let condensation belong to cold: condensation could take place, it seems, more rightly into a spherical form.
 Indeed, if cold is considered as widely diffused and meeting the vapor only along its surface, it is more reasonable that condensation should occur in an altogether flat form, like a surface, and that of arbitrary boundaries—so that if the entire outermost surface of the vapor acquired density from the cold, weight from density, fall from weight, and shattering into fragments or thin flakes from the fall,
 surely not all flakes—indeed, very few, and I doubt whether any—would turn out six-cornered, especially with rays so neatly striated.

Those striations reminded me of what happens in vaporous steam-baths when winter's rigor besieges pierced windows. Around those narrow openings cold air and vapor wrestle together.
 Whenever they touch each other, heat seeks the upper regions and cold the lower. For in warmth there is an expansion of matter, in cold density and weight, and it drives warm things upward. Therefore, as the vapor strives to rush out in a dense stream, nature abhors a vacuum, so that cold air also rushes in thickly, whereby the rims of the open window or chink are made coldest.
 Whatever vapor drives against those rims freezes at once, and a cold equally great passes into that substance, so that whatever further vapor strikes this hoarfrost also freezes by continuous accretion—yet with the cold air insinuating itself inward along straight lines in between: by which alternation of entry and exit, those frosted formations of vapor acquire striations and sharp rays.

Nothing of this sort can be said concerning the shaping of our little snowflake. For what entry is there here, what exit, what narrow passage, what struggle in the wide-open plains of the air? I will grant that while falling from on high through vaporous air some accretion onto its tufts occurs from touching vapors.
 Yet why in six places—what principle of the sixfold number is there? Who shaped the little head into six cold horns before it fell? What cause establishes six points on that surface just about to condense, to which the six outer rays may be joined?

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0036
   :page: p. 14

Since, then, an external cause—cold—cannot bring these things about, there must be some internal cause, either attending the vapor or belonging to it in some other way.

Yet while weighing these matters, a wonder struck me: why are the rays not rather arranged around an entire sphere?
 Why, if internal heat is the author of this work, does it operate only upon a flat surface, when heat behaves alike on every side and does not reside merely in a flat surface of the vapor?

While I struggle with these reflections, while reason demands rays distributed around the entire circumference,
 there occurred to me what I have often watched with wonder on other occasions: little stars of this sort are not laid flat on a plane at the first instant of their fall, but are held aloft by certain particles, and finally after a brief delay settle down onto the plane. From that reasoning as father, and from this experience as mother, this opinion of mine was born:
 that these little stars while falling consist of three downy diameters joined crosswise at a single point, with their six tips distributed evenly in a sphere, so that they land upon three downy rays only, while the remaining three—opposite them on the same straight diameters—stand aloft until, once those on which the starlet rested bend, the hitherto raised ones sink into the same plane with the first, in the spaces between them.

I shall pursue the force of this opinion throughout, and only afterwards examine whether it is true—lest perhaps an untimely exposure of its emptiness prevent me from giving what I set out to give, a discourse on Nothing.

Let this then be laid down in the inquiry: whatever the cause of these six rays,
 it is diffused equally on all sides in every direction: so that if cold is the cause of the six rays, cold surrounds the individual portions of vapor equally, or at equal intervals on every side; but if internal heat, it too operates from one and the same center toward all regions of the sphere.

Yet thus the question is not solved, but shifted. For it is not yet clear why not five or seven, but precisely six downy rays are joined from the same center?

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0037
   :page: p. 15

And if you ask geometers in what figure three diameters cut one another orthogonally, or in the form of a double cross, at the same center, they will answer: in the octahedron, when opposite vertices are joined. For the octahedron has six vertices.
 Why then does it happen that snow, while falling and before it flattens out, rests upon three downy diameters cutting one another orthogonally—the very skeleton (*τὸ σκελετὸν*) of the octahedron—so that if you join neighboring tips of the rays with twelve straight lines, you would display the whole body of an octahedron?

What cause is there, then, that condensation takes place along these three downy radii rather than into a whole globe?

I can indeed state one way in which these things happen by material necessity; yet that assumes something which again carries more wonder than the very point that was to be demonstrated.
 Still, I shall state it, in case perhaps the truth may be drawn out by comparing many falsehoods. Let it be that vapor, when it first feels the rushing cold, curdles into little spheres of a definite quantity. This is reasonable.
 For just as in water, a drop is the natural minimum of fluid—because water no longer flows apart by its own weight when below the quantity of a drop—so it may easily be granted that there is in the matter of vapor some tenacity able to resist cold in a certain quantity, say a vaporous drop.

Secondly, let these vaporous spheres touch one another in a definite arrangement—say quadrangular in the plane, cubic in the solid, as discussed above: for thus each little sphere will be touched by six others, of which only four can be depicted here in the plane, while the fifth and sixth must be understood as placed above and below.
 These things being thus supposed and assumed, as cold rushes in through the interstices, the little spheres will be shielded against the cold from one point of contact to its opposite: therefore condensation will take place toward the centers of the little spheres, yet in such a way as also toward the diameters of contact, at which places they are safe from the cold.

Yet not without reason, as I said before, someone may ask: by what force are the little spheres thus arranged in straight ranks?

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0038
   :page: p. 16

If materially it could not happen otherwise, the matter would already be settled. Yet materially they can be arranged in two other ways, as was said above.
 And moreover all three regular orders can be jumbled together, so that a mixed arrangement results.

Or shall we adopt this as the cause of that arrangement, that in this order alone the arrangement is everywhere like itself and the points of contact are distributed equally, whereas in the others by no means so?
 For although, as was said above, individual globes are touched by twelve others, the spaces between the globes are alternately triangles and squares: here all spaces on every side are squares. There, certain two diameters of opposite contacts intersect orthogonally,
 the remaining four not likewise: here all three diameters intersect one another equally and orthogonally. There, when the ends of the diameters are joined, a cuboctahedron is formed; here, an octahedron within each little sphere.

Hence indeed the superiority of the straight over the oblique arrangement is clear; yet the cause does not yet appear that arranges the spheres in this manner rather than that. Does cold do this? But how?

For if it acts at all, it condenses or penetrates matter where the latter gaps or weakly resists. And to be generous, a vertical alignment downward could indeed be caused by a straight-line descent toward the earth; yet transversely, whence comes this alignment?

It remains, therefore, that the internal heat of the vapor brings about this cubic arrangement of the drops—if indeed their arrangement is cubic, that is, if our Nothing is Something.

To this pass having come, it is now all one whether heat disposes each drop by itself into the form of an octahedron,
 or parcels out the whole substance into an ordered array of stars and so assists the internal arrangement of individual spheres through the external array of all together. In neither case can order arise so consistently where confusion is prone to happen, as indeed is the case here.

Moreover there are arguments for rather believing that individual drops arrange themselves without the help of external contact. For if the figure of individual ones arose from order and mutual contact of many, all little stars would have to be equal to one another. Yet great difference of size is seen among them; and the ordering of many together is itself very unusual.

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0039
   :page: p. 17

Therefore we have accomplished nothing unless the manner becomes clear whereby internal heat strengthens the vaporous drop along three diameters in an octahedral—or at least six-cornered—form, so that matter gathers toward them by condensation.

Someone might think that those downy shreds float about singly and meet crosswise by chance while falling.
 Yet that is false. For they would not always meet in threes, nor at their midpoints, nor at a single point. Add that all the downy tufts turn outward away from the center—or star, or twin intersection—almost like needles on fir branches: which proves that a formative power nests at the center and distributes itself equally in all directions from there.

But perhaps the reason for three diameters is that there are just as many diameters of directions in animals? For they have upper, lower, front, back, right, and left parts. Should anyone say this, he will come near to my opinion, yet by his concession he will be drawn beyond his opinion into paradoxes.
First let him consider what the nature of this heat is, that it should build the likeness of an animal in a starlet of snow. Next let him see to whose benefit: what has an animal in common with snow? Snow, lacking life, has no need of those directions.
Thirdly, let him weigh how the parts of an animal are fitted not so much to geometric figures—and the cube, the first of solid figures—as to their archetype, as directed by a certain necessity toward achieving an end. For the first distinction, of above and below, is from place, namely the surface of the earth:
therefore the feet tend downward to brace against the weight of the body, the head is upward so as to moisten the nerves continually with fitting humor, and so that eyes and ears, farthest from the ground, may keep the greatest circumference in view with obstacles removed,
and finally so that food by its weight and drink by its moisture, falling headlong, may descend to their proper place without needing continuous attraction as in plants fixed in one spot. A second distinction, of front and back, is granted to animals for exercising motion, which advances in a straight line over the earth's surface from place to place.
Thus these two diameters necessarily intersect each other orthogonally and mark out a surface. Yet since animals cannot be surfaces, but necessarily take on bodies, a third diameter—of right and left—had to be added by reason of bodily bulk, whereby the animal becomes

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0040
   :page: p. 18

as it were twin, so that in walking too there might be an alternate distinction of mover and moved.
Therefore things that are cubic do not bear the likeness of man on account of any beauty of figure; rather, man has acquired the likeness of the cube, fitted together as it were from diverse uses as from elements.

And so, having examined everything that came to mind, my judgment is this:
the cause of the six-cornered figure in snow is none other than that of the ordered figures and constant numbers in plants. And since in these nothing happens without supreme reason—not indeed such as is discovered by discursive reasoning, but such as existed from the beginning in the Creator's design,
and from that beginning until now is preserved through the wondrous faculty of living natures—neither in snow do I believe this ordered figure exists by mere chance.

There is therefore a formative faculty in the body of the Earth, whose vehicle is vapor, as spirit is of the human soul:
so that nowhere does any vapor exist without being contained also by a formative reason—which others call the artificer heat—just as by a certain heat it was made what it is said to be, namely vapor, and by the same heat is preserved so that it continues to be so.

But by solving two objections I shall declare what remains of my opinion. For you might object that in plants the subsequent end—which is the constitution of a definite natural body—proves that a formative reason preceded in some matter: for where means are ordered to a definite end, there is order, there is no chance, there is pure mind, pure reason;
whereas in the formation of snow no end can be looked to, nor does it come about through the six-cornered figure that snow endures or becomes a definite natural body of fixed and lasting form. I reply that the formative reason acts not only for an end, but also for ornament, and aims not merely at making natural bodies, but is also wont to sport in transient things, as is clear from many examples of fossils.
The principle of all these I transfer from jest—when we say Nature plays—to this serious design: I think that the heat which hitherto shielded the matter, once overcome by surrounding cold, just as hitherto it acted in order (imbued as it is with formative reason), fought in order, so now in a certain order and flight

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0041
   :page: p. 19

composes itself, withdraws step by step, and lingers longer in those scattered branches arranged in orderly rank as if in battle array than in all the rest of the matter, and thus takes care that—as histories tell of Olympias—it may fall neither unseemly nor immodestly.

Someone else might object that individual plants have individual soul-faculties, since the bodies of individual plants also subsist separately, and therefore it is no wonder that individual figures are fitted to individual plants; whereas in snow to imagine a separate soul for every starlet is altogether ridiculous;
therefore the figures of snow must not be derived from the work of a soul as in plants.

I reply that the matter is more alike on both sides than the objector could believe. Grant that individual plants have individual faculties: yet all these are offshoots of one universal faculty residing in the earth,
which stands toward plants as the faculty of water toward fishes, that of the human body toward lice, of a dog toward fleas, of sheep toward another kind of vermin. For not all plants come from seed; most arose at first spontaneously (*ἐξ αὐτομάτου*), even though they propagate themselves afterward.
For the faculty of the earth, which in itself is one and the same, divides itself into bodies and with bodies, grows into them, and builds one thing and another according to the internal or external condition of each matter.
So in vapor too, which as a whole the soul possessed as a whole, it is no wonder if, when cold endeavors the division of a continuous whole, by reason of the contraction of the parts the soul occupies itself around the parts themselves, as around single wholes, in shaping them.

O truly dead life without philosophy! For had that adulteress of Aesop's fable known of this formative faculty in snow, she could have persuaded her husband that she had conceived from snow, and would not so easily have been deprived of her bastard by her husband's cunning.

I have spoken of the author of the figure; it remains to inquire into the figure itself, whether it arises from the crosswise intersection of three diameters—which has hitherto been among our assumptions—or whether it is hexagonal from its very origin, of which later. Now let us press on along the path we have begun.
The reason, therefore, why this faculty rather imitates the arrangement of the octahedron's angles may be this. First, the entire genus of souls is akin to geometric and regular—or world-shaping—figures: which

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0042
   :page: p. 20

can be proved by many testimonies.
For since souls are certain likenesses of God the Creator, surely in the mind of God the Creator abides the truth of these figures, coeternal with God. Moreover, since it is most certain that souls themselves in their inmost essence receive quantities—whether without physical matter or with it, I do not argue—
it is fitting that they receive figured rather than unformed quantities; and if figured, why not regular, solid figures, since souls belong not to surfaces but to solid bodies.

Now among regular solid figures the first is the cube, the firstborn, parent of the rest.
Its mate, as it were, is the octahedron, having as many corners as the cube has faces, and their centers, to each of which corresponds an angle from the octahedron.

A small portion of vaporous matter, therefore, when about to be shaped, if it must receive a figure—which we have already shown to be reasonable—
will first lay hold of the cube and its companion the octahedron. Toward this, indeed, material necessity also hinted earlier—that of equal globes packed into one heap. For the rudiments of a cube and an octahedron were blended and foreshadowed in the points of contact.
But why the figure of an octahedron rather than a cube? Is it because the cube is the figure of expansion, the octahedron of contraction? And now both matter and heat-force are being gathered together, beset hostilely by the cold.
And whence is it certain that the former is the figure of expansion, the latter of contraction? Namely because the eight corners by which the former spreads outward are the very same that surround the center within the latter in equal number.
For if you take away from a cube its eight corners, cut off with equal edges, and join them together inward, you will plainly construct an octahedron. Moreover, the cube spreads into more angles, namely eight; the octahedron into fewer, namely six.

Jewelers say that natural octahedra are found in diamonds, of most perfect and polished form. If that is so, it greatly confirms my view: for the living faculty which in the earth imparted to the diamond the form of an octahedron, drawn from the innermost bosom of its nature,
that same faculty, marching forth with vapor from the earth, has imparted the very same figure to snow formed from that vapor.

Still, as far as the crosswise intersection of three diameters is concerned, the form of an octahedron is no more present in it than that of a cube.
In the former the corners,

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0043
   :page: p. 21

in the latter the centers of the faces are joined by three diameters of this kind; in the former the angles of the diameters at the center, in the latter the angle that bounds the body is expressed.
In vain, therefore, do we trouble ourselves over the choice of the whole figure, where there is at least a rudiment of either.

Yet whither am I swept away, fool that I am, who while striving to give almost Nothing, almost achieve Nothing as well: since out of this almost-Nothing I have fashioned almost the World itself, in which all things are contained;
and though above I shrank from the little soul of the tiniest animalcule, now I display the soul of that thrice-greatest Animal, the globe of the earth, in an atom of snow?

Therefore I shall retrace my steps, and take diligent care that what I gave and what I spoke may become nothing.
And this will come to pass if, as swiftly as my little snowflake melts, so swiftly I rout and annihilate those petty reasonings with opposing petty reasonings.

For while I am writing these things, it has snowed again, and more thickly than recently. I observed the little bodies of snow intently: all fell rayed, then, yet of two kinds:
some were very tiny, with rays planted all around in no fixed number and simple, without down, without striations, and exceedingly slender, yet gathered at the center into a fairly large little globe; and of these was the greatest part.
Scattered among them, however, were the rarer six-cornered starlets of the second kind, and not one of them was anything but flat, neither as it drifted nor as it fell, with its tufts also laid out in the same plane as its stalk.
Yet a seventh tiny ray pointed downward underneath, like a kind of root, upon which as they fell they rested, and by it were held aloft for a time: a thing that did not escape me earlier, though I misconstrued it, as though three diameters were not in the same plane.
Therefore no less what I have said hitherto than what I have unsaid comes as close as possible to Nothing.

The first, lumpy kind I judge to arise from vapor already almost abandoned by heat and on the very point of condensing into watery drops.
Accordingly they are both round and achieve no fair figure, already forsaken by the architect, and they are rayed on every side by those very principles which were applied above to our study of frosted crusts upon windows.

In the second kind, however, which is that of the starlets,
there is no place for contemplating the cube or octahedron, nor any contact of drops: since

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0044
   :page: p. 22

they fall flat, not—as I supposed above—intersected by three diameters.

Even if, then, the formative soul keeps its place here as well, and remains among the causes, still the question concerning the choice of figure must be renewed. First, why flat? Or was I wrong above to take plane figures away from the formative powers of bodies?
For in all blossoms there is a plane pentagon, not a solid dodecahedron. Here then the cause of a plane figure would truly be this: that cold touches the warm vapor along some plane, and does not surround the whole vapor so equally when starlets are born as when lumps fall.

But why six-cornered? Is it because among regular figures this is the first truly plane one, and one that never folds together with itself into any solid body? For the triangle, square, and pentagon compose solids. Or because the hexagon tiles a plane with empty space excluded?
Yet the triangle and quadrangle do the same! Or because among those that tile a plane with empty space excluded, this is nearest to the circle? Or is there this distinction between the faculty that shapes sterile things and that other which shapes fertile ones, that the former makes triangles or hexagons, the latter pentagons?
Or finally, does the nature of this formative power in the inmost bosom of its essence partake of the hexagon?

Of the five causes adduced, the first, second, and third assume this: that the formative faculty takes counsel from the occasion and draws up its battle-line according to the fitness of the field:
so that since the battle of warm vapor and cold air takes place in a plane, not through solid bulk, it also chooses a figure that belongs to planes rather than to solids. And so in the second and third it would have regard to material necessity as well.
For the first cause rests solely on the property of the hexagon, looking to the fitting congruity of this figure to this battle. In a plane battle, therefore, a plane figure is necessary—yet not necessarily a figure such as cannot unite with itself into any solid,
but for this reason alone, that just as figures that enclose a solid correspond to physical bodies, so to planes corresponds a figure that does not enclose a solid: here formal fitness is looked to, not material necessity.

Yet in the second and third it would have to be said that the hexagon is chosen by the formative power even from material necessity, namely so that nothing empty may be left,

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0045
   :page: p. 23

and so that the gathering of vapor into the consistency of snow may proceed more conveniently.

For in a circle it would happen most conveniently; yet because little circles leave empty spaces, therefore a figure closer to the circle is chosen. But against this cause there has already been set above the inequality of the starlets, some of which are exceedingly tiny, with very slender and simple rays, without down.
Which proves that no large plane of vapor gathers into snow all at once, but separately the tiniest little planes, one after another, and those unequal. Therefore the consideration of excluding empty space has no place here, since it reigns only in the unbroken division of a surface into equal hexagons.
Thus it will come about that the second and third causes are struck from the count, unless insofar as they can be reduced to the first: that the formative faculty chooses the hexagon, compelled by no necessity of matter or spaces,
but solely by this innate fitness, that elsewhere the hexagon builds a plane with empty space excluded and is, among the figures that can do the same, closest to a circle.

The fourth cause indeed cannot stand thus bare. For white lilies are fashioned with three and six petals, and they are not sterile;
in like manner the cups of nearly all wild flowers. Unless perhaps there is this distinction: that beneath a five-petaled flower there grows a fleshy fruit, as in apples and pears, or a pulpy one, as in the rose and cucumbers, with the seeds hidden inside flesh or pulp;
whereas beneath a six-cornered flower nothing grows except seed in a dry chamber, and the fruit is as it were inside the flower. Or perhaps there is this distinction, that no six-cornered flower occurs on trees and shrubs, but on herbs and chiefly bulbous plants.
Or let another examine the juices themselves, whether there is any difference in them according to the figures of the flowers.

The matter is not yet ascertained by me; therefore let it suffice to have lightly cautioned others concerning this fourth cause.

In favor of the fifth cause speak other works of this formative faculty,
such as rock crystals, all six-cornered, whereas octahedral diamonds are most rare. Yet the formative faculty of the earth does not embrace a single figure, being versed in all geometry and practiced therein. For I saw at Dresden, in the royal house called the Stable, a table adorned with silver-bearing ore,
from which there sprouted as it were a dodecahedron of the small size of a hazelnut, projecting halfway out. There is also extant in the description of the baths of Boll

.. folio:: strena_sexangula:0046
   :page: p. 24

the forepart of an icosahedron among the fossils.
Therefore it is likely that this formative faculty becomes diverse according to diverse humor. In vitriol the cubic rhombic figure is frequent; in niter it has its own figure. Let the chemists tell, then, whether there is anything of salt in snow, and what kind of salt, and what figure that salt puts on elsewhere.
For I, having knocked at the doors of Chemistry, since I see how much remains to be told before the cause of the matter can be reached, would rather hear from you, most discerning Sir, what you think, than weary myself further with discoursing.

Nothing follows.

THE END.
