Chapter XVI: The Moon's Orb & Celestial Matter
==============================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0065
   :page: p. 53

Because adding the tiny Lunar sphere (scarcely 3 parts in 60 of Earth's eccentricity) dramatically improved the agreement of Mars, Jupiter, and Mercury in Chapter XV's table, Kepler devotes Chapter XVI to two frank questions: **Does the Moon's orb belong inside the thickness of Earth's shell?** And **what is the physical nature—or immateriality—of the five regular solids and planetary orbs?**

With disarming scientific honesty, Kepler declares that he will follow whichever treatment matches observed planetary numbers more closely: if including the Moon yields closer agreement with Copernicus, then Earth's *Orbis Magnus* encompasses the Lunar circuit; if excluding it fits better, then half of the Moon's small epicycle simply protrudes alternately above and below Earth's sphere without interfering with the Dodecahedron or Icosahedron.

This immediately raises the question of physical collision: how can solid crystalline spheres coexist with inscribed polyhedra, or allow comets and Lunar protrusions to pass through? Kepler cuts clean through medieval solid-orb physics—citing :person:`Tycho Brahe's <brahe>` demonstration from the great comet of 1577 that solid impenetrable celestial spheres do not exist. The **five regular solids have no material substance whatsoever**; they are purely **archetypal geometric intervals** (*mensurae*, immaterial blueprints in the Creator's mind) prescribing the distances at which free planets move through fluid celestial ether. Thus there is no collision between matter and geometry.
