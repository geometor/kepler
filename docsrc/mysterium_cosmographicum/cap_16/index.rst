Chapter XVI: The Moon's Orb & Celestial Matter
==============================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0065
   :page: p. 53

*A special warning concerning the Moon, and on the matter of the bodies and orbs.*

Not a small scruple, therefore, does the Moon's orb raise, however small it may be. Wherefore it is time now to say something further concerning the Moon.
And I begin indeed without circumlocution to lay open my mind to you sincerely, Reader: that in this cause I shall follow wherever the nearness of the numbers leads the way. So if the interposition of the Moon renders the numbers and arcs of :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>` more truly, I shall say that system must be reckoned into the thickness of the Great Orb.
But if, with the Moon cast out, we can agree better with :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>`, I too shall say that the Great Orb is not so thick all around as to cover the Lunar heaven, but that sometimes upward, sometimes downward, a whole hemisphere of the Moon projects above or below the margins of the Great Orb, and sometimes—indeed for the most part—less than a hemisphere stands out;
altogether according as the body itself

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0066
   :page: p. 54

of the Earth, which is the center of the Moon's orb, has either ascended or descended through the thickness of its own orb. Nor, by Hercules, do I know to which side cosmographical or even metaphysical reasons incline the more.
It seems indeed a fitting arrangement that there should not be in the heaven some orb bearing such a knot, like a ring with a gem whose prominence prevents the orb's roundness from remaining utterly perfect. Yet conversely, in reckoning the figure of an orb, what need is there to take account of the Moon, when it does not properly belong to the Earth's orb as the wanderings of the other planets upward and downward
(which physically are most conveniently demonstrated by epicycles)—as, I say, these epicycles belong each to its own orb? For it is the Earth to which that third orb from the Sun is owed; the Earth itself by its own oarage goes round the Sun among the other planets; by itself and through its own epicycles, using no service of the Moon for this, it accomplishes its variations, as the teachings of :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>` show;
whereas the Moon holds this tiny little cottage about the Earth as if by favor or on lease; the Moon follows, or rather is dragged along, whithersoever the Earth proceeds in whatever variation. Imagine the Earth standing still: the Moon will never find a path around the Sun, much less go round it. For she runs to and fro, shut within narrow spaces around the Earth, attendant of light and humors to the Earth,
just like some hall-steward about his master, or like those who pace up and down on a ship, yet do not advance on their journey by tiring themselves, unless a great force of waters carries them forward, uncertain whither they are going and even when they sit still. And just as the Moon has received her space and motion from the terrestrial orb, so too has she obtained many conditions of the terrestrial globe,
namely continents, seas, mountains, air, or things in some way corresponding to these, as :person:`Mästlin <maestlin>` proves by many conjectures, nor do I lack some of my own; so that even on this account alone :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>` is more likely, who bestows the same fellowship of place and motion upon these two bodies. And surely the man-loving (φιλάνθρωπος) Creator seems last of all to have clothed the Earth
with this Lunar orb because He willed to assign to her a seat like the seat of the Sun: so that if she too were the center of some orb (as the Sun is the center of all), she might be regarded as a kind of Sun, on account of which she was commonly held, as it were, to be the common center of the whole universe.

Altogether, to play once more with an allegory, man is as it were a God in the world, and his dwelling is the Earth; just as of God, if He have anything

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0067
   :page: p. 55

bodily, it is surely the Sun, that unapproachable light. As therefore man ought to correspond to God, so ought the Earth to the Sun.
A proof of this is nearly the same proportion of the globe of the Earth to the orb of the Moon as of the Solar globe to the mean elongation of Mercury from the Sun.

Nor indeed need it be feared that the lunar orbs, compressed by the neighboring proportions of the bodies, should be crushed if they are not hidden and enclosed within the orb itself.
For it is absurd and monstrous to place in the heaven these bodies clothed in some matter which would not grant passage to another body. Certainly many are not afraid to doubt whether there are in the heaven any such adamantine orbs at all, or whether by a certain divine virtue—the understanding of geometric proportions governing their courses—the stars are carried freely through the ethereal fields and air without those fetters of the orbs.
Surely no weight will cause doubtful and faltering steps in the mover so that it ever wanders out of its circle. For no point, no center is heavy. Rather, all things of the same nature as a body follow its center. Nor does a center acquire weight from this, that it draws other things to itself or is sought by them: no more than a magnet grows heavier while it is actually drawing iron.
Or this very Earth, which we altogether hold with :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>` to be carried along—by what bars, by what chains, by what heavenly adamant is it fastened into its orb? By that very air, namely, which all men round about on the surface of the Earth breathe (fermented and mingled with vapors): which we penetrate with our hand, with our body, yet do not cleave apart or push aside,
since it is the vehicle of celestial influences into the middle bodies. For this is the heaven in which we live, and move, and have our being—we and all mundane bodies. Still, what need of so many words? For even if the little orb of the Moon protrudes above the Earth's orb, what is there in the Dodecahedron or the Icosahedron that would forbid it passage?
You saw above in Chapter XI that where the plane of the Zodiac cuts these two bodies, no angle and no center of a face meets it, but from the section there arises on both sides a decagon whose perpendicular falling from the center to a side is far greater in the Dodecahedron than the radius of the inscribed sphere, and far shorter in the Icosahedron than the radius of the circumscribed sphere;
and so long indeed that not only that little heaven of the Moon, but something far greater projecting above the orb, could pass through that middle way and between those decagons.

Yet however all these matters be left in their place, the case fares none the worse. For you see that through the interposition of the Moon, except in Venus, we approach as closely as possible to the arc numbers set forth through the sines of :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>`.
