Chapter XI: Orientation of the Solids & Origin of the Zodiac
============================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0046
   :page: p. 36

In these chapters I shall have the physicists hostile against me, because I have deduced the natural properties of the planets from immaterial things and mathematical figures, and furthermore dare to investigate even the origins of the circles from the bare imagination of certain sections. To this I wish to reply in a few words: that God the Creator, since He is Mind, and does what He wills, is not hindered from looking, in adapting forces and marking out circles, to things that subsist either without matter or in imagination.

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0047
   :page: p. 37

And since He wills nothing except with the highest reason, and nothing has come into existence apart from His will: let our Adversaries therefore tell what other reasons God could have had for adapting forces, and the like, when besides quantities there was nothing? And if, while they find nothing, they take refuge in the unsearchable powers of the Creative Wisdom: let them keep to themselves this moderation in inquiry, and enjoy it with a reputation for piety; yet let them suffer us to render probable causes from quantities, provided only that we say nothing unworthy of so great a Maker. Bound therefore by no scruple, I proceed to the investigation of the Zodiac.

And at the outset I judge that no more probable position of the bodies can be devised than when the Cube, the greatest of the figures, is inserted into its orb in any manner whatsoever, for in a circle there is no beginning. Moreover, we must establish beginnings without a reason, lest there be an infinite regress, and so that at last we may have a passage from infinite potentiality to finite act. Now therefore let one of the faces be reckoned as a base. The Pyramid, then, which is to be inserted into the cube by the mediation of the Jovian orb, ought to hold its base parallel to the base of the cube, and the Dodecahedron to the base of the pyramid. Otherwise do the properties of the secondary bodies behave, as we have seen. The Icosahedron must therefore be set upright within the Dodecahedron in such a way that its diagonal becomes perpendicular to both opposite bases of the dodecahedron at their centers. In the same way the Octahedron, the least of the figures, will have to be suspended within the Icosahedron, so that a straight line drawn shall pass: (1) through the center of a base in the cube, (2) through the center of a tetrahedral base, (3) through the center of a Dodecahedral pentagon, (4) through an angle of the Icosahedron, (5) through an angle of the octahedron, (6) through the center of the world and the solar body, and onward at like intervals through the opposite (7) angle of the Octahedron, (8) angle of the Icosahedron, (9) center of a Dodecahedral plane, (10) angle of the Tetrahedron, and (11) center of a cubic plane. For the sake of greater light I refer you to the plate of the second chapter, where all the bodies are represented in this manner. With these things thus established, not only will the square appearing in the Octahedron, equidistant from the said two angles, if produced round about, divide all the figures, and indeed the whole world, into two equal halves; but also the mutual intersections of all the sides which one may reckon as lying midway between the said angles and centers—of all of them, I say, if they be regularly placed—as they

appear to one looking out from the center, lie in the same continued plane of the octahedral square. And this appears especially in the many-faced bodies as kindred ones; for the said sides of the remaining bodies cannot be placed congruently together.
