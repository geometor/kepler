Chapter XII: The Division of the Zodiac, and Aspects
====================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0048
   :page: p. 38

Many have taken the division of the Zodiac into twelve fixed signs for a human invention—such a one, indeed, as has no natural reality underlying it. For they suppose that these portions (*moria*) do not differ in natural powers or properties, but were

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0049
   :page: p. 39

adopted because of the fitness of the number for ratios. Although I do not wholly reject their view, nevertheless, lest anything be rashly discarded, I shall set forth from the same principles a cause for this division, to which it will not be unlikely that the Creator adapted those properties (if they possess any distinct ones).

What the subject of numbers is, we have seen above. And surely, apart from quantity, or what resembles quantity endowed with some potentiality, there is nothing numerable in the whole universe except God, who is the most venerable Trinity itself. Now therefore we have sectioned all the bodies through the Zodiac. Let us see whether by this section the Zodiac itself has gained or undergone anything. When the bodies are cut in the manner stated, the resulting face of the Cube from the section will be a square, as also that of the Octahedron; that of the Pyramid, a triangle; and those of the remaining two, decagons. Therefore, when inscribed in a circle at the same point, the square, the triangle, and the decagon mark off various arcs on the circumference, all of which are measured by a portion no greater than one one-hundred-and-twentieth part of the whole circle. Natural, therefore, is the division of the Zodiac into 120 parts from the regular arrangement of the bodies among the spheres; and since its triple is 360, we see that this division does not rest altogether upon no reason. Now if we again describe the square and the triangle separately from the same point, the smallest portion of the circle will be the twelfth part of the circuit—namely, a Sign. So it is remarkable how both the monthly motion of the Sun and Moon and the great conjunctions of the Superior planets square so aptly with the portions that are marked off by the triangle and the square belonging to those very bodies.

And further, how highly this twelvefold division is prized by nature, take from an outside example: so that even though the cause be not wholly known, an occasion may lie open for thinking ever more nobly of these five figures.

Let there be proposed some string, and its sound *Gamma ut*. Then as many notes occur from *Gamma* up to the octave that are consonant with *Gamma*, so many times, and no more often, can you divide the string rationally so that the divided parts of the string are consonant both with one another and with the whole. Moreover, how many notes of this kind occur, the ears indicate; I shall declare it by diagram and numbers.

See now both the harmonies themselves and the proportions of the strings in numbers: where the lowest note signifies the pitch of the whole string; the highest, the pitch of the shorter part; the middle, the pitch of the longer part; the lowest number indicates into how many parts the string is to be divided; and the remaining numbers give the lengths of the parts.

And these alone appear to me to be natural tones, for the reason that they possess an indisputable number. The remaining tones cannot be expressed by a fixed proportion to those already laid down. For you will draw forth one *F fa ut* downward from *C sol fa ut*, and another upward from *B mi* flat, however much these two may seem to be perfect fifths. But to the matter in hand. The first and second concords are in a way companions; so too the fifth and sixth. For since all of these are imperfect, two of them—one major (*dura*), the other minor (*mollis*)—always conspire together, so that they are in a certain manner equated to single perfect concords. Nor do they have widely differing divisions. For :math:`\frac{1}{6}` and :math:`\frac{1}{5}` stand to each other as :math:`\frac{5}{30}` and :math:`\frac{6}{30}`, which differ by only one thirtieth. Likewise :math:`\frac{3}{8}` and :math:`\frac{2}{5}` stand to each other as :math:`\frac{15}{40}` and :math:`\frac{16}{40}`. They differ, therefore, by only one fortieth particle.

And thus, properly speaking, we have only five concords in Music, matching the number of the five bodies. And if you seek the least common dividend of the seven divisions into 6, 5, 4, 3, 8, 5, 2, you will again find 120, as above when we were treating of the division of the Zodiac; whereas the least dividend of the perfect concords is again 12—plainly as though the perfect concords proceeded from the square and triangle of the Cube, Tetrahedron, and Octahedron, and the imperfect ones from the decagon of the

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0051
   :page: p. 41

remaining two bodies. And this is the second kinship of the bodies with the Musical concords. Yet because we are ignorant of the causes of this kinship, it is difficult to fit the individual harmonies to the individual bodies.

We do indeed see two orders of harmonies—three simple perfect ones and two double imperfect ones—just as there are three primary bodies and two secondary ones; yet since the rest do not agree, this reconciliation must be abandoned and another tried. Namely, just as the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron with their decagon earlier increased the twelvefold number up to 120, so here the imperfect harmonies do the very same.

Therefore the perfect harmonies will have to be adapted to the Cube, Pyramid, and Octahedron, and the imperfect ones to the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron. To this is added something else, which indeed points an index finger toward the most hidden cause of these things, of which we shall treat in the next chapter: namely, that there are **two treasures of Geometry**—the one, **the ratio of the hypotenuse to the legs in a right triangle** (:just:`I.47`); the other, **the line cut in extreme and mean ratio** (:term:`sectio divina <sectio-divina>`, :just:`II.11`, :just:`VI.30`). From the former flows the construction of the **Cube**, **Pyramid**, and **Octahedron**; from the latter, the construction of the **Dodecahedron** and **Icosahedron**. Hence comes the so easy and regular inscription of the Pyramid in the Cube, and of the Octahedron in either, just as of the Dodecahedron in the Icosahedron. Yet how the individual harmonies are to be adapted to the individual bodies is not so readily at hand. This alone is clear: that to the Pyramid is owed the harmony which men call the fifth—the fourth in order—because in it the smaller portion is :math:`\frac{1}{3}` of the whole part, just as the side of the triangle (which the Pyramid uses) subtends :math:`\frac{1}{3}` of the circle. More things below will confirm this, when we treat of the aspects; and that we may understand them here as well, let us think of it altogether as if the string were not a straight line, but a circle. The division of the said harmony will therefore yield a triangle, in which an angle is opposed to a side, plainly as in the Pyramid an angle is opposed to a plane. There remain, then, for the Cube and Octahedron those called the octave and the fourth—the third and seventh in order. But which of them will hold which harmony? Shall we say that the secondary bodies receive those which trace lines, and the primary ones those which trace figures? Then to the Cube will be owed the so-called fourth. For if you make a circle out of the string, and from one fourth draw a straight line to another, and so on until

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0052
   :page: p. 42

you return to the first point, a quadrangle will be formed, which plane figure the Cube also obtains. Conversely, to the Octahedron will be owed the octave, which is of half the string. For in a circle, a line drawn to the half and back to the same point makes nothing but a straight line. Thus to the Dodecahedron will be owed the first double imperfect harmony. For chords drawn across fifths and sixths of the circle make a pentagon and a hexagon. There will remain, then, for the Icosahedron the latter double imperfect harmony, because chords drawn across two fifths repeatedly back to the same point make only lines, †; and likewise chords drawn across three eighths, \*. Or do we prefer to give the fourth to the Octahedron, because it subtends a quarter of the circle twelve times—which no side of the Cube does? Thus the octave, the most perfect harmony, will be left to the Cube, just as it is itself the most perfect body. Perhaps it is also more fitting to leave to the Icosahedron the first imperfect harmony on account of the hexagon, which is more akin to a triangular base than to a pentagonal one; and to give to the Dodecahedron the eightfold division on account of the cubic number 8, because a cube is inscribable in a dodecahedron. Let these questions surely remain open until someone discovers their causes.

Let us come now to the aspects. And since we have just made a circle out of the string, it is easy to see how the three perfect harmonies can be most beautifully compared with the three perfect aspects—namely with opposition (☍), trine (△), and quartile (□). While the first imperfect harmony, *B mollis*, is to a hair's breadth like the sextile, whose mark is ⚹, and which they report to be the weakest.

We have the cause (such as Ptolemy certainly did not give) why planets distant by one or five signs are not reckoned to be in aspect. For as we have seen, Nature recognizes no such concord among tones. And since in the rest the principle of influence and of harmonies is the same, it is credible that it is the same here too. The cause on both sides is beyond doubt the same, and arises from the five bodies—which I leave for others to seek out. Since, then, all four harmonies agree with their aspects, and yet three harmonies still remain in Music, I have sometimes suspected that it ought not to be neglected in judgments of nativities if Planets stand 72, or 144, or 135 degrees apart—especially since I see one of the imperfect harmonies has its own aspect. Yet to any sharp-eyed observer of atmospheric phenomena it will easily become clear whether there is any power in these three rays, since the other aspects

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0053
   :page: p. 43

are confirmed by weather changes through the most constant experience. The reasons which one might plausibly give why :math:`\frac{3}{8}`, :math:`\frac{1}{5}`, and :math:`\frac{2}{5}` sound in a string yet do not operate in the Zodiac may be these:

1. Opposition alone, two quartiles, and a trine with a sextile each complete a semicircle; whereas these three rays have no companion for this office which Music does not altogether repudiate.

2. The remaining rays have an easy ratio from the diameter; the side of the pentagon, the chord subtending two sides of the pentagon, and three of the octagon stand in a more remote degree and are irrational.

3. Because a trine with a sextile, and a quartile with a quartile, form a right angle; the remaining rays do so in no way with any received line.

4. The imperfect *B mollis* is in a manner perfect, because it uses the same division as the perfect ones and is half of a fifth. Hence it is no wonder that it alone among the imperfect harmonies answers to an aspect—namely the sextile, which is likewise half of a trine. For the rest are neither fitted into the twelvefold division nor are parts of any perfect one.

5. Finally, six angles of an equilateral triangle, four of a square, three of a hexagon, and two spaces bounded by two semicircles fill the entire space in a plane. Whereas three angles in a pentagon are less than four right angles, and four are greater. Hence it is also clear why neither the octangular ray, nor the dodecangular ray, nor any of the rest operates. And here I almost separate the causes of the aspects from the causes of the concords. For surely the reasoning drawn from angles is the genuine one for the rays, since the effect arises on account of the angle made at a point on the terrestrial surface, not on account of a figure described in the circle of the Zodiac, which consists in imagination rather than in the truth of things. But the division of a string neither takes place in a circle nor uses angles, but is accomplished in a plane along a straight line. Nevertheless, both concords and aspects may have something in common, because the same things cause both, as was said above. That, however, I leave to the diligence of others to investigate. Ptolemy's *Harmonics*, which Regiomontanus was going to publish with Porphyry's commentary—though Cardanus asserts it has not yet been printed—doubtless deals with this matter. See also what can be brought to bear here from Euclid's *Sectio Canonis* (*Musica*).
