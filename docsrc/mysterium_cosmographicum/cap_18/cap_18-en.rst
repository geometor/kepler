Chapter XVIII: Observational Errors in Copernicus & Astronomy's Precision
=========================================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0070
   :page: p. 58

Facing the small angular discrepancies tabulated at the end of Chapter XV—$-41'$ for Saturn, $-6'$ for Jupiter, $+20'$ (or $+30'$) for Mars, $-2^\circ 18'$ for Venus, and $-61'$ for Mercury—Kepler launches a searching methodological critique of the **limits of observational precision** in ancient and Renaissance astronomy. Should the five-solid hypothesis be rejected because it fails to agree *ad unguem* (to the exact fingernail) with :person:`Copernicus's <copernicus>` printed parameters?

No, Kepler argues, because Copernicus himself built *De revolutionibus* largely upon coarse Ptolemaic observations supplemented by only a handful of his own instruments—never claiming sub-minute perfection. Drawing on :person:`Rheticus's <rheticus>` testimony and correspondence (:cite:`1540 / 1596 <rheticus:1540>`), Kepler recalls that Copernicus openly acknowledged his tables might stray by a sixth or quarter of a degree ($10'$–$15'$), and confessed that Mercury remained almost wholly unexamined because northern mists hid it near the horizon.

Kepler enumerates the systematic uncertainties still afflicting sixteenth-century planetary tables: atmospheric refraction near the horizon, instrument graduation errors, reliance on unverified ancient star catalogs, and Copernicus's assumption that planetary eccentricities stayed anchored to the mean sun rather than the true solar body. Had Copernicus known of the five-solid proportion, Kepler asks the reader, what pains would he not have taken to reconcile observations with the polyhedra? Urging patience until :person:`Michael Mästlin <maestlin>` and contemporary observers (above all :person:`Tycho Brahe <brahe>`) deliver refined measurements, Kepler requests a stay of judgment (*litis dilationem*) rather than premature dismissal.
