Chapter XIX: Planet-by-Planet Residual Discrepancies
====================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0077
   :page: p. 65

Moving from general observational limits to each planet individually, Kepler examines the residual discrepancies of **Saturn**, **Jupiter**, **Mars**, **Venus**, and **Mercury** one by one, incorporating a detailed critical evaluation supplied by his mentor :person:`Michael Mästlin <maestlin>`:

* **Saturn**: Because Saturn stands at an immense distance from the Sun, even a large radial adjustment alters its geocentric *prosthaphaeresis* by no more than $41'$—well inside errors revealed during the very winter preceding publication.
* **Jupiter**: Already agrees with the Tetrahedral gap to within a mere $6'$—virtually exact to sixteenth-century naked-eye observation.
* **Mars**: Its residual drops dramatically once the Moon's orb is accounted for and once Copernicus's mean-sun eccentricity is shifted toward the true physical Sun.
* **Venus & Mercury**: Here the sharpest tensions remain—especially Venus's $-2^\circ 18'$ discrepancy with the Icosahedron. Kepler quotes :person:`Mästlin's <maestlin>` incisive diagnosis (folio ``0081``, p. 69): Copernicus made the sweeping postulate (*De revolutionibus* V.30) that the relative sizes of planetary circles had remained unchanged since :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`, even though the diminishing eccentricity of Earth's *Orbis Magnus* mathematically demanded recalculating the interior circles from fresh observations rather than coarse Ptolemaic sightings!

Closing the polyhedral verification section, Mästlin congratulates Kepler warmly—echoing Copernicus's encouragement to Rheticus—expressing confidence that this ingenious discovery will soon illuminate the remaining perplexities tormenting astronomers.
