Chapter XIX: Summary
====================

Individual Planetary Residuals & Mästlin's Critique of Copernicus
-----------------------------------------------------------------

Moving from the general observational limits of Chapter XVIII to each planet individually, Kepler examines the residual discrepancies of **Saturn**, **Jupiter**, **Mars**, **Venus**, and **Mercury**, incorporating a detailed letter from his mentor :person:`Michael Mästlin <maestlin>`:

* **Saturn**: Because Saturn stands at an immense distance from the Sun, a large radial correction alters its *prosthaphaeresis* by no more than $41'$—comparable to a $37'$ discrepancy observed during the very winter preceding composition (November 2/12, 1594, when Saturn appeared between the neck and heart of Leo where calculation had placed it on October 21/31).
* **Jupiter**: Agrees with the Tetrahedral interval to within less than a sixth of a degree ($10'$), leaving nothing reasonably to be desired.
* **Mars**: Its half-degree ($30'$) surplus is modest compared with real observational scatter: as Mästlin testifies in the preface to his 1577 *Ephemeris*, Mars's errors from calculation cannot even be confined within two degrees.
* **Venus & Mercury**: Measured via greatest elongation near the horizon, both inferior planets suffer from atmospheric refraction ("physical parallax" and dense vapors, as Mästlin notes in Thesis 58 of his *Disputatio de Eclipsibus*), while Copernicus leaned heavily on :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` and :person:`Rheticus's <rheticus>` rule of minimal alteration. For Venus, the Copernican arc lies midway between the Icosahedral values obtained with and without the Moon's orb included in the *Orbis Magnus*. For Mercury (whose $1^\circ$ discrepancy is smaller than Venus's), Kepler urges astronomers first to refine Earth-Moon and eclipse parameters.
* **Mästlin's Letter (pp. 68–69)**: Closing the polyhedral verification section, Kepler quotes Mästlin's incisive diagnosis of Mercury's apogee across Ptolemy, Copernicus (*De revolutionibus* V.30), and Reinhold's *Prutenic Tables*: Copernicus made the sweeping postulate that planetary circle ratios remained unchanged since Ptolemy, ignoring the diminished terrestrial eccentricity and relying on coarse whole-degree (*partiles*) Ptolemaic sightings. Echoing Copernicus's encouragement to Rheticus, Mästlin congratulates Kepler warmly on his discovery.
