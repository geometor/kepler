Chapter XX: Proportion of Orbital Periods to Solar Distances
============================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0081
   :page: p. 69

*What the proportion of the motions is to the orbs.*

And thus far indeed has been dispatched that argument by which I judge the greatest strength is brought to the renewed hypotheses; and it has been demonstrated that the distances (*apostemata*) of the orbs in the hypotheses of :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>` observe the proportion of the five regular solids. Let us now see whether by another argument also, drawn from the motions, both the new hypotheses and these very Copernican dimensions of the orbs can be confirmed, and whether in the proportion of the motions to the distances a surer ratio may be had from Copernicus than from the customary hypotheses. In which undertaking, while I construct amplitudes of the orbs very close to the Copernican ones out of the well-known periodic times of the motions, look favorably, gracious Urania, upon this fairest endeavor: it is your own honor that is now at stake.

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0082
   :page: p. 70

First, all astronomers desire that the farther any orb is from the center, the slower the motion with which it proceeds. For nothing is more agreeable to reason, as :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` bears witness (*On the Heavens*, Book II, Chapter 10), when he says that the motions of each body take place in ratio to their distances. In which place, although the Philosopher adduces another reason foreign to our purpose—namely, the hindrance caused by the counter-rush of the swiftest First Mobile—yet by that other reason he fights on my side thus far, and by his whole opinion against :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`, as well as against himself. For it pleases him that equality of motion comes from the movers into all the orbs, while inequality of return is caused by the orbs themselves: so that every particle of Saturn is indeed just as swift as the lowest sphere of the Moon, by an equal force of motion, yet it happens to Saturn that, having obtained a wider space while being no swifter than the rest, it returns more slowly. And this humbler equality the Philosopher could not secure in the tradition of the ancients, because they were compelled to assign equal returns to three Planets of unequal orbs—the Sun, Venus, and Mercury—and thus always to make a higher one swifter in its orb than a lower. In Copernicus, at first blush, such a proportion presents itself. For of the six moving orbs, whichever is narrower always returns sooner: Mercury's course is three months, Venus's seven and a half months, the Earth's a year, Mars's two years, Jupiter's twelve, and Saturn's thirty years. Yet if you reduce this to calculation, so that whatever ratio Saturn's motion has to the circuit of its orb, or to its distance (for the ratio of circles is the same as that of their radii), you make the same ratio of each of the remaining motions to its own orb, you will discover that a simple proportion of this kind has no place. Look closely for me here at the mean motions—sufficiently well known, and that long before Copernicus thought about any definite ratio of distances—look, I say, at the very same diversity that exists among the distances themselves, whether deduced by Copernicus from the prosthaphaereses or by me from the five solids: smallest on either side around Mars, then around Mercury, Jupiter, and the Earth, and greatest around Venus; nearly equal on either side around Jupiter and Mercury, and likewise around the Earth and Venus. Already, therefore, Copernicus's victory over the old world is right at once well enough proven.

If, however, we wish to draw still closer to the truth and hope for any equality of proportions, one of two things must be laid down: either the Moving Souls, the farther they are from the Sun, are so much the feebler; or there is one moving soul in the center of all the orbs—namely, in the Sun—which, the closer any body is to it, urges it on the more vehemently, while in the farther bodies, because of the lengthening of distance and the attenuation of its power, it grows as it were faint. Just as, therefore, the fountain of Light is in the Sun, and the origin of the circle is at the Sun's place—namely, at the center—so now life, motion, and the soul of the world fall back into that same Sun: so that rest belongs to the fixed stars, the secondary acts of motion to the Planets, and the primary act itself to the Sun, which is incomparably nobler than secondary acts in all things, just as the Sun itself

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0084
   :page: p. 72

far surpasses all other things in beauty of form, in efficacy of power, and in splendor of light. Here far more rightly belong to the Sun those noble epithets—Heart of the world, King, Emperor of the stars, Visible God, and the rest. But the nobility of this subject requires quite another time and place, and already appears clearly enough beforehand from the *Narratio* of :person:`Rheticus <rheticus>`.

Now, however, we must reflect upon the manner of establishing this sought-for proportion. Above it was seen that if the amplitude of the orb alone acted to increase the periodic time, the difference of the motions and of the mean distances would be the very same—namely, whatever the proportion of Mercury's 88 periodic days is to Venus's 225 days, the same would be the ratio of the radius of Mercury's orb to Venus's. Now, however, the feebleness of the moving soul in the farther body mingles itself with this proportion of motions. Let us examine, then, how it stands with this feebleness. Let us suppose—what is exceedingly likely—that motion is dispensed from the Sun by the same ratio as light. And in what proportion the weakening of light sent forth from a center comes about, the Writers on Optics teach. For as much light as is in a small circle, just so much light or solar rays is also in a great circle. Hence, since it is more crowded in a small circle and thinner in a great one, the measure of this attenuation must be sought from the very proportion of the circles, and that both in light and in motive power. Therefore, by how much Venus is wider than Mercury, by so much is the latter's motion stronger—or swifter, or nimbler, or more vigorous, by whatever word one pleases to express the thing—than the former's. Yet by how much one orb is wider than another, by so much more time does it also require for its circuit, even if the force of motion were equal on both sides. Hence it therefore follows that one greater distance of a Planet from the Sun tells twice toward increasing its period; and conversely, that the excess of the period is double the difference of the distances (*apostemata*).

Half of the excess, therefore, added to the lesser period, ought to display the true proportion of the distances: so that the sum stands as the distance of the upper Planet, while the simple lesser period represents the distance of the lower—that is, of its own Planet—in the same measure. Example: Mercury's periodic motion is about 88 days, Venus's about 224 and two-thirds; the difference is 136 and two-thirds, and its half is 68 and a third. Joined with 88, this makes 156 and a third. Therefore, as 88 is to 156

Closer, as you see, have we come to the truth. Yet although I doubt whether that practice of the divided difference has attained in every respect, by demonstrative method, what the theorem had set out to do, nevertheless another method of reckoning—by which I come back to the very same numbers—bids me believe that there is not nothing at all hidden in these numbers. For since it is probable that the strength of motion is in proportion with the distances, this too will be probable: that whichever Planet surpasses a higher one in strength of motion, is by so much surpassed in distance. Let both the distance and the power of Mars, for instance, be unity. Then by whatever tiny part of Martian power the Earth is stronger than Mars, that entire tiny part of Mars's distance it will lose. This is easily done by the rule of false position: for I set the Earth's radius to Mars's as 694 to 1000. Therefore, I say, if the amplitude of the circle denoted by 1000 is traversed by the Martian motive force in 687 days, the smaller circle denoted by 694 will be traversed by the same Martian force in 477 days. Now since it is certain that the Earth's circuit is not 477, but 365 days, I proceed by the inverse rule thus: 477 days would be spent by the simple Martian force; how much of the Martian force does a circuit of 365 and a quarter days consume over the same perimeter which Mars would complete in 477 days? For there is no doubt that a stronger force is required than Mars's. There comes out, therefore, above the whole Martian force, a further :math:`\frac{306}{1000}` part of that same force. And by so much is the Earth stronger than Mars: it ought therefore to be by so much closer to the Sun—namely, if Mars has receded 1000 from the Sun (for the distance of the upper planet is always a whole unit), the Earth will be closer by 306 of those parts; and when 306 is subtracted from the in-

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0086
   :page: p. 74

ferior 1000, the number posited at the outset—namely 694—must come out, if that supposition was true; whereas if it were false, you would work according to the precepts of the rule and elicit the true supposition.

You see that by this second theorem there come out no other numbers than the former ones: whence it is certain that both theorems differ indeed in form, yet in reality coincide and rest upon the very same foundation—though in what manner that comes about, I have hitherto never been able to trace out.
