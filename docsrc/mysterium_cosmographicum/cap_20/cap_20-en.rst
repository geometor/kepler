Chapter XX: Proportion of Orbital Periods to Solar Distances
============================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0081
   :page: p. 69

Having completed the static polyhedral argument, Kepler opens an epoch-making new line of inquiry—invoking Urania (*fave facilis Uranie*) as he inaugurates **celestial dynamics**: **What mathematical proportion connects the orbital periods of the planets to the radii of their spheres?**

In :person:`Copernicus's <copernicus>` system, outer planets take far longer to complete a circuit than inner ones—not merely in direct proportion to the larger circumference they must traverse, but **doubly slower**: Saturn's orbit is roughly ten times Earth's radius, yet its period is nearly thirty years rather than ten! Why? Kepler proposes a bold physical choice: either each planet possesses a weaker moving soul the farther it stands from the center, or—far more unified and physically luminous—**one single moving soul (*una anima motrix*) resides in the central Sun**, driving every planet around it with an impulse that **attenuates with distance**, just as light weakens outward from its source.

Thus an outer planet takes longer for **two compounded reasons**:
1. It must travel a **longer circular path** (proportional to distance $r$).
2. It is propelled by a **weaker solar motive virtue** (inversely diminishing with distance $r$).

Testing the simplest linear attenuation law in 1596—where the excess of period over circumference scales proportionally, yielding his provisional formula $T_2 / T_1 \approx (r_2 / r_1)^2$ approximated via arithmetic mean interpolation—Kepler computes "motive distances" (*distantiae motoriae*) purely from the well-known sidereal periods (Mercury 88d, Venus 224.7d, Earth 365.25d, Mars 687d, Jupiter 4332.6d, Saturn 10759.2d). Though his 1596 formula overshoots slightly—it would take twenty-two more years of labor before he discovered the exact :term:`sesquialterate Third Law <lex-tertia>` $T^2 \propto a^3$ on May 15, 1618—Chapter XX marks the birth of modern astrophysics: the **Sun as the physical dynamical engine of the planetary system**.
