Chapter III: Primary and Secondary Solids & Earth's Station
===========================================================

.. folio:: mysterium_cosmographicum:0036
   :page: p. 26

*That these five solids are distinguished into two orders; and that the Earth is rightly stationed.*

Furthermore, it might seem a mere accident, flowing from no cause, that the six orbs of :person:`Copernicus <copernicus>` admit these :term:`five regular solids <corpora-quinque-regularia>` within the distances of one from another, were it not for that very order among them in which I have placed each one between. For if Saturn were as close to Jupiter as Venus is to Earth, and conversely if these two were separated from each other in Copernicus by as great an interval as separates Jupiter and Mars, another order would have had to be used in inserting the solids: between the first two orbs, in the first place, would stand the Dodecahedron or Icosahedron, and in the fourth place the Tetrahedron.
And since that order cannot be admitted by mathematical reasoning, it would be easy to lay bare the futility of a contrived theorem. Now let us see by what reasons it is proved that the solids had to be arranged between the orbs in this very order. First, these solids are divided into three primary ones—the Cube, Tetrahedron, and Dodecahedron—and two secondary ones—the Octahedron and Icosahedron. That this distinction is altogether true, note the properties of each kind: 1. The primary solids differ from one another in their plane faces; the secondary use the same triangular face. 2. Each of the primary solids has a face of its own—the cube a square, the Pyramid a triangle, the Dodecahedron a pentagon—whereas the secondary borrow the triangular face from the Pyramid.
3. All the primary solids use a simple angle, namely one contained by three planes; the secondary admit four or five planes into one solid angle. 4. The primary owe their origin and properties to nothing else; the secondary have obtained most of theirs from the primary by interchange, as though begotten from them. 5. The primary cannot be turned gracefully unless an axis is drawn through the centers of one or opposite planes; the secondary, when an axis is drawn through opposite vertices. 6. It is proper to the primary to stand, to the secondary to hang; for whether you roll the latter onto a base or set the former up on a vertex, the eye will shrink from the deformity of either aspect.
7. Add finally that the primary are three, a perfect number, while the se-

condary are two, an imperfect number; and that the former possess every species of angle—the Cube a right angle, the Pyramid an acute, the Dodecahedron an obtuse—whereas both the latter dwell solely in the kind of the obtuse. Indeed, the angle of the Octahedron ranges through all three species: obtuse at the junction of its sides, right between two opposite meeting edges, and acute in the solid vertex itself. Since, therefore, the distinction between the solids was manifest, nothing more fitting could have come to pass than that our Earth—sum and compendium of the whole world, and thus most worthy of the moving stars—should divide between those orders by its orb, and obtain the station which we assigned to it above.
