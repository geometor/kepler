**CHAPTER VI.**

*What kinship the Aspects bear to Musical Consonances in Number and in its causes.*

On what occasions the effective Configurations were discovered and their number increased is not the business of this place to recount, for that belongs to Astrology; and I dealt with that matter twelve years ago in my book *On the New Star and the Fiery Trigon*, Chapters VIII, IX, and X, where I not only displayed the immense gulf between the Aspects and the remaining

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0241
   :page: p. 151

fabrications—truly astrological vanities—but also, as I believe, solidly refuted the philosophical arguments of Count Giovanni Pico della Mirandola brought against this part of Astrology too, in so far as they needed to be resolved and answered. And when nine years ago Helisaeus Roeslin, M.D. and a philosopher of no small renown, devoted himself to the old Astrology and undertook in a book published in the German tongue to attack this new philosophy, while another physician, Philipp Feselius, went to the opposite extreme and assailed all chapters of Astrology indiscriminately, including the doctrine of configurations,
I stood up against both of them by publishing two German booklets, the first titled *Response to the Objections of Roeslin*, and the second *The Third Man Intervening* (*Tertius Interveniens*). In the latter I defended the truth of the Aspects, while in the former I maintained the mode of causation by which the Aspects are effective. Accordingly learned men testified in letters written to me that now at last astrologers are being taught a purer philosophy by me. In all these booklets I mentioned the kinship that holds between musical consonances and the Aspects; yet in the first I was still uncertain about the number of the Aspects,
counting certain spurious—or at least weak—ones among the chief Aspects while wholly passing over some stronger ones. In the German booklets, however, I began to uncover these defects in my own inquiry, which I recently thought fit to repeat with a somewhat fuller exposition—as the occasion demanded—in the Prolegomena to the *Ephemerides*, folios 33, 34, 35, and 36. Whatever could not be set out more amply there on account of the brevity we had set ourselves, I shall now supply here as the order of the argument invites.

The Axiom which I had adopted in the year 1606 and undertook to examine and refute in the cited passage of the *Ephemerides* was this:
*that God the Creator either took the laws for ordering the Aspects from the Harmonies of Song below the Octave described in Book III, or else tuned human ears—the judges of those Concordances—to the heavenly Aspects.* If this Axiom were true in every respect, there would have to be just as many Aspects in number as there are Concordances up to the Diapason. For the Sextile answers to the minor Third, the Quintile to the major Third, the Square to the Fourth (*Diatessaron*), the Trine to the Fifth (*Diapente*), the Sesquadrus to the minor Sixth, the Biquintile to the major Sixth, and the Opposition to the Octave (*Diapason*):
because if you remove from the whole string the same fraction that any Aspect cuts off from the circle, the remainder of the string forms with the whole that very Consonance assigned here to each Aspect. Since therefore that Sevenfold number of Concordances—or rather of harmonic Sections, each followed by its single Concordance below the Octave—is certain and demonstrative, no less than the Fivefold number of Regular solids in Geometry (as appeared in Book III, Chapter II), the method of demonstrating the number of Aspects would have been easy and brisk by accepting this Axiom, nor would there have been any need for this laborious apparatus of new Axioms in Book IV.

And indeed, if meteorological observations had concurred in every point with this sevenfold group of Aspects,
I would have rested content with the axiom stated above, nor would I have been greatly troubled by what might be objected from the origin of harmonic ratios and the consideration of a metaphysical cause. Yet because it has repeatedly been found that sublunary Nature is also aroused by the Semisextile, which intercepts one twelfth of the circle—even though when one twelfth is taken from a string, the remainder of

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0242
   :page: p. 152

eleven parts is not consonant with the Whole—and because the stirring of storms by the Sesquadrus (which marks off three eighths of the circle) has been found unreliable, even though when three eighths are taken from a string the remainder of five eighths is wholly consonant with the whole of eight:
hence there arose for me the necessity of probing more deeply in this Fourth Book into the causes of this disagreement and divergence. Although I believe this has been properly accomplished and the clearest causes unearthed, it will not be amiss to press home once again the sum of the entire inquiry, since the exposition undertaken here of what was stated more concisely in the Preamble to the *Ephemerides* requires it.

The reason, therefore, why *there are not as many Aspects as there are Harmonic sections in Music, as I had believed up to the year 1608, is this: that Music draws certain principles of its sevenfold number from the very straightness of the string, whereas the circle in which we mark the Aspects returns into itself, nor can another circle be made from the Remainder of the Zodiac as another string can be made from the Remainder of a string.*
Those are the words of the aforementioned *Prolegomena*, folio 35, which are to be explained thus. As was stated in Chapter IV of this Book, Harmonic sections arise in one way and Aspects in another, even though both arise from the same circle. For in Book III the Axioms are so framed that any string and any part of a string, whether long or short, can again be compared with the whole circle, no less than the whole or longest string was compared with that same circle; whereas in this Fourth Book an arc of a circle, whether greater than a semicircle or less, could not be compared with the whole.
I shall put it more clearly: in however many ways a circle is divided demonstrably, all those ways can be transferred to a straight line—that is, to a string—and to any part of it whatever; but a demonstrative division of the whole circle, say into three or into five, cannot on that account also be transferred to any arbitrary arc of the circle, as has been sufficiently proved in the closing propositions of the First Book. The cause lies in the figure itself: for a straight line remains a straight line whether it is cut short or extended, whereas a truncated circle does not remain a circle. There is therefore a proportional division of any two straight lines, and likewise of any two circles, but not of any two arbitrary arcs of one circle.
These truths apply to our purpose as follows. If a string be divided into eight parts—namely by a demonstrative division of the circle—one eighth is consonant with the whole, and three eighths are likewise consonant, for the reason that one eighth of the circle can be cut off demonstrably, as can three eighths. That very same demonstrative determination is also among the causes why the Octile and Trioctile are effective. Yet here lies the difference: in the string a harmonic SECTION is not yet established unless the Remainder too—seven eighths and five eighths—be consonant both with the whole of eight parts and with the cut-off parts, 7 with 1 and 5 with 3.
Now the demonstrative determination of 1 part and of 3 parts does not confer consonance upon their remainders 7 and 5: for 5 indeed is consonant, whereas 7 is dissonant. Thus the division of the whole 8 into 5 and 3 is harmonic, while into 7 and 1 it is not harmonic. Whence, then, does the Remainder 5 obtain its consonance with the whole 8, and the Remainder 7 its dissonance from it? Precisely from this: that a circle divided into 5 has a chord subtending two fifths that is demonstrative; whence a string divided into five (like the part here proposed from the string 8) is consonant with a part 2 portions long, and therefore also with

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0243
   :page: p. 153

4 and 8, which stand in continuous double proportion with 2.
By contrast, a circle divided into 7 does not yield a demonstrative chord of one seventh part, so that Remainder of the string which has 7 is dissonant from 1, and thus also from 2, 4, and 8. Behold how the harmonic division of the whole 8 into 3 and 5 derives one consonance only from the Octagon, and another from the Pentagon! Indeed it derives a third from yet another figure, namely the Decagon: for the part 3 would not be consonant with the Remainder 5 unless it were first consonant with its double 10—and that because when the circle is divided into 10, the chord subtending three tenths is demonstrative. Yet this coupling of several figures has no place in a circle and its parts.
For if you first inscribe a regular Octagon in the whole circle, even supposing you could divide the arc holding three eighths into five (although it is impossible to do so demonstrably), certainly no Pentagon could be inscribed in it at the divisions made—for it would turn out both most irregular and rather a hexagon, since an arc already has two endpoints before its division. Since therefore there is an immense difference between Remainders—of a string on the one hand, and of a circle on the other—it is plain that unless we advance from the circle to a straight line as well, no account of a Remainder can be taken. Now an Aspect is an angle whose measure is not any straight line,
but an arc of a circle described from the meeting-point of the rays: therefore the circle does not measure an Aspect, nor form it, nor render it effective by reason of its Remainder, but solely by reason of the arc intercepted by the Aspect. If not by reason of the Remainder, then not by reason of the harmonic section of a string arising from that division of the circle—for a harmonic section cannot be defined without a Remainder. And thus that axiom is overthrown which held that the harmonic proportions of song are the causes of the Aspects. Nor does it follow: the Trioctile or Sesquadrus answers to the harmonic Section that generates the minor third, and it is effective; therefore its efficacy comes from the harmonic section of the circle as such.
What is true, however, is this: *that Harmonies have a great kinship with the Aspects, and both kinds share the same origin from noble figures inscribable in a circle*—as the said *Prolegomena* put it—meaning that the demonstrative knowability of the chord subtending three eighths of a circle stands among the elements from which both a harmonic section in Music and an effective Aspect in Physics are constituted. Yet I speak here on the hypothesis that knowability of the figure alone would suffice for efficacy, just as it suffices for the simple Consonance of a Part with the Whole. For if we deal accurately, there is also this difference: in Consonances Knowability carries the chief weight, whereas in Aspects the Congruence of figures holds first place; and the Semisquare and Sesquadrus are effective not only because the side of the Octagon and its star are knowable, but also and most of all because the Octagon and its star are congruent figures.

This indeed is the reason why the Dodecagon especially generates a potent Aspect, yet does not generate a harmonic section—that is, a triple Consonance—although it generates a simple one. For the Remainder 11, when one twelfth is removed, blocks a harmonic section, yet does not block the efficacy of the twelfth part. But of this I shall say more presently.

For in order that a true, mathematical, and causal comparison of Concordances with Aspects may stand, that Axiom must be wholly overturned,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0244
   :page: p. 154

since it is not only insufficient but diametrically contrary to the truth. Indeed no Aspect properly corresponds to any smaller concordance—except the Opposition to the Diapason—but each corresponds to the larger concordances, the companions of the smaller ones, from the triad of each section.
That is, Aspects are defined by the same Segments of the circle as the larger concordances, whereas the smaller concordances are defined by the Remainders of the circle. For example, the Trine Aspect does not answer to the Diapente, but to the Diapason plus Diapente (twelfth); the Square does not answer to the Diatessaron, but to the Disdiapason (double octave); the Quintile not to the major Third, but to the interval compounded of this and the Disdiapason; the Sextile not to the minor Third, but to the Disdiapason plus Diapente; the Biquintile not to the major Sixth, as we supposed above, but to the interval compounded of a major Third and a Diapason; the Sesquadrus not to the minor Sixth, but to the interval compounded of a Diatessaron and a Diapason—as is plain from the same proportion on both sides of the part to the Whole.

In this correspondence, therefore, no limit to the Aspects would appear, since the larger concordances are infinite; and those who rely on this sole reason to establish the Semisquare Aspect—because, forsooth, it answers to the Trisdiapason (triple octave)—have no excuse for refusing to admit also the Decile, the Tridecile, the Vigintile, and very many others which they nevertheless reject; for these too answer to their own larger concordances, which pile on one further Octave every time the division of the circle doubles the number of parts.

What is it, then, that sets a boundary to the number of Aspects? Why is no Semisquare or Octile, no Decile or Tridecile introduced except only after the chief ones? Why is the Sesquadrus, ennobled by its musical kinship, either omitted or held cheap, while the Semisextile, a foreigner in Music, is not only admitted but even paraded among the first?
Because it is not Music that forms the Aspects, but Geometry that forms both kinds—yet under one set of laws for Music, and another for the Aspects. For both what is Harmonic in Music and what is effective in Meteors springs from a noble figure holding singular privileges in Geometry. Yet Meteorology and Music are like two distinct nations sprung from the same fatherland, Geometry: one of them—namely the Harmonic proportions of Book III—acknowledged the circle as its fatherland and owed its origin to it no less than the Aspects do here, yet went forth as it were out of the circle, founded a colony of its own, and propagated itself living under its own laws; whereas the Aspects remain within their fatherland the circle and use no laws other than those which the roundness of the circle prescribes to them, drawn from Regular Congruent plane figures inscribed in a circle.

For in Music that seven-man senate (*septemviratus*) of sections is constituted out of certain Marriages, with women also counted in the census. For example, the chord subtending three Eighths—or the Octagonal star—is indeed one of the citizens in Geometry and of the class of regular figures, yet is not of the foremost nobility. In Music, however, its arc (three Eighths of the circle) obtained a seat because it was born of a marriage in which a plebeian woman named Remainder (namely three Quarters of the circle) had been wedded to a Patrician (namely one Quarter of the circle), whose nobility in Geometry comes from the Square. For from this mother (three

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0245
   :page: p. 155

quarters) by musical generation (by the addition of one Diapason) was born the Part three eighths; who, without impairing his senatorial honor, is now allowed to wed another plebeian woman named five eighths, whose birth stands on the same footing—for her mother too is a Remainder, namely four fifths, and her patrician father is one fifth, whose Geometric nobility comes from the Pentagon.

On the other hand, the twelfth part of the circle—although it holds the right of citizenship in Music, since its chord is of the foremost nobility in Geometry, both won by its own virtue (through Congruence) and inherited from its parents (for the Hexagon and Triangle are noble figures, from the doubling of whose sides arises the Dodecagon, surpassing them by its expressible area)—nevertheless, because this twelfth part has as wife the Remainder of eleven twelfths, who in Music and therefore in Geometry is of foreign extraction, tracing her lineage to the Hendecagon, an undemonstrable figure, and on that account cannot obtain the right of citizenship, her husband therefore holds no right to a seat in the sevenfold senate in Music for determining the number of divisions of the monochord.

By contrast, in Meteorology another custom prevails. For according as each individual is himself noble either by birth or by merit (that is, by Knowability or Congruence), so does he prevail in authority, while the rest flit about like shadows: no account is taken of wives.
Here, then, the Octile (or Semisquare) and the Sesquadrus, their musical right set aside because their chords are ignoble, belong to the common people, whose power is nothing unless magistrates are absent under the pressure of urgent business—that is, if primary Aspects were lacking for a long time, perhaps these Aspects too would accomplish something, especially if the Earth is full of moisture, in which case it sometimes unburdens itself without any prompting of configurations at all. Yet because primary Aspects are usually close at hand, sublunary Nature, wearied by them, does not feel these lesser promptings.

The Decile and Tridecile, on the other hand, spring indeed from an illustrious family—the tenfold division of the circle, which employs the divine proportion—yet they have not shed luster on their lineage by deeds (since they do not form a solid congruence in every respect), nor are they heads of their house; and if they can do anything at all (being congruent to some extent in the plane, and even with other figures in the solid), all of that is snatched away from them by the nobles of other houses, or their glory is eclipsed by a brighter splendor.
For if Nature has been sufficiently exercised by the Aspect of 30 degrees and the Earth sufficiently drained by it, little is left for the weaker neighboring Aspect of 36 degrees to work upon.

Finally, the Semisextile of 30 degrees possesses an outstanding nobility from the Dodecagon, which is both Knowable and eminently Congruent; nor does the foreign marriage which barred its way to public honors in Music hinder it in Meteorology in the slightest degree. As for the figures that follow after this Dodecagon—both in the three distinct families (Tetragonal, Trigonal, and Pentagonal) and in the Quindenary mixture—besides the fact that all of them are already of lower nobility, they have absolutely no merits of their own (of Congruence), and therefore by the inviolable law of the commonwealth they are debarred from honors and from the power of constituting Aspects.

From these considerations appears what I said in the *Prolegomena* to the *Ephemerides*: *that diverse causes and their concurrence carry weight in the constitution of Aspects, and that Nature makes her choice among those endowed with the greatest number of prerogatives;*
*and that the Semisextile shares certain rights with the Square*—namely the expressibility of the area in its figure—*and certain others with the Sextile*, namely varied congruence in the plane; *with which combined forces the Semisextile turns out in a manner more potent than the Sextile itself*, inasmuch as the expressibility of the dodecagonal area outweighs the expressibility of the hexagonal side, because a more perfect Congruence in the plane follows the former. Yet in this Fourth Book I have placed the Semisextile after the Sextile, though in the same rank.

The *Prolegomena* to the *Ephemerides* also make the Trine, the Square, the Quintile, and the Biquintile *equal in the first and strongest cause*—namely pure Congruence in the solid, for constructing the Regular solid figures; while I said that *the Trine and the Square add a second cause, not much lighter*—understand pure Congruence of figures in the plane. This indeed is shared by the Sextile as well; yet the Sextile does not add it to the first cause, but possesses it apart from the first.
In like manner *the Square is said to add a third cause as well*, namely the expressibility of its plane area—not that the Semisextile does not also share in this, but because the causes that stand singly in the others are accumulated together in the Square, making it the most powerful of all.

So too, in gathering the votes for the Sextile, I said that *it shares a certain nobility in common with the Opposition*, hinting at the expressibility of the side of its figure, because it is half the diameter of the circle. Again, *it shares another nobility in common with the Trine and the Square*, namely pure Congruence in the plane, of which we spoke before. For *only a small portion of this*—that is, Congruence with figures of other species—*belongs to the Semisextile and the less noble ones*. Within this portion of Congruence, however, *the figures of the Semisextile and the Octile hold the first rank* (to which the star of the Semisextile could also be joined),
because the Congruence they initiate can be continued without mixing diverse forms; *the Quintile and Biquintile hold the second rank*—except that they possess a nobler congruence in the solid—*and likewise the Trioctile, Decile, and Tridecile*, because their figures do indeed continue Congruence, yet not without a mixture of diverse forms; and all these are congruent in some degree in the solid too, according to more and less, save for the Dodecagonal star of the *Quincunx*, which is therefore relegated to the *Third rank*, to which the *Sesquadrus* (or Octagonal star) may also be added because of the obscurity of its knowability.

I did indeed extend the ranks even further to the *Vigintile and Quindecile*—that is, to the figures of Fifteen and Twenty angles—to which I gave the *Fourth rank* (in the said portion of congruence alone), the reason for which is contained in our Proposition III of this Book; while to their *stars* the *Fifth rank* was assigned, because in Knowability they equal their primitive figures, yet in Congruence they lag very far behind, since it touches only their individual angles. Yet there was no need for me to do so, since not even up to the third rank are thoroughly indubitable aspects produced, and we can fittingly set a boundary to the proliferation of Aspects at solid Congruence and at the perfect Expressibility of side or plane. Wherefore let the degrees of nobility of the Figures rather be sought from the ends of Books I and II, from which the ranks of the Aspects have been distinguished in this Book.
