Chapter IX: Staves, Clefs, Note Shapes, and the Musical Scale
=============================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0140
   :page: p. 47bis

**Chapter IX.**

*On the diagram [staff], that is, the modern notation of strings or voices by lines, letters of the alphabet, and notes; and on the System.*

This matter is not without dispute; yet I follow necessary reasons. The musicians of our age depict strings in one way in lute tablatures, and in another in vocal scores. In the former, individual lines of the diagram denote individual strings, each of which yields different sounds according to different stops when plucked; in printed vocal music, however, not only do single lines denote single voices or strings, but the very spaces between two lines also signify an intermediate string.

In this manner four transverse lines with three spaces denote seven strings, and five lines with four spaces denote nine strings. Next, they express the voices of the melodious System either by special notes, each in its own place on the diagram—which notes at the same time indicate the measure of time—or by the first seven letters of the alphabet, or finally by the six syllables *ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la*, or, as the Belgians do today, by these seven: *Bo, ce, di, ga, lo, ma, ni*.

For since there are two genera of song, hard and soft, whose difference when begun from one common lowest voice is that the third and sixth in hard song are one diesis higher than in soft, the third and sixth strings cannot remain fixed in both kinds of song, but must either be doubled in instruments and organs—as was done in numbers at the end of Chapter VII—or varied by stopping, as I believe was done of old on the lyre and panpipes. The same must be said of the diagram and the human voice. For since a single diagram is made for octaves of both genera rising from the same root, and in it there are only eight places, serving as indices of eight sounds,

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0141
   :page: p. 51

while from both genera of song ten principal strings are heaped together into one octave, by Chapter VII, and thirteen of every variety, by Chapter VIII: it comes about that two sounds differing by a diesis or a limma are drawn in the same place on the diagram, whether that be a line or an interlinear space; because dieses and limmata are not sung in succession like tones and semitones—for which reason I also refused, with some writers, to call them minor semitones. For this reason, therefore, certain signs had to be prefixed to the lines and spaces, the images of strings, from which hard song might be distinguished from soft, and the place of the semitone—whether the ordinary one or the others cut off from major tones—might be established. There is also another more hidden necessity, not from the diversity of the genera, but in any song whatever, from the inequality of the major and minor tones, so that they may be distinguished against one another on diagrams. For the syllables *Ut, Re, Mi* must necessarily be general and applicable equally to a major and to a minor tone; hence, however much these names satisfy those learning to sing and practical organists, to theorists they give no satisfaction at all. From this office, then, these letters, when prefixed to diagrams, are wont to be called *Claves* [clefs/keys], because without them no entrance into the diagram lies open to the singer.

The same letters are also applied to musical organs: for they are inscribed on the handles of the jacks in stringed instruments, or of the valves in wind instruments, from which inscription those handles are by a private right called *Claves* [keys], and the row itself, or System of all the handles, *Claviarium*, *das Clavier*, and a kind of instrument *Clavichordium* [clavichord], because the strings are struck by keys—that is, handles pressed down outside while jacks leap up inside.

Moreover, there are diagrams commonly made for organists in which, instead of black lines, there are rows of letters indicating which keys ought to be touched; here the letters stand in place of the notes which vocal diagrams used, set upon lines or spaces.

Concerning these letters, some pleasant observations come to us. First, not all letters are placed upon their own lines and spaces, but only these: *F, G, C*, whenever the place of one of them is upon a line, and *B* even when it has its place in a space.

Second, the letter *C* has a far different sign, such as is pictured here at the side: I think this sign arose from a distortion of the old writing of the letter *C*. For because scribes used broad nibs of reed pens, most notes in rapid writing were made square, nor could a round *C* be drawn with those pens; therefore they made *C* out of three little strokes, one thin in place of the hollow, and two thick in place of the horns, drawing the pen sideways thus *[square C]*. Soon the thin little stroke, as the pen hurried on, was mostly made longer and protruding on both sides beyond the horns, thus *[C with closed horns]*. And so that the horns might be bounded, they drew little lines parallel to the first, thus *[C clef]*. And so at last out of two such little lines one was made, similar and parallel to the first; and the whole sign became such as *[double C clef]*, which, like the notes themselves upon the lines, becomes hollow through the gap of the pen, thus *[hollow C clef]*.

Yet this also occurs to me: whether this sign may not have arisen from the use of the word *Scala Musica* [musical scale], since it bears the likeness of a ladder [*scala*], as if marking the beginning

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0142
   :page: p. 52

of the musical scale wherever it was placed. Or is it truer that first the figure of a ladder was made from the letter *C*, and afterward from the frequent use of this figure what was properly a diagram began to be called a scale?

When, however, there is no place for the letter *C* on a line (since formerly there were for the most part only four lines), then there is a place for the letter *F* on one of the lines; this is written in the higher voices with its genuine mark, and in the Bass by a curved semicircle with two dots beside it: which sign I think was originally, before it was distorted, the Greek minuscule gamma :math:`\gamma`, so that the two added dots signify a Digamma, because our *F* is the double :math:`\Gamma` of the Greeks. For Vincenzo Galilei bears witness, bringing forward the most ancient examples of Greek melodies, that this method of marking the sounds of the System by letters of the alphabet flowed down from the Greeks. And it is acknowledged that the simple Greek :math:`\Gamma` also found a place in our Latin music, as will shortly be told.

This *F*, therefore, is the second marked clef; and both are marked side by side wherever convenient places occur for each. The third is *G*, which is expressed in the higher voices when its place is on a line. By ancient custom it is also marked on the lowest line of the diagram. For the musical scale today begins from the voice :math:`\Gamma` *ut*, or *Gammut*, which indicates the lowest pitch *G*: so that Digamma and Gamma are the same as *F* and *G*.

Moreover, *b* is drawn in addition on the diagram of soft song to distinguish the genus of the song, whether it falls on a line or in a space: its presence signifies soft song, its absence hard song. Yet not only this, but even on other lines or spaces which are not assigned to the key *b*, the letter *b* is nevertheless found and by a certain abuse is repeated as the sign of a semitone or of the syllable *FA*.

On the contrary, when extraordinarily a semitone is set in place of a tone, and the syllable *Mi* in place of the syllable *Fa*, then before the note is placed the letter *♮*, or a sign derived from it. For the ancients doubtless drew *♮* thus, whereas for it we draw *♯* or *※*, which Galilei thinks wishes to say to the reader the same thing as the ancient Greek word *diaschisma* [διασχίσμα]; for it clearly enough expresses a splitting, and indicates to us the cuttings off of semitones, of which we treated in the preceding Chapter VIII.

On the keyboards of stringed instruments we do not commit this ambiguity with the letter *b* out of its proper place. For first, all keys or handles of those sounds which are constituted by cutting off semitones from a tone, and which increase the eightfold number of the octave System, are raised—that is, standing above the principal flat handles—and black in color; second, upon each black key is inscribed the letter of the neighboring key, but with a tail for distinction (except *♮*, in place of which a simple black *b* key is put), and the tail signifies a higher voice, as *f, f♯, g, g♯, b, ♮, c, c♯, d, d♯*; yet between *f* and *f♯*, *g* and *g♯*, *c* and *c♯* is a limma, and between *b* and *♮* a diesis—that is, between the same letters, just as their two voices are also expressed in a single place on the diagram.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0143
   :page: p. 53

Between *d* and *de*, however, is a semitone; so that these two letters, although the same, are nevertheless not ascribed to the same place, but to successive ones. A diesis, however, is between *de* and *e*, and these different letters fall into the same place: which diversity must be granted to ancient custom and to the usage of softening [*molliendæ*]. Finally, between *fe* and *g*, *g* and *ge*, *a* and *ab*, *♮* and *c*, *d* and *de*, *e* and *f*, are semitones. Therefore caution must be exercised here, lest by some analogy from the same writing we presume anything concerning the identity of an interval.

This also is especially wonderful, and likewise to be pardoned to ancient custom: that on some clavichords and organs (in which octaves are continued), when because of the identity of the consonance of the octave the same letters are rightly and deservedly repeated, this nevertheless does not happen beginning from the first *A*, but from the second *b*. For this is the order of characters: *C, D, E, F, G, A, b, c, d, e, f, g, a, bb, cc, dd, ee, ff, gg, aa, bbb, ccc, ddd, eee, fff, ggg, aaa, &c.*

Furthermore, what a diagram is on paper, this a *System* is on an instrument: namely, the series of all strings dividing one consonant interval, which, as was said in Chapter V, first of all belongs to the interval of the octave, and from it to all larger intervals, as many as any instrument embraces.

And although it is not my purpose to dwell long on the music of the ancients, since it is full of obscurity, I have nevertheless judged that the names should not be passed over by which they designated the eight sounds in the System of one octave; for that naming has a kinship with the letters of which we have treated thus far.

Thus the ancients in the time of Aristotle numbered the strings as follows: *Hypate, Parhypate, Lichanos, Mese, Paramese, Trite, Paranete, Nete*. *Hypate* was the lowest voice, and seems to have been so called from its position on the strung instrument when applied for playing; for it holds the same position to this day on the lute, pandora, and cithara.

And since Aristotle affirms that between *Hypate* and *Parhypate* there intervened a *diesis* (that is, the Platonic semitone, which, when two major tones were subtracted from a fourth, left :math:`243 : 256`, which he called a *diesis*), therefore *Hypate* was the same string that we mark with the letter *A*, and *Parhypate* is that which the letter *b* marks. Therefore *Lichanos* (so called from the index finger) is *c*. *Mese*, however, is *d*; and it was called by this name because it was the middle among seven, at the time when there were only seven strings on the psaltery and the first was plucked in place of the octave. *Paramese* has the letter *de* or *e*; the more ancient omitted this one. *Trite* has the letter *f*; it was so called because it was third in order from the highest, just as we call it the sixth from the low *A*. *Paranete* is marked with the letter *g*. And *Nete*, as if it were the last to be touched by the plectrum drawn downward, returns to the letter *a*. This distribution is also confirmed by the fact that a fifth is said to have intervened between *Nete* and *Mese*.

Such a System of eight strings, however, could not serve either genus of song unless either by the touch of a finger *Parhypate*

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0144
   :page: p. 54

*b* and *Paramese* *de* were shortened so as to become *♮* and *e*, or were tightened by tuning pegs in hard song, or slackened in soft song. And on this occasion was born the distinction of pitches into fixed and movable [*stabiles & mobiles*], and the very name of *diesis* [διέσεως], from the slackening of movable strings.

In the composition of several octave Systems, the method of the ancients was more intricate; yet this must be observed, that they assumed one lower than *Hypate* itself, which for this very reason was called *Proslambanomenos*. A trace of this survives in the musical scale which our boys learn by heart, which assumes below the Latin letter *A* another letter signifying a lower sound, for it marks that extra-ordinary pitch with the Greek letter :math:`\Gamma`: for thus the scale begins, *Gammut, Are, &c.*

Let it suffice to have touched upon these matters concerning the ancient names of strings in this place. For below in Chapter XI all fifteen strings of the composite System will be explained with the ancient names, though of later musicians.

Now at length let us approach our natural System of one octave, and apply to it the letters from customary music.

I establish, therefore, that the lowest voice of this natural and demonstrative System is that which the ancients called *Proslambanomenon* and the musical scale calls *Gammut*, and that to it must certainly be assigned the letter *G*. The reasons are these: because the voices constituted through harmonic divisions determine below the octave such intervals with the voice of the whole string as musicians ordinarily establish between *G* and *b, ♮, c, d, de, e*.

Someone might say that the same intervals can be understood between *D* and *F, Fe, G, A, b, ♮*, and likewise between *c* and *de, e, f, g, ge, a*. Nor do I deny that instrumental musicians use these letters generally, and do not distinguish in them the major and minor tones. For what should hinder them from transposing a song from *G* to *d*, who in their instruments first melt together two tones, the major :math:`8:9` and the minor :math:`9:10`, into one interval :math:`4:5`, and afterward bisect it precisely into two equal tones, equal to one another?—although Galilei is more accurate in this tempering. We, however, look here not to the artlessness [*ἀτεχνίαν*] of empirics, but to the exactness [*ἀκρίβειαν*] of Nature; wherefore we cannot imitate them. And indeed as to why the letter *c* cannot be the starting point from which the natural octave System rises, even they themselves would reject it by this argument: for between their *ge* and *a* is a semitone, and this interval is in the fifth place, because *a* is a sixth from *c*. Yet in the natural System, before the sixth we need a diesis. But musicians themselves place a diesis only between *b* and *♮* and between *de* and *e*. Therefore, even with instrumentalists agreeing, our System must rise either from *D* or from *G*, so that the pure natural System may be expressed.

Nor yet can the System begin from *D*. For it is their custom to place a semitone between *d* and *de*, whereas in my natural System there is in the lowest place a limma, which is less than a semitone.
