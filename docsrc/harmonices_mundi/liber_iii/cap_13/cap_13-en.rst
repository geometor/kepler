**Chapter XIII.**

*What is naturally concinnous and apt song.*

We shall say nothing of that shrill manner of singing which the Turks and Hungarians are wont to use for their battle-trumpet, imitating rather the rude cries of brute animals than human nature.

It seems clear that its first author drew such an uncouth melody from an improperly shaped instrument, and by long custom, together with the very manufacture of the instrument, handed it down to posterity and to the whole nation. I was present at Prague at the prayers which the priest of the Turkish envoy was wont to chant at fixed hours, kneeling and frequently striking the earth with his forehead: it appeared easily that he sang by training, and had acquired practice and readiness through labor, for he did not hesitate at all; yet he used intervals so strange, unwonted, abrupt, and abhorrent that no one seems ever able consistently to devise the like by the guidance of his own nature and from himself of his own accord. I shall try to express something nearest to it by our musical notes.

*[Musical example of a Turkish melody.]*

Concinnous song, therefore, and apt to the judgment of human ears, is that which, beginning from a certain sound, tends from it through concinnous intervals to sounds consonant both first with it and for the most part also mutually among themselves—skimming swiftly over dissonant intervals, but lingering on consonant ones, whether by measure of time and length of syllables, or by frequent return to them, as if striving after a consonance of two voices between themselves by the leading of a single voice from one place of the System to another. *Example.*

Here the initial sound is on the key *G*, with which in soft song *b, c, d, g* concord. Therefore the song runs out (first having made a downward turn) to the consonant key *c*, and leaps cleanly over the dissonant place *A*—though it would have been the same if it had touched it, but with a brief stay; while the whole remaining series intones chiefly on the places *b, d, g*, expressing such a skeleton of the octave, returning most frequently to *d*, afterward to *b*, and occasionally rising above to *g*, to all these places markedly: not so to *a* or to *f*, places dissonant with the first; and at length it returns to *G* and ends there.

Concerning this definition of song, many things come to us to be noted.

I. The parts of song, of all or some of which every song consists, Euclid names these four: *Agoge* [Ἀγωγὴν], *Tone* [Τονὴν], *Petteia* [Πεττείαν],

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0152
   :page: p. 62

and *Ploke* [Πλοκὴν], the words themselves indicating what they are. For *Agoge* is the leading of the voice from the proposed root up to a place consonant with the root, or from one consonant to another consonant either with it or with the first root. *Tone* is the dwelling in a place, either the first or one consonant with it, or even one consonant with the preceding, though it be not consonant with the first. *Ploke*, or interweaving, is a certain species or coloring of *agoge*, just as *petteia*, or sporting, is of *tone*; and as *Agoge* is to *Tone*, so is *Ploke* to *Petteia*; because *agoge* crosses as it were directly, while *ploke* wanders about in crossing around *agoge*, like a dog around a traveler.

Thus *agoge* is as it were direct motion, *ploke* varied motion; while *tone* is as it were the goal of motion, or rest in a sought-after place of the System, and *petteia* is many goals of smaller motions and as it were resting-places. In our example, the syllables [*demit oves Christus in*] are a kind of perpetual *tone*, with only two syllables excepted; whereas the syllables [*cens rec li peccat*] express *petteia*. And if in this melody you look at the ligatures of syllables [*laud an patr tor*] and consider them as two-note neumes, then there will be no pure and unmixed *agoge* in it. Yet if you consider that it happened in rustic fashion that out of a sound which ought to have been sung longer, a two-note figure rising at the end was made—and if to these syllables you restore the simple prolonged sound which is the first of the ligature—you will find pure *agoge* in these: [*Paschali laudes*], likewise [*immolent*], likewise [*Christiani*], likewise [*Innocens patri*], and a brief one in [*catores*]; whereas pure *ploke*, though unnatural, is in the Turkish example.

Just as the skeleton stands to the body among anatomists, so in one System of the octave do the sounds consonant both among themselves and with the root or base of the octave stand to the melody itself, or song. For just as flesh fills the hollows of the bones and clothes them for beauty, so these parts just reviewed fill the skeleton of the octave—especially *agoge* and *petteia*, wandering through dissonant places interposed between consonant sounds—and shape and as it were embody the melody.

II. When song is defined thus, that it begins from a certain sound which is the base of the octave System, this must not always be understood in act; for since variety delights and serves emphasis, very often a melody begins as if abruptly from another key or place: yet in potency some definite fixed beginning is understood, which shines forth everywhere from the whole course of the melody—as in this most ancient German hymn [*Christ ist erstanden*] it is easily understood, even though in act the beginning is made from *d*, that the root is *G*.

*[Musical example: Christ ist erstanden (in F/C clef, beginning on d).]*

III. A similar exception holds in that regular song is said with its *tone* or *petteia* to aim at places consonant with the root of the octave. For frequently in the middle of a longer melodic course these parts turn around

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0153
   :page: p. 63 [impr. 73]

places dissonant with the first root; yet this is done for the sake of variety, and is just as if a new melody were mingled with the former melody, in whose dissonant place some new beginning were fixed, or a new octave skeleton were marked out. It is like an excursion or digression in an oration; therefore we do not linger in such things, but quickly return as it were to the principal skeleton. And as long as *tone* and *petteia* turn about dissonant notes, we understand that the song is not yet ending; for at the very end one must return not only to consonances, as a rule, but also to the very root of one's own octave. From this articulation of song and aiming at consonances, the ancients seem to have called even simple song *Harmony*, not otherwise than as a seemly and apt correspondence of limbs, which is the soul of form, would be called harmony—as it were, beauty; for *Harmonia* was also the name of a woman.

IV. As for *Agoge*, it again is various; for it does not always pass in act through all intervening places, but often leaps over some and is carried by a leap from one note to one consonant with it; and then it is *agoge* only in potency, and almost mere *petteia* or *tone*, which is the most frequent custom of our trumpeters when they sound a battle-call or cavalry charge: for then scarcely anything is heard except the skeleton of the octave. Consider whether that ancient *Nomos orthios* [Νόμος ὄρθιος] was of this kind.

V. Since concinnous song consists of its elements, which are concinnous intervals, those must first be defined more accurately.

For in a twofold sense intervals are called concinnous or inconcinnous. First, intervals taken singly each by itself, with respect to their origin, are called concinnous or inconcinnous: those indeed which Nature admits in the divisions of consonant intervals, and these which do not result from comparisons of consonances, altogether alien from Harmony, as was explained in Chapter IV. Second, with respect to song or melody itself, there is this distinction even among the intervals of music, or those concinnous in the first sense: that certain of them, considered jointly, although they are concinnous by Nature—namely having arisen from the comparison and abstraction of harmonies—are nevertheless by usage rejected as inconcinnous by melodies in a certain respect. *Tone* and *petteia* presuppose and regard the former sense, whereas *Agoge*, both stepwise and leaping, regards chiefly this latter sense. These become inconcinnous, again, on two accounts: either with respect to the Genus or skeleton of the octave, because they are simply not used in it and do not form it, or if they are arranged in an order other than Nature taught above in Chapter VII—as if someone wished to arrange three semitones in a row: a semitone is indeed concinnous in the first sense, yet this ordering of semitones is not concinnous in this second sense. Thus the diesis, concinnous by Nature, is nevertheless in either the hard or the soft Genus of song inconcinnous by itself; or finally, they are not sung in succession, nor permitted to a leap of a melodic voice, nor suited to *Agoge*.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0154
   :page: p. 64

In this second sense, therefore, the inconcinnous and forbidden intervals are comprehended as it were by these principal laws:

I. A diesis is not regularly sung before or after a semitone.

II. Two semitones, even though they are arranged in succession in the order of minimal intervals of Chapter VII, cannot be sung on three notes in succession, but must coalesce with two other elements into two tones.

III. Two semitones within the compass of one fourth or fifth which stands in the lowest place of the octave skeleton are not ordinarily sung. For it would be brought about that the fourth or fifth voice in order would not concord with the first, neither perfectly nor imperfectly, and so would not truly be a fourth or fifth.

IV. Four tones in succession are not sung, except in the highest part of the octave, when for the sake of color and variety the skeleton is changed; not, therefore, ordinarily. *[T. T. To. To.]*

V. The seventh and all dissonances above the octave are not ordinarily sung on notes arranged in order after the root, unless the preceding has in some way the character of an end, and the following the character of a new beginning.

VI. Sixths, although consonant, we admit more rarely, and only minor ones. *[In me transierunt.]*

VII. Two tetrachords of one octave, when the place of the semitone is not the same in both of them, are more rarely sung—namely for the sake of color, or of syncopations (of which in Chapter XVI); nor yet is there sheer freedom in placing the semitone, for it is restricted to that place which the Genus of the song admits.

VIII. Akin to this is that three tones in succession in the lowest place of the octave are not sung; yet higher up they are admitted by the laws already stated.

IX. Two semitones do not naturally coalesce into one tone, although the difference in hearing is small; as if the octave were to begin from *E*, between *ce* and *de* there would arise the interval $225:256$ from composition, which is naturally inconcinnous—see Chapter IV.

X. In general, every System of the octave is inconcinnous which does not establish both a fourth and a fifth below.

Most of these laws will soon serve us in establishing the number of the Modes in the following Chapter.

VI. As for that clause of the definition, *skimming swiftly over dissonant intervals, lingering on consonant ones*: although it is not my purpose in this work to dispute about measures, yet in general I advise that the chief grace of harmonic song lies in measure. For what was said most generally—*skimming swiftly over dissonant intervals, etc.*—is made true in particular as follows. First, the Latins have a distinction between a long and a short syllable, of which they make the latter of one beat and the former of two, and thus of double length. Today, however, not only poetry is sung, but also prose, in which the distinction of long and short syllables is utterly neglected by modern musicians, and regard is paid solely to the manner of pronouncing, corrupt though it be today: any syllable is held to be long which is wont to be pronounced with an acute accent, and short which is pronounced with a grave accent.

It is no wonder this happens in the Latin language, since the later Greeks did the same: although they wrote purely, they embraced a most corrupt pronunciation, in which there is no longer any distinction of acute accent from length of syllable, or of grave from brevity, even though they abound in signs for this, which among the Latins are not held in such frequent use. Accordingly, the Greeks, having abolished the custom of composing quantitative verse, for some centuries now have begun to write so-called *political* verses, in which syllables are counted, not measured, and the verse takes as its penultimate not the syllable that is long by nature, but that which is sharpened by accent in popular speech. Our musicians imitate this in Latin also, so that they do not spare even metrical verses, except very rarely, and never altogether—I believe because perpetual duple and triple measure has been received in songs, whereas metrical verses mingle these unequally, except the Hexameter, which is content with the proportion of equality in the spondee and the perfect double in the dactyl. A short syllable, therefore, or one standing in place of a short when a spondee follows an iamb, and in general a syllable to be pronounced with an accent, musicians assign to a dissonant sound, if one must pass through it, and a long syllable to consonant ones, or nearly so.

Second, the Ionic [or vernacular] tongues which use rhythm—German, French, Spanish, Italian—make either the final or the penultimate syllable of a verse acute, and utter the whole verse as if acute and grave syllables followed one another alternately, whether this actually holds, as in apt German verse, or does not hold, as frequently in French. Thus all their verse is similar either to Trochaic or to Iambic, and those either Acatalectic—*Schöpffer Himmels und der Erden*; *Nun bitten wir den Heylgen Geist*—or catalectic—*Deß sich wundert alle Welt*; *Er ist der Morgensterne*. Therefore musicians fix the seat of dissonant sounds on grave or shortened syllables, and of consonant ones on acute syllables.

Third, when song receives its measure not from the syllables of the text, but from the *Tactus* [beat]—and this follows either duple or triple proportion—they reserve the first part of the beat indeed for consonances, and the latter part (and in triple time the shorter part, which is signified by the raising of the hand) chiefly for dissonances;

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0156
   :page: p. 66

and they take care as far as possible lest any syllable occupy the first half of the beat other than one that is to be uttered acutely, or lest two longs be thrust into the last part of the beat.
