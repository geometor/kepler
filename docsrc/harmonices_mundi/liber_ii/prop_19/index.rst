Proposition XIX: Six Ways of Filling a Plane with Two Species of Figures
========================================================================

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0069
   :page: p. 51

**Proposition XIX.**

*A plane place is filled by planes of two figures in six ways: twice with five angles, once with four, and thrice with three angles.*

For six planes cannot meet if the angle of even one of them is greater than that of a triangle: since the magnitude of the first, the triangular angle, is two thirds of one right angle, so that taken six times it makes twelve thirds, or four right angles. Therefore, if one of these six is greater—namely, when it becomes a figure

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0070
   :page: p. 52

of more sides—four right angles are exceeded, and then the plane is not paved, by Proposition XVI of this book.

1. Five planes, however, meet when to four triangles is joined one angle equal to two triangles, and such is the hexagonal angle; a form such as at letter *L*.

2. Or if to three triangles two squares are joined, since they equal three other triangles. A form such as in *M*, or such as in *N*, namely continuously uniform; or finally, such as in *O*, of non-uniform continuation.

But if to two triangles you take three squares, these already exceeded four right angles; much more so if two greater angles are added to two triangles.

3. Four angles of two species meet when to two triangles are joined two hexagons. A form such as in *P*, or such as in *R*.

In whatever other way you couple four angles, you will always produce either more or less than four right angles, and therefore will not pave a plane place.

If therefore we join three angles, taking care that there be no more than two species: first, there cannot be two triangles, nor two squares, for they would not exceed two right angles, and there would remain for the third an angle which no single figure fills by itself.

4. If, however, one triangle is placed among the three, two dodecagons are congruent with it; and continuation is possible, nor are other junctions mixed in. Let the form of this plane be seen at letter *S*.

Refer hither the twelve-pointed star, on the ground that its concave angle equals the triangular angle: thus a dodecagon is divisible into a star and twelve triangles; therefore five triangles and two rays of two stars meet: a continuable form, let it be viewed at letter *T*.

5. And if one square is taken among the three, two octagons are congruent with it, and this form also is continuable: let it be seen at letter *V*.

Refer hither the eight-pointed star, on the ground that its concave angle equals the square angle: thus an octagon is divisible into a star and eight square triangles, two of which make a square. And thus three squares and two rays of two stars fill the place: in a mixed form, as at letter *X*; or mixed in another way also, as at letter *Y*.

6. Leaving aside now the triangle and square in fitting three together, if we come to the pentagon, two of them can be taken, because joined together they exceed two right angles; and into the remaining place one decagon is congruent—for a decagon is crowned by ten pentagons—yet this form is not continued purely. See the diagram at letter *Z*, within.

Refer hither therefore the five-pointed star, when there are three pentagons and one ray of a star; because the concave angle of the star also takes in one pentagonal angle, no less than the gap of three pentagons takes in a ray of the star. See at the same letter *Z*, on the outside.

Yet not even so does continuation succeed to infinity; for unsociable is the kingdom of this sect, which, after admitting a few of its own kind, straightway fortifies itself: behold these two forms in another way also, at letter *Aa*.

But if you wish to continue it by all means, certain monsters must be admitted: two decagons joined into one another, with two sides cut out in each; and there arises a five-angled series of structure even in infinite continuation, such that in the first and narrowest five-angled series there are five decagons with no intermediate monster; in the second, wider series, linear rows of decagons mediate, and single

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0071
   :page: p. 55

places are occupied by single couples of two decagons joined together, which a single decagon mediates; in the fourth, whole decagons stand once more at the corners, and two decagons arranged at equal intervals mediate each pair of corners; in the fifth, stars stand with individual extreme rays at each corner, while two whole decagons fill the lines, and in the middle two joinings of decagons. And so consequently, every five-angled form brings something new. The structure is most laborious and most ingenious, to be viewed at the same letter *Aa*.

Refer hither also the ten-pointed star, into whose concave angle fits the angle of a pentagon; and conversely two rays (which are two thirds of a square?—no, *trideciles*, three tenths) together with two pentagons meet and fill the plane place. This form admits unequal pentagons; and although it is continuable, yet it admits empty gaping decagons in the middle. The form is to be viewed at letter *Bb*.

A single pentagon cannot be taken into a junction of three planes, for its angle has six fifths of one right angle, by Proposition XXXIII of Book I; therefore to the two remaining angles there would be left fourteen fifths, seven fifths to each, such as no figure has. Nor can two hexagons be taken, for the remainder is also the angle of a hexagon, and the form becomes one of those reported above, whereas here we seek structures of two species, not of figures of a single species. Through the succeeding many-sided figures whose angle is greater than that of a hexagon, when two angles are subtracted from four right angles, there remains for the last angle less than a hexagon has; and when one is subtracted, there remains for the two remaining less than two hexagons have. Yet concerning those figures which have fewer and smaller angles than the hexagon, we have already dealt above as to which and how many there can be when three pave a plane.
