*CHAPTER IX. The Origin of the Eccentricities in the Individual Planets from the Procurement of Harmonies among their Motions.*

Since therefore we see that the universal Harmonies of all six Planets cannot happen by chance, especially through the extremes of the motions, which we have seen all concur toward universal Harmonies, except two which concurred toward those nearest to universal;
since much less could it happen by chance that all the places of the system of the Octave, established in the third book through Harmonic sections, should be marked out by the extremes of the Planetary motions;
and least of all could the most subtle business of the distinction of celestial Harmonies into two genera, hard and soft, come about by chance, without the singular care of the Maker: it follows therefore that the Creator, fountain of all wisdom,
perpetual approver of Order, eternal and superessential spring of Geometry and Harmonics—that this very Maker of the heavens Himself, I say, joined the Harmonic proportions arising from regular plane figures to the five regular solid figures,
and out of both classes fashioned one most perfect Archetype of the heavens; in which, just as through the five solid figures there shone forth the Ideas of the orbs on which the six stars are borne,
so also through the offspring of the plane figures, the Harmonies (deduced from them in the third book) should contain the measures of the Eccentricities in the individual orbs, for proportioning the motions of the bodies;
and from these two things one tempering should be made, and the greater proportions of the Orbs should yield somewhat to the lesser proportions of the Eccentricities necessary for procuring the Harmonies;
and conversely, out of the Harmonic proportions, those chiefly should be accommodated to the Planets which had a greater kinship with each solid figure, insofar as this could be done through Harmonies;
and by this method at last both the proportions of the Orbs and the Eccentricities of each should spring forth together from the Archetype, while from the amplitude of the Orbs and the bulk of the bodies the periodic Times of each should result.

While I strive to bring this method forth into the light of the human Intellect through the elements used by Geometers, may the Author of the Heavens Himself, Father of Intellects, Bestower of mortal senses, Himself immortal and superblessed, look favorably upon me;
and may He prevent the darkness of our mind from bringing forth anything unworthy of His Majesty concerning this His work; and may He bring it about that, as imitators of God by the help of His Holy Spirit, we emulate the perfection of His works
by holiness of life, unto which He has chosen His Church on earth and cleansed it from sins by the blood of His Son; keeping far away all dissonances of enmities, all contentions, rivalries, wraths, quarrels, dissensions, sects,
envy, provocations, irritations through taunts, and the other works of the flesh: which all who possess the spirit of Christ

will not only wish along with me, but will also strive to express in deeds,
and to make their calling sure, spurning all the depraved morals of all parties, veiled and painted over under the pretext of Zeal, or love of truth, or singular erudition, or modesty toward contentious teachers, or any other specious excuse whatsoever.
Holy Father, preserve us in the consonance of mutual love, that we may be one, just as Thou art one with Thy Son our Lord and with the Holy Spirit, and just as Thou hast made all Thy works one through the sweetest bonds of consonances;

*Prior Reasons.*

**Axiom I.**

It is fitting that, wherever it could be done at all, all manner of Harmonies should have been established between the Extremes of the motions both of single Planets and of pairs, so that this variety might adorn the world.

**Axiom II.**

The five intervals of the six Orbs ought to correspond somewhat in magnitude to the proportion of the geometric spheres which are inscribed in and circumscribed about the five regular solid figures, and in the same order that is natural to the figures themselves.

*On this see Ch. I, the Mysterium Cosmographicum, and Book IV of the Epitome of Astronomy.*

**Proposition III.**

Between the Earth and Mars, and between the same Earth and Venus, the intervals ought to be the least in proportion to their orbs, and very nearly equal;
middling and again nearly equal between Saturn and Jupiter, and between Venus and Mercury; greatest between Jupiter and Mars.

*For by II, the Planets corresponding in position to the figures that make the least proportion of geometric spheres ought likewise to make the least;
middling those which correspond to the figures of middling proportion, and greatest those which correspond to the figure of greatest proportion. But in whatever order the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron stand among the figures, in the same stand the pairs of planets,
the one of Mars and the Earth, the other of the Earth and Venus; and in whatever order the Cube and Octahedron stand, in the same stand the pair of Saturn and Jupiter, and the pair of Venus and Mercury;
and finally in whatever order the Tetrahedron stands, in the same stands the pair of Jupiter and Mars; see Ch. III. Therefore the proportion between the Planetary orbs first mentioned will be least; between those of Saturn and Jupiter, nearly equal
to that between Venus and Mercury; and finally greatest between the orbs of Jupiter and Mars.*

**Axiom IV.**

All the Planets ought to have their own Eccentricities, no less than a motion in latitude, and according to them, different distances from the Sun, the source of motion.

*Just as the essence of motion consists not in BEING, but in BECOMING,
so also the form or figure of the region which any Planet traverses by motion does NOT BECOME solid all at once from the beginning, but in the course of time acquires at length not only its longitude, but also its latitude and depth,
the triad of dimensions being completed; and thus gradually by the weaving together and accumulation of very many revolutions it comes to represent the appearance of a hollow orb having the same center as the Sun, just as from very many
interwoven and wound circles of silk thread the Silkworm's little house is built.*

**Proposition V.**

To each pair of neighboring Planets two different Harmonies had to be attributed.

*For by IV, any planet has a longest and a shortest distance from the Sun;
wherefore by Ch. III of this book it will also have a slowest and a fastest motion. There are therefore two primary comparisons of extreme motions, one of the Diverging in two planets, the other of the Converging.
And it is necessary that they be different from each other, because the proportion of the diverging will be greater, that of the converging lesser. Moreover, through the different pairs of Planets, there had to be different Harmonies, so that this variety
might contribute to the adornment of the World, by Ax. I; and also because the proportions of the intervals between pairs are different, by III. But to each proportion of orbs there correspond certain Harmonic proportions by quantitative kinship,
as was demonstrated in Ch. V of this book.*

**Proposition VI.**

The two least Harmonies, 4:5 and 5:6, have no place between two planets.

*For 5 is to 4 as 1000 to 800, and 6 to 5 as 1000 to 833+.
But the spheres circumscribed about the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron have a greater proportion to the inscribed spheres, namely that of 1000 to 795 etc., and these two proportions indicate the intervals between the nearest Planetary Orbs, or the least spaces:
for in the other regular figures the spheres stand farther apart from one another. Now the proportion of the motions is still greater than the proportions of the intervals, unless the proportion of the Eccentricities to the Orbs be huge,
by Number 13 of Chapter III. Therefore the least proportion of the motions is greater than 4:5 and 5:6 are. Consequently, these Harmonies, hindered indeed by the regular figures, obtain no place among the planets.*

**Proposition VII.**

The Harmony Diatessaron can have no place between the converging motions of two Planets, unless their proper proportions of extremes, when joined, be more than a Diapente.

*For let 3:4 be between the converging motions, and let there be first no Eccentricity,
no proper proportion of motions in the individual Planets, but let the same motions be both converging and Mean: then it follows that the corresponding intervals, which by this supposition will be the semidiameters of the Orbs, constitute two-thirds of this proportion,
namely 4480:5424 by Ch. III. But this proportion is already less than the proportion of spheres of any Regular figure: Therefore the whole interior Orb would be cut by the planes of any regular figure inscribed in the exterior Orb.
But this is against Axiom II.*

*Secondly, let there be some sum of the proper proportions between the extreme motions; and let the proportion of the converging motions be 3:4, or 75:100, and let the proportion of the corresponding intervals be 1000:795,
since no regular figure makes a lesser proportion of spheres. And because that inverted proportion of motions surpasses this proportion of intervals by the excess 750:795, let this excess also be subtracted from the proportion 1000:795
according to the teaching of Ch. III; there remains 9434:7950, half the proportion of the spheres. Therefore the double of this, namely 8901:6320, that is, 10000:7100, is the proportion of the spheres. From this subtract the proportion of the converging intervals 1000:795;
there will remain 7100:795, about a major Tone. At least so great must be the sum of the two proportions which the mean intervals have to the converging ones on both sides, so that between the converging motions there may be a Diatessaron.
Therefore the diverging extreme intervals make to the converging extreme intervals a sum of proportions about double this, that is, two Tones, and the converging motions again the double of this, that is, four Tones; which is more than a Diapente.
If therefore the sum of the proper proportions of two neighboring planets is less than a Diapente, between their converging motions there cannot be a Diatessaron.*

**Proposition VIII.**

To Saturn and Jupiter were owed the Harmonies 1:2 and 1:3, that is, Diapason and Diapente upon Diapason.

*For they are the first and highest of the Planets, and they obtained the first figure, the Cube, by Ch. I of this book:
and these Harmonies are first in the order of nature and are the heads of the first figural families, the Bisecting or Tetragonal, and the Trigonal, by what was said in Book I. And what is the main point, the Diapason 1:2
is next greater than the Semitriple proportion of the spheres of the Cube; wherefore it is fit to become the lesser proportion of the motions of the cubic planets, by Ch. III, Num. 13; and consequently 1:3 serves for the greater proportion.
This same thing also follows thus. For if some Harmony stands to some proportion of figural spheres as the proportion of motions apparent from the Sun stands to the proportion of mean intervals, deservedly will such a Harmony be attributed to the motions.
Now it is natural that the proportion of diverging motions be much greater than the sesquialterate of the proportion of the Orbs, by the end of Ch. III, that is, it approaches the double proportion of the proportion of the Orbs;
and 1:3 is also the double of the proportion of the cubic spheres, namely that which we call Semitriple. Therefore the Triple is owed to the diverging motions of Saturn and Jupiter.
See very many other kinships of these Proportions with the Cube above in Ch. II.*

**Proposition IX.**

The proper proportions of the extreme motions of Saturn and Jupiter, when joined, ought to become 2:3, a Diapente, approximately.

*This follows from the preceding; for if the perihelion motion of Jupiter is triple the aphelion of Saturn, and conversely the aphelion of Jupiter double the perihelion of Saturn, wherefore subtracting 1:2 from 1:3, there remains 2:3.*

**Axiom X.**

When the choice is free in other respects, to the superior Planet is owed the proper proportion of motions which is prior by nature, or of a more excellent genus, or also which is greater.

**Proposition XI.**

The proportion of the aphelion motion of Saturn to the perihelion ought to become 4:5, a major Third, and of the motions of Jupiter 5:6, a minor Third.

*For because jointly they possess 2:3,
and this cannot be divided harmonically otherwise than into 4:5 and 5:6, God the Harmonist therefore divided the Harmony 2:3 harmonically, by Ax. I, and gave its greater Harmonic part, and of the more excellent Hard genus—since it is masculine—
to the greater and higher Planet Saturn, and the lesser 5:6 to the lower Jupiter, by X.*

**Proposition XII.**

To Venus and Mercury was owed the Greater Harmony 1:4, the Disdiapason.

*For just as the Cube is the first of the primary figures,
so the Octahedron is the first of the Secondary figures, by Ch. I of this book. And just as the Cube, geometrically considered, is exterior, and the Octahedron interior, namely inscribable in it, so also Saturn and Jupiter
are the beginning of the superior and exterior Planets, or from without; whereas Mercury and Venus are the beginning of the interior ones, or from within: and the Octahedron is placed between their courses; see Ch. III.
Therefore to Venus and Mercury also is owed some primary Harmony cognate to the Octahedron. Further, among the Harmonies after 1:2 and 1:3, there follows in natural order 1:4, and it is cognate to the cubic 1:2,
because it arose from the same sect of figures, namely the Tetragonal, and is commensurable with it, namely its double; and the Octahedron is also cognate and commensurable with the Cube. And 1:4 is cognate to the Octahedron by a private title,
on account of the number four in the former, and the Quadrangular figure hidden in the Octahedron, whose proportion of spheres is called Semidouble. Therefore the Harmony 1:4 is continuously multiple of that proportion, in the double proportion,
namely it is the quadruple of the Semidouble; see Ch. II. Therefore 1:4 was owed to Venus and Mercury. And because 1:2 in the Cube was made the lesser Harmony of the pair, since the outermost position fell to it,
in the Octahedron this 1:4 will now be the greater Harmony of the pair, as being that to which the innermost position fell. But this too is the cause why 1:4 is here given for the greater, not for the lesser.
For since the proportion of spheres of the Octahedron is Semitriple—supposing that the inscription of the Octahedron between the planets is perfect (although it is not perfect, but Mercury penetrates somewhat, which works to our advantage)—
the proportion of the converging motions ought therefore to be less than the sesquialterate of that Semitriple: yet even 1:3 is plainly double the Semitriple, and thus greater than is right; how much greater than is right will 1:4 be,
since it is greater than 1:3. Therefore not even half of 1:4 is tolerated between the converging motions. Therefore 1:4 cannot be the lesser Harmony of the Octahedron; therefore it will be the Greater.*

*Moreover, 1:4 is cognate to the Octahedral quadrangle, whose proportion of spheres is Semidouble, just as 1:3 is cognate to the Cube, as being that whose proportion of spheres is Semitriple. For just as 1:3 is a multiple of the Semitriple, namely its double;
so also here 1:4 is a multiple of the Semidouble, namely its twice double, that is, quadruple. Wherefore if 1:3 ought to become the greater Harmony of the Cube, by VII, therefore 1:4 also ought to become the greater Harmony of its Octahedron.*

**Proposition XIII.**

To the extreme motions of Jupiter and Mars were owed the Harmonies: the Greater indeed 1:8, a Trisdiapason approximately, and the Lesser 5:24, a minor Third above a Disdiapason.

*For because the Cube obtained 1:2 and 1:3,
while the proportion of the spheres of the Tetrahedron, which is situated between Jupiter and Mars, called Triple, is double the proportion of the spheres of the Cube, called Semitriple: it was therefore fitting that to the Tetrahedron also should be accommodated
proportions of motions double the cubic proportions. Now the doubles of 1:2 and 1:3 themselves are these Proportions, 1:4 and 1:9. But 1:9 is not Harmonic, and 1:4 has already been used up for the Octahedron. Therefore Harmonies neighboring these proportions
had to be taken, by Ax. I. Now first the neighbors of 1:9 itself are the lesser 1:8 and the greater 1:10. Between these the choice is made by kinship with the Tetrahedron, which has nothing in common with the Pentagon,
since 1:10 is of the pentagonal sect: whereas the Tetrahedron has a greater kinship with 1:8 on many grounds, which seek out from Ch. II. Moreover, this also makes for 1:8, that just as 1:3 is the greater of the Cube,
and 1:4 the greater of the Octahedron, because they are multiples of the proportions between the figural spheres, so also 1:8 ought to become the greater of the Tetrahedron; because just as its body is double the Octahedron inscribed in it,
as was said in Ch. I, so also the term 8 of this Tetrahedral proportion is double the term 4 of the Octahedral proportion. Moreover, just as 1:2, the lesser of the Cube, is one Diapason, and 1:4, the greater of the Octahedron,
is two Diapasons, so now 1:8, the greater of the Tetrahedron, ought to become three Diapasons. And more Diapasons were owed to this than to those, because since the lesser Tetrahedral Harmony is necessarily the greatest of all the lesser harmonies
in the other figures (since the proportion of Tetrahedral spheres is also the greatest of all figural proportions), the greater Tetrahedral Harmony also ought to surpass the greater ones of the others in the number of Diapasons themselves.
Finally, the triad of Diapason intervals has a familiarity with the Trigonal species of the Tetrahedron, and has a certain perfection, according to the saying, "Every threefold thing is perfect"; since also the number Eight, its term,
is the first of the Cubic numbers of perfect quantity, namely of three dimensions.*

*II. Neighboring to 1:4 itself, or 6:24, are the Harmonies: the greater indeed 5:24, the lesser 6:20 or 3:10.
Again, 3:10 is of the Pentagonal sect, with which the Tetrahedron has nothing in common. But 5:24, on account of the numbers 3 and 4 (whose offspring are the numbers 12 and 24), has a familiarity with the Tetrahedron.
For the other lesser terms, namely 5 and 3, we pass over here, because their degree of kinship with the figures is very slight, as is to be seen in Ch. II. Besides, the proportion of the spheres of the Tetrahedron is Triple:
and so great ought also to be the proportion of the converging intervals approximately, by Ax. II. Now by Ch. III, the proportion of the converging motions approaches the inverted sesquialterate of the intervals;
and the sesquialterate of the Triple is between 1000 and 193 approximately. Of such parts, therefore, as the aphelion motion of Mars is 1000, that of Jupiter will be a little greater than 193, and much less than 333, the third part of it:
therefore not the Harmony 10:3, that is 1000:333, but the Harmony 24:5, that is 1000:208, has place between the converging motions of Jupiter and Mars.*

**Proposition XIV.**

The proper proportion of the extreme motions of Mars ought to become greater than the Diatessaron 3:4, and approximately 18:25.

*For let the Harmonies 5:24 and 1:8, or 3:24, already be precisely attributed in common to Jupiter and Mars, by Prop. XIII.
Subtract the lesser 5:24 from the greater 3:24; there remains 3:5, the sum of the proper proportions of both. Now the proper proportion of Jupiter alone was found above in Prop. XI to be 5:6.
Subtract this therefore from the sum of the proper proportions 3:5. That is, subtract 25:30 from 18:30; there remains the proper proportion of Mars, 18:25, which is greater than 18:24 or 3:4. And it will become still greater if, by the following reasons,
the common greater 1:8 be increased.*

**Proposition XV.**

Between the converging motions of Mars and the Earth, the Earth and Venus, Venus and Mercury, had to be distributed the Harmonies 2:3 Diapente, 5:8 soft Sixth, 3:5 hard Sixth; and those in this very order.

*For the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron, figures placed between Mars, the Earth, and Venus, have the least proportion between their circumscribed and inscribed spheres: therefore to them out of the possible Harmonies are owed the least,
since they are cognate on this account, and so that Ax. II may hold. Yet the least Harmonies of all, namely 5:6 and 4:5, are not possible, by VI. Therefore to the said figures are owed Harmonies next greater than these,
namely either 3:4, or 2:3, or 5:8, or 3:5.*

*Again, the figure placed between Venus and Mercury, namely the Octahedron, has the same proportion of its spheres as the Cube: yet to the cube for the lesser Harmony, which is between converging motions,
there fell the Diapason, by VII; therefore by analogy so much would be owed also to the Octahedron, namely 1:2 for the lesser, if no diversity intervened. Now this much diversity intervenes, that of the Cubic planets indeed, Saturn namely and Jupiter,
the proper proportions of motions of each joined together made a sum no greater than 2:3; whereas here of the Octahedral Planets, Venus and Mercury, the proper proportions of each joined together will make a sum greater than 2:3,
which easily appears thus. For let there be what the analogy between the Cube and Octahedron would require if it stood alone; let the Lesser Octahedral, I say, be greater than those prescribed here, and so let it be plainly as great
as the cubic was, namely 1:2; now the Greater was 1:4 by XII. Subtracting therefore hence the Lesser 1:2 which we just posited, there still remains 1:2 for the sum of the proper proportions of Venus and Mercury;
but 1:2 is more than the sum 2:3 of the proper proportions of Saturn and Jupiter. And indeed this greater sum is followed by a greater eccentricity, by Ch. III, and a greater Eccentricity is followed by a lesser proportion of converging motions,
by the same Ch. III. Wherefore it happens by the addition of a greater Eccentricity to the analogy between the Cube and Octahedron, that a lesser proportion of the converging motions of Venus and Mercury than 1:2 is also required.
This was also fitting to Axiom I, that since the Harmony Diapason was used up on the Cubic planets, another neighboring one should be procured for the Octahedral ones, and by the former demonstration less than 1:2.
Now the one next less than this is 3:5.*

*which, as the greatest of the three, was owed to the figure of greater proportion of its spheres, namely the Octahedron:
Therefore the lesser ones 5:8 and 2:3 or 3:4 were left to the Icosahedron and Dodecahedron, figures of lesser proportion of their Spheres.*

*Now these remaining ones are distributed between the two remaining figures thus.
For just as out of the figures, although of equal proportions between their spheres, the Cube obtained the Harmony 1:2, whereas the Octahedron obtained the lesser 3:5, because the sum of the proper proportions of Venus and Mercury surpassed
the sum of the proper proportions of Saturn and Jupiter: so also here to the Dodecahedron, although it makes the same proportion of its spheres as the Icosahedron, was owed a lesser Harmony than to the Icosahedron, yet the nearest,
on account of a like cause, namely because this figure is between the Earth and Mars, whose Eccentricity was made great in the foregoing; whereas the Eccentricities of Venus and the Earth, as we shall hear in what follows, are Very Small.
And since the Octahedron has 3:5, and the Icosahedron, whose proportion of spheres is lesser, has the succeeding one, a little lesser than that, namely 5:8, there was left to the Dodecahedron either the remaining 2:3 or 3:4:
rather indeed the former, as being nearer to the Icosahedral 5:8, because the figures are also similar.*

*But 3:4 was not even possible. For although in the foregoing the proper proportion of the extreme motions of Mars was sufficiently great,
yet the Earth, as has already been said and will appear in what follows, contributed a proper proportion too small for the sum of both to surpass a Diapente. Therefore 3:4 could not have place, by VII.
And this the more so, because, as will follow in Prop. XLVII, the proportion of the converging intervals had to be greater than 1000:795.*

**Proposition XVI.**

The proper proportions of the motions of Venus and Mercury, joined together, ought to make 5:12 approximately.

*For subtract the lesser harmonic 3:5, attributed in common to this pair by Prop. XV, from their greater 1:4 or 3:12, by XII; there remains 5:12, the sum of the proper proportions of both.
Therefore the proper proportion of the extreme motions of Mercury alone is less than 5:12 by the quantity of the proper proportion of Venus. Understand this of these first reasons.
For below, through the second reasons, with some leaven added to the common Harmonies of both, it will be brought about that the proper proportion of Mercury alone obtains 5:12 perfectly.*

**Proposition XVII.**

The Harmony of the diverging Motions of Mars and the Earth could not be less than 5:12.

*For Mars alone obtained in the proper proportion of his motions more than a Diatessaron, and more than 18:25, by XIV.
Now their lesser Harmony is the Diapente 2:3, by XV; therefore these two parts joined together make 12:25. Yet to the Earth also is owed its own proper proportion, by Axiom IV.
Therefore, since the Harmony of the Diverging motions consists of the said three elements, it will be greater than 12:25. Now the Harmony next greater than 12:25, that is 60:125, is 5:12, namely 60:144.
Wherefore if there is need of a Harmony for this greater proportion of the motions of the two Planets,*

Thus far, therefore, to all the remaining pairs of Planets their respective two Harmonies have come by necessary reasons; only the pair of the Earth and Venus has hitherto obtained one single Harmony, 5:8, through the Axioms used up to this point.

*Posterior Reasons.*

**Axiom XVIII.**

Universal Harmonies of the motions had to be established by the tempering of the six motions,
chiefly through the extreme motions. *Proved by Axiom I.*

**Axiom XIX.**

The same universal Harmonies ought to occur through some latitude of motions, namely so that they might happen all the more frequently.

*For if they had been determined to individual points of the motions, it could have happened that they would never occur, or certainly very rarely.*

**Axiom XX.**

As the distinction of the Genera of Harmonies into Hard and Soft is most natural,
as was proved in Book III, so universal Harmonies of both genera had to be procured among the extreme motions of the Planets.

**Axiom XXI.**

Different species of Harmonies of both Genera had to be instituted, so that the beauty of the World
might be framed out of all possible members of variety; and that through the extreme motions, at least some of them. *By Axiom I.*

**Proposition XXII.**

The extreme motions of the Planets ought to mark out the places or strings of the Octave system,
or the keys of the Musical scale.

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0315
   :page: p. 223

*For the origin and comparison of Harmonies begun from one common term begot the Musical scale, or the division of the Octave into its places or sounds,
as was proved in Book III. Since therefore varied Harmonies are required between the extremes of the motions, by Ax. I, XX, and XXI, wherefore a real distinction of some celestial system or Harmonic scale through the extremes of the motions is required.*

**Proposition XXIII.**

There had to be one pair of Planets between whose motions no Harmonies could exist except the two Sixths, the Major 4:5 and the minor 5:6.

*For since a distinction of the genera of Harmonies was necessary, by Axiom XX,
and that through the extremes of the motions in the Apsides, by XXII—because only the extremes, namely the slowest and the fastest, require the determination of the one procuring and ordering, while the intermediate tensions arise of their own accord
with the planet's very transit from the slowest motion to the fastest, without special care—this ordering therefore could not be brought about otherwise than that in the extremes of two Planetary motions a Diesis, or 24:25, should be marked out;
because the Genera of Harmonies are distinguished by a Diesis, through those things which were explained in Book III. Now a Diesis is the difference either of the two Thirds, 4:5 and 5:6, or of the two Sixths, 3:5 and 5:8,
or of the same increased by one or more Diapason intervals. Now the two Thirds 4:5 and 5:6 had no place between two Planets, by Pr. VI; nor indeed were Thirds or Sixths increased by a Diapason interval found anywhere,
except 5:12 in the pair of Mars and the Earth; nor yet that otherwise than with its companion 2:3, and therefore the intermediate ones 5:8, 3:5, and 1:2 were also admitted alongside. Therefore it remains that to one pair of Planets
the two sixths, 3:5 and 5:8, had to be given. Moreover, only the Sixths were to be conceded to the variation of those motions, in such a way that they should neither dilate their bounds to the comprehension of the next greater interval,
one Octave 1:2, nor contract them to the narrowness of the next lesser Diapente 2:3. For although it is true that two planets making a Diapente at the converging extremes of their motions, and a Diapason at the diverging,
can also make Sixths, and thus pass through the Diesis as well, yet this would not savor of the singular providence of the Orderer of motions. For the Diesis, the least of intervals, lying hid in potency in all the greater ones
which are comprehended by the extremes of the motions, is indeed then passed through by the intermediate motions varied by continuous tension, yet is not determined by their extremes, since a part is always less than the whole,
namely the Diesis is less than the greater interval 3:4 which is between 2:3 and 1:2, which whole would here be posited as determined by the extremes of the motions.*

**Proposition XXIV.**

Two planets which change the genus of Harmony ought to make the difference of their proper proportions between the extremes of motions a Diesis, and the proper proportion of one ought to be greater than a Diesis:
and by their aphelion motions they ought to make one of the Sixths, and by their perihelion the remaining one.

*For since the extremes of the motions make two Harmonies, differing by a single Diesis,*

.. folio:: harmonices_mundi:0316
   :page: p. 224

*this can be done in three ways: either so that the motion of one Planet remains constant while the other's varies by a Diesis; or so that each varies by half a Diesis and they make 3:5, a major Sixth, when the upper is at Aphelion and the lower at Perihelion:*

**XLVII. Proposition.**

If the inscription of Figures among the Planets was free, the Tetrahedron above ought to touch the perihelion orb of Jupiter exactly with its angles, and below, the aphelion of Mars exactly with the centers of its faces: 
whereas the Cube and Octahedron, each standing with its angles upon the perihelion Orb of its own Planet, ought to penetrate the orb of its interior planet with the centers of their faces, so that those centers lie between the aphelion and perihelion Orbs: 
the Dodecahedron and Icosahedron on the contrary, pressing lightly on the outside with their angles against the perihelion Orbs of their own Planets, ought not quite to reach the aphelion orbs of their interior planets with the centers of their faces: 
finally, the Dodecahedral Echinus, standing with its angles on the perihelion orb of Mars, ought with the midpoints of its inverted edges—which divide pairs of solid rays—to come very close to the aphelion orb of Venus.

*For the Tetrahedron is the middle figure of the Primary ones, both in origin and in position in the World; equally therefore, if nothing hindered, it ought to separate both regions, both ♃ and ♂. 
Since the Cube stood above it and outward, and the Dodecahedron below and inward, it was fitting therefore that their inscription should strive for the contrariety of that whereof the Tetrahedron held the mean, 
namely that one made an excess of inscription and the other a defect; that is, one penetrated the interior orb to some extent, while the other did not reach it. And because the Octa-*

**IL. Epiphonema.**

It was good that the solid figures in the generation of intervals yielded to Harmonic reasons, and the Major Harmonies of pairs to the universal Harmonies of all, so far as this was necessary.

*By a happy chance we have arrived at the Square of seven, XLIX, so that this may follow as a kind of Sabbath, after six solid Octonaries of Utterances concerning the work of the Heavens have gone before. 
With good reason also I have made an Epiphonema of what could have been set beforehand among the Axioms: because God too, having already finished the work of Creation, saw all that he had made, and behold, it was very good.*

*The Epiphonema has two members: the first, concerning Harmonies in general, is demonstrated thus. For where there is a choice between diverse things not wholly compatible with one another, that the more excellent must be preferred, 
and the cheaper things curtailed as much as is necessary, the very word *tou kosmou*, which signifies adornment, seems to argue. Now by as much as life surpasses body, and form surpasses matter, by so much does Harmonic adornment surpass simple Geometric adornment.*

*For just as life perfects the bodies of Living beings, because they were born to live it—which follows from the Archetype of the World, which is the divine essence itself— 
so motion measures out each of the regions assigned to the Planets for its own star; because in order that the star might be able to move, its own region was assigned to it. 
Now the five solid figures, by the force of the very word, belong to the spaces of the regions and to the number of them and of the bodies; whereas Harmonies belong to the motions. 
Again, just as Matter is diffuse and indefinite of itself, while Form is definite, unified, and bounding matter itself: so also of Geometric ratios there are infinitely many, whereas Harmonies are few. For although among Geometric ratios too there are certain degrees of definitions, formations, and restrictions, 
nor can more than three exist from the ascription of orbs to regular figures: nevertheless an accident common to all the rest follows these also, because an infinite possible division of quantities is presupposed, which even those ratios whose terms*

*are incommensurable with one another in some way involve in act. Yet Harmonic ratios are all expressible, with all their terms commensurable, and drawn from a certain and finite species of plane figures. Now infinity of division represents matter; whereas commensuration, or Expressibility of terms, represents form. 
As therefore matter desires Form, as a rough stone—of just the right size—desires the Idea of a human body: so Geometric Figural ratios desire Harmonies; not that they fashion and form them, but because this Matter fits this Form, this quantity of stone fits this likeness, 
and this figural ratio also squares more aptly with this Harmony: so that they may therefore be fashioned and formed further—Matter indeed by its Form, the stone by the Sculptor into the likeness of a living being, 
and the ratio of the figural orbs by its own, that is, a near and fitting Harmony.*

*What has been said thus far will become clearer from the history of my discoveries. 
When twenty-four years ago I fell upon this contemplation, I first inquired whether the individual orbs of the planets were separated from one another by equal intervals (for the orbs stand apart in Copernicus, nor do they touch one another): 
for I knew nothing fairer than the ratio of equality. Yet that lacks both head and tail: for this material equality yielded no definite number of movable bodies, and no definite quantity for the intervals. 
I thought therefore of a similarity of the intervals to the orbs, that is, of Proportion. Yet the same complaint followed; for although intervals between the orbs did indeed come out unequal, 
still neither were they unequally unequal, as Copernicus would have it, nor was the quantity of the proportion or the number of the orbs produced. I passed over to regular plane figures; intervals were formed from them by circumscribing circles, yet still in no definite number. 
I came to the five solid figures: here both the number of bodies and the magnitude of the intervals lay open nearly true, so much so that for the remaining discrepancy I appealed to the perfection of Astronomy. 
Astronomy has been perfected over these twenty years; and behold, the intervals still differed from the solid figures; nor yet did the causes lie open of the Eccentricities so unequally distributed among the Planets. 
For in this house of the World I was seeking nothing but stones, of an elegant form, yet such as befits stones; unaware that the Architect had fashioned them into the most finely articulated likeness of a living body. 
Thus little by little, especially in these last three years, I came to the Harmonies, abandoning the Figures in the smallest differences: both because those stood on the side of Form, which the finishing hand should put on, 
while the figures stood on the side of Matter, which in the World is the number of bodies and the rough extent of spaces; and also because the former gave the Eccentricities, which the latter did not even promise: 
those namely bestowed nose, eyes, and the remaining limbs upon the statue, for which these had prescribed only the external quantity of a rough mass.*

*Wherefore, just as neither are the bodies of Living beings made, nor is a block of stone wont to be shaped, to the pure rule of some geometric figure, 
but something is taken away from the outer round figure, however elegant (while the due magnitude of the bulk nevertheless remains), so that the body may acquire the organs necessary for life, and the stone the likeness of a living being: 
so also the ratio which the solid figures were about to prescribe for the planetary Orbs, as inferior and regarding only body and matter, had to yield to Harmonies, so far as was necessary for them to stand alongside and adorn the motions of the globes.*

*The second member of the Epiphonema, concerning universal Harmonies, has a proof akin to the former. 
For the supreme finishing touch, as it were, of perfection is owed rather to that which more perfects the World; conversely, that must be curtailed (if one of the two must be curtailed) which holds the secondary role here. 
Now the universal Harmony of all perfects the World more than the single twin Harmonies of each neigh-*

   :page: p. 243

*boring pair. For Harmony is a certain ratio of unity: therefore they are more united if all together conspire into one Harmony than if each pair separately conspires into twin Harmonies. 
Wherefore in a conflict between the two, one of the two single Harmonies of each pair of Planets had to yield, so that the universal Harmonies of all might stand. Rather, moreover, the Major ones, of diverging motions, had to yield than the Minor ones, of converging motions. 
For if the former diverge, they look toward Planets not of the proposed pair, but other neighbors; whereas if the latter converge, then the motions of either Planet incline toward the motion of the other, mutually: 
as in the pair of Jupiter and Mars, the aphelion of the former inclines toward Saturn, the perihelion of the latter toward the Earth; whereas the perihelion of the former inclines toward Mars, the aphelion of the latter toward Jupiter. 
Therefore the Harmony of the latter is more proper to Jupiter and Mars; the Harmony of the former, diverging ones, is somewhat more foreign to Jupiter and Mars. 
Now the principle of union, which binds each pair of neighbors together, was less injured if a Harmony more foreign and remote from them were fermented, than if their proper one—namely, that between the closer motions of neighboring Planets—were so. 
Nor yet was this fermentation very great: for a ratio was found whereby both the universal Harmonies of all the Planets should stand—and those of two distinct kinds, and with some latitude of tuning that at least equaled a Comma— 
and the twin single Harmonies of each pair of neighbors should also be maintained: those of Converging motions indeed perfect in four pairs; those of aphelia in one, and of perihelia in two, likewise perfect; 
while those of diverging motions in four pairs are within the discrepancy of a single Diesis, the smallest interval, by which the human voice in figured song almost perpetually errs; and in the sole pair of Jupiter and Mars, between a Diesis and a Semitone. 
It appears therefore that this mutual yielding is on every side very good.*

Thus far then let us utter our epiphonema concerning the work of God the Creator. 
It remains now that at length, lifting my eyes and hands from the tablet of demonstrations and raising them toward heaven, I devoutly and humbly pray to the Father of lights: 
O Thou who by the light of Nature dost advance in us the longing for the light of Grace, so that through it Thou mayest translate us into the light of Glory; 
I give thanks to Thee, Lord Creator, because Thou hast delighted me in Thy handiwork, and in the works of Thy hands I have exulted. 
Behold now I have completed the work of my calling, having used as much strength of mind as Thou hast given me; 
I have made manifest the glory of Thy works to the men who shall read these demonstrations, so far as the narrowness of my Mind could grasp of its infinity; 
ready was my spirit to philosophize most faultlessly: if anything unworthy of Thy counsels has been put forth by me, a little worm born and nourished in the mire of sins, 
which Thou wouldst have men know, inspire that also, so that I may amend it: if by the wondrous beauty of Thy works I have been enticed into rashness, 
or if I have loved my own glory among men while advancing in the work destined for Thy glory, gentle and merciful, forgive it: 
and finally, that these demonstrations may yield to Thy glory and the salvation of Souls, and in no wise hinder them, graciously deign to bring it to pass.
